## Answer

**Recommendation:** Cite the exergy-correct, non-isothermal-source Carnot ceiling (≈54.3%/≈52.3%), not naive Carnot or Curzon-Ahlborn (chain C1). Practice sits ≈11 points below it gross, ≈14-16 net (chain C2). Of that gap, ≈2-4 points are near-term recoverable and ≈7-9 are locked in by the Second Law; raising the salt temperature is a separate lever, not a way to close this gap (chain C4).

**Band (from §6):** LOW — capped by chain C3 and chain C4

**Would change it:** A per-component exergy-destruction model or a measured commercial-scale operating record would tighten the recoverable/locked-in split (chain C3); the ceiling choice (chain C1, HIGH) and the gap size (chain C2) are comparatively robust already.

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** For a 565 °C molten-salt power tower driving a steam Rankine power block that
rejects heat near ambient temperature, what is the defensible law-permitted ceiling on
thermal-to-electric conversion efficiency, how far below it does current practice sit, and of
that gap how much is recoverable by known near-term engineering versus permanently locked in by
the Second Law once the heat-source temperature is fixed?

**Success criteria:**
- A single, named, correctly-derived "law-permitted ceiling" is identified and defended against
  two named rival candidates (the classical Carnot bound and the Curzon-Ahlborn/endoreversible
  bound), with an explicit ruling on which one is the right ceiling to cite and why the other is
  not — this is itself the analytical content the question asks for, not a preliminary to it.
- The ceiling is computed from the correct reservoir pair (not an assumed one), numerically, for
  stated cold-reservoir choices.
- Current practice is reported with real numbers (design-point and, where available, measured),
  sourced, with genuine data gaps named as gaps rather than filled with invented precision.
- The ceiling-to-practice gap is stated in percentage points and relative terms, on a stated
  basis (gross cycle vs. net plant).
- The gap is decomposed into the five named loss categories (HX/approach-temperature
  irreversibility; turbine/pump/mechanical losses; condenser cooling and ambient variability;
  cycle-topology/materials limits; parasitics and solar-specific operating losses), each with a
  magnitude bracket and a stated confidence level — not a single invented split with false
  precision.
- A final synthesis states, in percentage points and as a fraction of the total gap, how much is
  recoverable with known/near-term engineering versus fundamentally locked in by the chosen
  source temperature and the Second Law, with the reasoning for that split traceable to the
  decomposition above.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** The Carnot efficiency bound η_Carnot = 1 − T_cold/T_hot (both in kelvin) is the maximum
  possible thermal-to-electric conversion efficiency of **any** heat engine — any working fluid,
  any cycle architecture, any equipment quality — operating between two fixed-temperature
  reservoirs; no engineering refinement can exceed it. — source: the Second Law of
  Thermodynamics (Kelvin–Planck/Clausius statements) via Carnot's theorem; standard engineering
  thermodynamics (e.g., Cengel & Boles, *Thermodynamics: An Engineering Approach*, ch. on the
  Carnot cycle). This is a mathematical theorem derived from the Second Law, not an empirical
  measurement, so it passes the irreducibility test directly — it bottoms out at a physical law
  and is not reducible further.

- **GT-2** The Curzon–Ahlborn (endoreversible) efficiency η_CA = 1 − √(T_cold/T_hot) is the
  efficiency of an *idealized endoreversible* heat engine (internally reversible, but coupled to
  its reservoirs through finite-rate, irreversible heat exchange) operating **at the power output
  that maximizes power**, not the efficiency that maximizes efficiency — source: Curzon & Ahlborn
  (1975), "Efficiency of a Carnot engine at maximum power output," *Am. J. Phys.* 43(1); the same
  result was derived earlier by Chambadal (1957) and Novikov (1957). This is also a theorem
  within a specific, named engine model (reversible engine + finite-rate coupling, operated for
  maximum power), not a measurement — it bottoms out at a mathematical derivation from a stated
  model and is not reducible further, but note what it is and is not a bound *on*: it bounds
  engines tuned for maximum power density, not engines tuned for maximum efficiency, and a real
  plant in the second category can exceed it (see Assumptions §2 and chain C1).

- **GT-3** T [K] = T [°C] + 273.15 — source: SI definition of the kelvin relative to the degree
  Celsius. A definition, not a measurement; irreducible by construction.

- **GT-4** At Solar Two (the 10 MWe molten-salt power-tower demonstration plant near Barstow,
  CA, operated 1996–1999), hot salt was pumped "from the hot-salt sump (at 565°C)... through the
  shell side of the superheater, through the tube bundle in the kettle evaporator, and finally
  through the shell side of the preheater, where it returned to the cold salt storage tank at
  290°C" — source: Pacheco (ed.), *Final Test and Evaluation Results from the Solar Two Project*,
  Sandia SAND2002-0120 (OSTI 793226), §5.1; read-at-source: quoted verbatim from the extracted
  report text (the sentence spanning the SGS description). 565 °C hot-tank / 290 °C cold-tank is
  the reference operating window used across Solar-Salt (NaNO₃/KNO₃) commercial towers of this
  design generation, including the Crescent Dunes-class design basis used in GT-5/GT-6 below.
  T_hot = 565 °C + 273.15 ≈ **838 K**.

  is not reducible further; it reduces algebraically to GT-1's formula in the limit T₁→T₂ (an

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: The 290 °C cold salt tank as the cold reservoir

**What was tried:** Computing η_Carnot = 1 − T_cold/T_hot using the two salt-tank temperatures
directly (565 °C hot tank, 290 °C cold tank) as the reservoir pair, since both are well-sourced,
round, and salient numbers (GT-4).

**Why abandoned:** The cold tank is the return leg of the heat *source* loop, not a heat sink —
nothing in the plant rejects waste heat at 290 °C. The arithmetic is well-formed (1 − 563/838 =
32.8%) but the result is not a ceiling on the actual machine: GT-6's real, operating design
practice (43.0% gross) already exceeds it. A bound real equipment exceeds has the wrong reservoir
pair, not a measurement error — this is the decisive test, and this framing fails it.

**What it ruled out:** Any later re-derivation that reaches for the tank pair because both
numbers are salt-side and convenient. The correct cold reservoir is always the point where the
*cycle* rejects heat (chain C1), never a temperature internal to the heat-delivery loop, however
tempting its availability.

### Dead End: Curzon-Ahlborn as the "more realistic" law-permitted ceiling

**What was tried:** Using η_CA = 1 − √(T_cold/T_hot) as the ceiling instead of (or alongside)
Carnot, on the reasoning offered in the question itself — that it "accounts for the unavoidable
finite-rate heat transfer needed to deliver nonzero power" and so is a tighter, more physically
honest bound than the classical, zero-power-limit Carnot figure.

**Why abandoned:** Curzon-Ahlborn is the efficiency of one specific, named engine model —
internally reversible, finite-rate coupled to its reservoirs, operated at the power level that
*maximizes power output* — not a bound on heat engines generally. GT-6's real design-point
efficiency (43.0% wet / 41.2% dry) already exceeds the CA value computed for the same reservoir
pair (38.6%/37.3%, chain C1). A plant engineered for efficiency, not power density, is not
described by the CA model, and the fact that real equipment already beats it is decisive, not
merely suggestive.

**What it ruled out:** The intuitive but incorrect move of treating "accounts for finite-rate heat
transfer" as the same thing as "is a valid ceiling." Finite-rate heat transfer is real and does
destroy exergy (this is exactly what GT-15's exergy-correct ceiling and chain C3's category (a)
and (b) brackets price in), but Curzon-Ahlborn prices it in for the wrong optimization target.

### Dead End: An exergy ceiling that bakes in current HX approach-temperature gaps and steam-metallurgy limits

**What was tried:** Following the question's third suggested candidate — constructing a
"law-permitted ceiling" that already incorporates the ≈28 °C salt-to-steam approach gap current
heat-exchanger design accepts, and the live-steam-temperature cap current turbine metallurgy
imposes, on the reasoning that these constraints are "unavoidable" in practice.

**Why abandoned:** Approach-temperature gaps and metallurgy limits are engineering and economic
conventions (current constraints, per the Assumptions Table's taxonomy), not laws — GT-6 itself
documents three different metallurgy/pressure classes (subcritical, supercritical,
ultrasupercritical) at three different approach gaps, all operating today or in near-term design
studies, which is direct evidence these limits move with investment and materials choice. Folding
a movable convention into "the ceiling" produces a number that looks rigorous but is actually
just a restatement of current best design practice — the same failure mode as citing
best-in-class incumbent practice as a theoretical limit, just with the incumbent figure computed
rather than observed.

**What it ruled out:** Treating "the ceiling" and "the best achievable design today" as the same
quantity. They are not: the first is what chain C1 computes (GT-15's exergy-correct bound), the
second is GT-6's design-point figure, and the distance between them is exactly chain C2's gap —
collapsing them would have made the central question of this analysis unanswerable by
definition.

### Dead End: Reporting the naive single-reservoir Carnot gap (≈19 points) as the headline figure

**What was tried:** Early in Phase 4, computing the gap as naive-Carnot-ceiling minus
current-practice (62.3% − 43.0% = 19.3 points), matching the treatment a prior theoretical-limit
pass on this same domain used.

**Why abandoned:** Once GT-15's exergy-correct derivation (chain C1) established that the salt's
own temperature glide lowers the correct ceiling by roughly 8 points, continuing to report the
naive 19-point figure as "the gap" would have attributed real, structural exergy content of the
heat source — exergy that no engine, however perfect, could ever have extracted, because it was
never thermodynamically available in the first place — to the plant's own shortfall. That
materially changes the headline relative-gap figure (≈21% vs. a prior ≈31%) and therefore the
answer to Part 5's recoverable/locked-in split.

**What it ruled out:** Any synthesis in §6 that quotes "~19 points" or "~31% relative" as the
gap. Both numbers are real and reported (chain C2's fifth hop, for direct comparability with
other treatments of this domain), but they are explicitly labeled as the looser, naive-ceiling
figures, not the headline result.

### Dead End: Resolving Crescent Dunes' and Gemasolar's exact cycle parameters from secondary sources

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Cite the exergy-correct, non-isothermal-source Carnot ceiling —
**≈54.3% wet-cooled / ≈52.3% dry-cooled** — as the law-permitted ceiling, not the naive
single-reservoir Carnot figure (≈62.3%/60.6%, valid but loose) and not Curzon-Ahlborn (≈38.6%/
37.3%, not a valid ceiling at all — real design practice already exceeds it) (chain C1). Against
that ceiling, current commercial design practice (43.0% wet / 41.2% dry gross; an estimated
35.8–38.3% net after parasitics) sits roughly **11 points below on a gross basis (≈21% relative)
and 14–16 points below on a net basis (≈27–31% relative)** (chain C2) — a materially smaller gap
than the ≈19–25 points the naive Carnot figure would report, because the naive figure
overstates the ceiling by treating the salt as isothermal rather than as the cooling,
sensible-heat stream it actually is (chain C2). Of the ≈11-point gross gap, roughly **2–4
points (a quarter to a third) are recoverable with known near-term engineering** at the current
565 °C salt temperature (supercritical steam pressure, tighter HX approach, wet cooling where
water is available, better storage dispatch), while roughly **7–9 points (two-thirds to
three-quarters) are fundamentally locked in** by the Second Law applied to a real, finite-power
heat exchanger and real turbomachinery (chain C4). Raising the salt temperature (toward 650 °C
nitrate-salt ultrasupercritical steam, or beyond via chloride-salt/sCO₂ designs) is a separate,
substantial lever on *absolute* efficiency, not a way to close this fixed-T_hot gap — and on the
one case this analysis could compute, it narrows the *relative* gap too (≈12% at 650 °C against
≈21% at 565 °C) (chain C4).

**Key insight:** The question "which ceiling is defensible" is not a preliminary to this
analysis — it is most of the analysis. Molten salt is not an isothermal reservoir; it is a
finite stream that cools 275 °C while delivering heat, and a Carnot ceiling computed at its peak
temperature silently assumes away roughly 8 points of "headroom" that was never thermodynamically
available to begin with (chain C1). Correcting for this shrinks the apparent gap by nearly half
in relative terms (≈21% vs. a naive ≈31%) and, more importantly, inverts the naive intuition that
"raising T_hot closes the Carnot gap": raising T_hot resets the whole bracket upward and, if
anything, makes the cycle *more* reversible relative to its own new ceiling, but it does not
touch the locked-in component of the gap at the original operating point at all (chain C4).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This synthesis accepts a wide, explicitly stated uncertainty band on
the gap decomposition (chain C3, LOW confidence) in exchange for not inventing false precision: no
plant-specific exergy/heat-and-mass-balance model for a molten-salt tower was located, so
categories (a), (b), (c)'s residual, and the cycling portion of (e) are Fermi brackets, not
measurements (chain C3). It also accepts a genuine, named data gap on Crescent Dunes' and
Gemasolar's actual cycle parameters rather than filling it with numbers dressed as facts (chain
C2). Finally, it deliberately leaves the economic question
of whether the locked-in and recoverable efficiency figures are worth pursuing at all, given
competing dispatchable-generation cost trajectories, as a named but unquantified second-order
channel (chain C5) rather than folding an unsupported claim into the headline numbers. It also scopes itself explicitly to steady-state thermodynamic loss mechanisms: reliability and availability losses (unplanned outages, equipment failures, the kind of extended downtime Crescent Dunes itself experienced) are a real, additional gap between nameplate design efficiency and actual annual energy capture that none of categories (a)-(e) (chain C3) quantifies, and this analysis does not estimate it — no chain — flagged assumption only.

**Pre-check:** head C1 (HIGH), C2 (MEDIUM), C3 (LOW), C4 (LOW) · ?-marked: none · lowest cited: LOW · Inputs ceiling: LOW
**Confidence:** LOW — the headline recoverable/locked-in split (chain C4) is capped by chain C3's
LOW rating, which is itself capped by the Fermi-bracket apportionment (A-9, A-10) rather than a
plant-specific exergy model. This is not a weak conclusion in the sense of being poorly
reasoned — chain C1, which establishes *which* ceiling is defensible, is rated HIGH, and chain C2,
which establishes *how large* the gap is, is rated MEDIUM; both rest on read-at-source primary
documents (Sandia SAND2011-9320, SAND2002-0120, the DOE SunShot fact sheet) — it is LOW specifically on the *apportionment* of the
gap across mechanisms, which is the one piece of Part 4 that would need a per-component

chain C3's own confidence line states. The direction of every finding here (ceiling choice, gap
size, dominance of the locked-in component, T_hot as a separate lever) is robust to that
remaining uncertainty; only the precise point-estimates within the 2–4 / 7–9 split would move.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Open self-serve freemium rollout (mechanism A) as the primary recommendation

**What was tried:** Early in the analysis, recommending that the business build a full open freemium funnel immediately — matching competitors' apparent playbook — was considered as the headline recommendation.

**Why abandoned:** Chain C1 established that the self-serve/inbound infrastructure mechanism A requires (marketing, content, self-serve signup UX) does not exist today, and chain C2 established that the breakeven economics plausibly do not close without it, using both an illustrative cost bracket and the real published benchmark in GT-7. **What it ruled out:** Building a public self-serve signup/freemium product as the first move, before validating conversion behavior through a cheaper, gated pilot.

### Dead End: Treating external freemium conversion benchmarks as a single monolithic planning input

**What was tried:** Initially considered using "the" industry benchmark as one expected conversion figure to plug into an ROI model for this product.

**Why abandoned:** GT-7's actual published data splits sharply by population type — open/standard freemium at 3–12% versus PQL-driven (pre-qualified) conversion at 25–39% — a 3x+ spread. Chain C3 shows this company's likely free population doesn't cleanly match either benchmark population without a specific design choice (gated vs. open). Collapsing this into a single average would have hidden the single most important lever in the whole analysis: which population a given free-offer design actually attracts.

**What it ruled out:** Modeling expected ROI from a single average conversion assumption before running a pilot or deciding whether the offer is gated or open.

### Dead End: Doing nothing / maintaining status quo indefinitely

**What was tried:** Considered simply not responding to the competitive pressure named in GT-5? at all, on the grounds that freemium is risky and unproven here.

**Why abandoned:** Chain C5's trade-off scoring shows doing nothing scores 71 against the gated pilot's 83 — a 12-point gap driven by the pilot's combination of bounded cost exposure and direct learning value, neither of which doing nothing provides. Doing nothing forgoes the only concretely-evidenced, low-risk, low-cost mechanism (gated pilot, chain C3) that fits the current GTM motion and that addresses a competitive pressure every named competitor already exploits.

**What it ruled out:** Treating "no free tier at all, indefinitely" as the default safe choice without weighing it against a cheap, reversible, instrumented pilot.

**Premise.** The recommended plan — run a gated pilot with a pre-committed decision gate before any broader freemium commitment — has already failed. What caused it?

**Causes** (unfiltered, generated from at least three stakeholder viewpoints before any grouping):
- *Outbound rep viewpoint:* reps quietly use the free offer as a way to avoid a harder close, letting deals drift into "they're on the free tier" limbo instead of closing at $10K; reps aren't trained on when the offer is appropriate to extend, so it gets offered inconsistently or too early in the cycle; reps resent the extra step of gating/approving the offer and route around it informally.
- *Support/success-team viewpoint:* free accounts generate a disproportionate share of support tickets because they have no onboarding investment or dedicated success contact; support has no way to distinguish a free account that is a near-term paid prospect from one that will never convert, so triage is inefficient; the pilot's "small" scope creeps upward because sales keeps asking for "just one more" gated account.
- *Finance/leadership viewpoint:* the pilot's real marginal cost per free account turns out to be materially higher than the illustrative $600–$3,000 bracket, because Finance's qualitative "cost scales with active users" hides fixed-cost components (e.g., a support headcount threshold) that a small pilot doesn't reveal; the pilot runs long enough, and ambiguously enough, that it becomes a permanent fixture without ever passing through the decision gate; nobody owns actually reading the pilot's data against the breakeven threshold once the pilot is live, so the decision gate is never actually applied.
- *Referred-prospect / buyer viewpoint:* a referred prospect who would have become a $10K customer takes the free offer instead and never revisits the paid conversation; a prospect mid-outbound-cycle uses the free offer as leverage to negotiate the paid price down, achieving via the pilot exactly the price-anchoring damage chain C4 warned about.
- *Competitor viewpoint:* a competitor, seeing this company's gated pilot, lowers the bar further (e.g., widens their own free tier's feature set) in a way that neutralizes the pilot's intended objection-handling benefit before it has run long enough to produce clean data.

**Clusters** (structural weaknesses, each naming the chains/ground truths it bears on):
1. *Scope creep / no real gate* — the pilot never stays "small and reversible" in practice, and nobody enforces the decision gate once it is live. Bears on: C5 (the chain that recommends the gate), GT-6 (the breakeven identity the gate is supposed to use).
2. *Rep behavior undermines the "rep-controlled, not self-serve" design* — reps use the offer in ways chain C4 explicitly tried to prevent (easier close, inconsistent timing). Bears on: C4 (second-order sales-cycle risk), C5 (the pilot's design intent).
3. *Cost blind spot* — the illustrative cost bracket in chain C2 understates a real fixed-cost component Finance's qualitative confirmation (GT-3?) doesn't surface until the pilot is already running. Bears on: C2, GT-3?, A-11.
4. *Referral/price-anchoring leakage* — exactly the risks named in chain C4 (referral cannibalization, price anchoring) materialize even inside the "gated" design, because gating controls who is offered the deal, not what the prospect does with it once offered. Bears on: C4, C5.

**Disposition:**
1. Scope creep / no real gate → **plan change:** name a single accountable owner (not "sales" collectively) for enforcing the decision gate, with the gate's pass/fail threshold and review date written down before the pilot starts, not derived after seeing early results.
2. Rep behavior undermines design intent → **plan change:** require manager approval before a rep extends the offer (rather than rep discretion alone) during the pilot, and track offer-to-close-outcome by rep as part of the pilot's own instrumentation.
3. Cost blind spot → **accepted risk, named mitigation:** accept that the illustrative cost bracket may understate real cost; mitigate by capping the pilot's free-account count at a small, fixed number agreed with Finance in advance, so even an understated cost estimate has a hard ceiling on total exposure.
4. Referral/price-anchoring leakage → **accepted risk, named mitigation:** accept that gating controls eligibility, not behavior; mitigate by having the pilot explicitly track referral-to-paid conversion and any observed price pushback referencing the free offer, as two of the pilot's named success/failure metrics rather than discovering them informally.

**Falsification.** This conclusion is false if: a gated pilot, run with the owner, approval gate, and fixed account cap above, measures a free-to-paid conversion rate and a real marginal cost per account such that GT-6's breakeven identity is cleared at the pilot's actual numbers (not the illustrative bracket) with no measurable degradation in outbound cycle length or referral-to-paid conversion versus a control group — in which case a broader rollout, not a bounded pilot, becomes the better-supported recommendation.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: The operator's hypothesis — low lubricant viscosity in a cold snap drove boundary lubrication and wore the race through

**What was tried:** The inversion technique was applied directly to the operator's stated claim. Claim: "insufficient EHD film thickness from low lubricant viscosity during a cold-snap period caused boundary-lubrication contact and wore the inner race through to failure." Inverted form: "insufficient EHD film thickness from low viscosity during the cold snap did not cause this failure." Five failure-guaranteeing conditions for the inverted form (i.e., conditions under which the original claim is false) were enumerated: (i) the cold snap in fact raised rather than lowered the oil's viscosity; (ii) the observed damage originates below, not at, the raceway surface; (iii) the crack-flank microstructure shows features (white-etching, butterflies) with no established formation pathway via viscosity thinning or asperity friction alone; (iv) the crack morphology (crescent-shaped networks) matches a published fatigue-crack morphology rather than the scuffing/polishing/micropitting morphology boundary lubrication produces; (v) the time-course and magnitude of the life shortfall (9×, over 14,000 h) do not match the time signature of a single-season viscosity-driven wear-through process. Two of these were tagged load-bearing — the claim does not survive either being false: (i) a genuine viscosity drop, and (ii) a surface-origin crack.

**Why abandoned:** Both load-bearing preconditions fail directly against already-established ground truths, independently of each other. Precondition (i) fails on GT-9: cooling raises, not lowers, lubricant viscosity, so the hypothesis's own named trigger has the wrong sign for a cold snap as stated — the only coherent cold-weather lubrication risk is the opposite one, cold-start starvation from oil too viscous to circulate quickly, which is a different failure story and still predicts surface-origin damage. Precondition (ii) fails on GT-1: the measured crack origin is about 0.4 mm below the raceway surface, inside the Hertzian subsurface-shear-stress band (GT-10), not at the surface where boundary-lubrication damage initiates by mechanism (GT-8?). The three corroborative preconditions independently reinforce the same conclusion: GT-3's white-etching/butterfly microstructure has no documented formation pathway via viscosity thinning alone (chain C3); GT-2's crescent-shaped microcrack morphology matches the WEC/WSF literature's reported crack networks (GT-11, GT-13?) rather than scuffing or polishing; and GT-5/C2's statistical improbability of a 9× life shortfall under ordinary scatter is not the signature a slow, single-season wear-through process would leave behind on a condition-monitoring trend (a sudden step increase in kurtosis, GT-6, is more consistent with a developing fatigue defect than with progressive abrasive wear). The counter-intervention the hypothesis would prescribe — a lubricant change-out or viscosity-grade change — does not address any of the three live candidate mechanisms identified in C4 (electrical, hydrogen-tribochemical, or transient-load skidding), so acting on it would not prevent recurrence even if it were cheap to try.

**What it ruled out:** Lubricant viscosity and boundary lubrication as the primary cause of this specific failure, and the corresponding counter-intervention (lubricant change-out alone) as an adequate fix. It does not rule out lubricant condition as a secondary, contributing factor via the hydrogen-tribochemical pathway (C4) — that remains open pending the oil-chemistry test in §6. A skeptic re-running this diagnosis on the next HSS bearing failure in the fleet should not re-open the boundary-lubrication pathway unless that bearing's failure surface shows a genuinely surface-origin crack; if it does, this dead end's reasoning does not transfer and the diagnosis restarts from a different observation set.

### Dead End: Classical Lundberg-Palmgren inclusion-only rolling-contact fatigue as the sole explanation

**What was tried:** Testing whether ordinary subsurface-inclusion-initiated RCF, with no non-classical mechanism at all, could explain the failure on its own — i.e., whether the butterfly microstructure and the premature life were both within the range classical fatigue alone produces, given enough scatter and ordinary inclusion content.

**Why abandoned:** Two independent lines of evidence weigh against sole classical RCF, though neither is individually dispositive. First, GT-3 reports white-etching cracks, not just butterflies, co-located with the subsurface origin; isolated butterflies around inclusions are a documented classical-RCF feature, but white-etching cracks are not a documented product of classical inclusion fatigue alone (A-16; this is treated as an unverified-but-literature-supported discriminator, not a certainty). Second, chain C2 shows that an 11%-of-L10 failure is a low-probability event (roughly 0.4%–0.9%) under classical Weibull scatter alone, which disfavors — without excluding — a "just unlucky, ordinary bearing" account. Neither line fully excludes classical RCF: an abnormally poor inclusion rating (A-8, unverified; no mill certificate or inclusion survey is in the case record) could independently shorten life through an entirely classical pathway, and that possibility is not yet closed. What tips this from "unresolved rival" to "abandoned as the sole explanation" rather than "ruled out entirely" is that classical RCF alone has no documented pathway to white-etching cracks specifically, so even if abnormal inclusions are later confirmed to be a contributing factor, they would not by themselves explain GT-3, and the WEC/WSF-family diagnosis (C3) would still stand as at least a co-contributor.

**What it ruled out:** Treating "ordinary bearing, ordinary bad luck, no non-classical mechanism at all" as an adequate stand-alone account of this failure. It leaves open, and does not rule out, an abnormal inclusion rating as a contributing amplifier alongside a WEC/WSF-family mechanism — the confirmatory inclusion survey in §6 is what would close this residual question.

### Dead End: Generator-shaft misalignment as the proximate driver of roller sliding/skidding

**What was tried:** Testing whether static or dynamic (rotor-moment-induced) misalignment between the generator and the HSS, rather than transient torque reversal, was the load-skid driver that could explain roller sliding in this bearing.

**Why abandoned:** Directly contradicted by GT-12: full-scale dynamometer testing of wind-turbine HSS bearings (Guo & Keller, 2017) found that neither rotor-moment-induced operational misalignment nor static generator misalignment measurably changed bearing loads relative to pure-torque conditions, and the authors concluded that generator misalignment is unlikely to be a causal factor for this damage class. This is a read-at-source literature finding, not an assumption, so it is treated as closing this specific path rather than merely disfavoring it.

**What it ruled out:** Misalignment as the mechanism behind any skidding contribution to this failure. It leaves the transient torque-reversal/sub-minimum-load pathway (also reported in GT-12) as the live skidding-related candidate in C4, distinct from and not caused by misalignment.

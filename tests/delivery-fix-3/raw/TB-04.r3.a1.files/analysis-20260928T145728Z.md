## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1?** Commuter mode choice is well-modeled as minimizing a generalized travel cost: out-of-pocket monetary cost + (travel time × value of time) + a schedule-delay/reliability penalty + a comfort/safety/convenience disutility (the discrete-choice / random-utility framework standard in transportation demand modeling). — unverified: general transportation-economics domain knowledge; no specific citation was opened against this claim in this run.
- **GT-2?** Short-run transit fare elasticity of demand is commonly cited as a rule-of-thumb value of approximately −0.3; more comprehensive estimates give short-run ≈ −0.34 and long-run ≈ −0.66 (bus-specific short-run ≈ −0.4, long-run ≈ −1.0), with roughly 90% of the long-run adjustment occurring within 18-36 months. — cited to: Schimek, "Dynamic Estimates of Fare Elasticity for U.S. Public Transit," as summarized via a WebSearch digest of WorldTransitResearch.info / USF Journal of Public Transportation listings; reported-by-delegate: WebSearch summary. - **GT-4** Stockholm's congestion charge has reduced inner-city traffic by approximately 18-20%, a reduction sustained for close to two decades; cycling mode share in the zone rose sharply alongside it. — source: e360.yale.edu digest ("reduced traffic in the inner city by 18 percent") and reason.com ("traffic through the congestion zone dropped 20 percent" during the 2006 pilot); read-at-source: both pages opened and quoted directly via WebFetch this run. A third, higher claim from the reason.com source ("inner-city traffic is down by half") is noted but not adopted as the central estimate, since it is not corroborated by the other read-at-source figure and may reflect a different metric or year.
- **GT-5?** Fare-free transit programs reliably produce large ridership gains but only small-to-negligible reductions in car use unless paired with a measure that raises the relative cost of driving: Dunkirk ridership +85% (2018); Aubagne bus ridership +142% with car usage −10% over three years; Tallinn transit demand +14% but car usage only −5% (with walking trips falling ~40%, indicating substantial mode-shift away from walking rather than from driving). — cited to: multiple transit-policy studies referenced in a WebSearch summary (ScienceDirect "Fare-free transit in the United States," NAP.edu "Fare-Free Transit Evaluation Framework," Tyndall fare-free study); reported-by-delegate — underlying papers not opened directly this run.
- **GT-6?** Protected/separated cycling infrastructure generates substantial induced demand: a simulated Copenhagen bike-lane network is associated with +60% bike trips and +90% bike-kilometers versus a no-network counterfactual; each added mile of protected lane in Manhattan is associated with roughly 285 additional bikeshare trips; protected (vs. painted) lanes reduce the generalized cost of cycling by roughly 34% on major roads. — cited to: PNAS "Bikeability and the induced demand for cycling" and related bike-lane/bikeshare studies referenced in a WebSearch summary; reported-by-delegate — source papers not opened directly this run.
- **GT-7?** Protected cycle-track construction cost varies by roughly three orders of magnitude by design standard: approximately $19,000/mile for flexible-post-separated lanes, up to $17.6 million/mile for a full rebuild with a raised, sidewalk-level track; ongoing maintenance runs to a few thousand dollars/mile/year. — cited to: infrastructure cost studies referenced in a WebSearch summary (Texas A&M Transportation Institute policy page; secondary cost-analysis sources); reported-by-delegate — not opened directly this run.
- **GT-8?** Average daily travel-time budgets per person are empirically stable across cities and eras at roughly 60-90 minutes round-trip regardless of mode or urban form ("Marchetti's constant" / the travel-time-budget hypothesis) — interventions that shorten trip time have historically produced compensating changes in trip distance or frequency (induced travel) rather than a permanent net reduction in time spent traveling. — unverified: general urban-transportation domain knowledge (Marchetti 1994; Zahavi's travel-time-budget hypothesis); no specific citation opened this run.
- **GT-9** The three intervention types differ structurally in revenue mechanics by construction, independent of any specific city's data: removing transit fares eliminates a revenue stream and requires a replacement operating subsidy (revenue-negative by definition); congestion pricing creates a new toll-revenue stream net of collection/enforcement cost (revenue-positive by definition, before any decision about how that revenue is used); protected cycle lanes are a capital investment with comparatively low recurring operating cost relative to transit service (capex-dominant). — source: definitional/logical — follows directly from what each policy instrument is, not from an external empirical citation; no `?` suffix required because this is an analytic claim about instrument structure, not an unverified empirical measurement.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not treat free transit, congestion pricing, and protected cycling lanes as three mutually exclusive options to rank and pick one from; treat them as three levers with structurally different, non-overlapping ceilings, and let eight city-specific conditions — existing transit capacity/coverage, budget/revenue need and legal capacity to earmark toll revenue, density and the trip-distance distribution of current car-commutes, political feasibility and available institutional-durability mechanisms, administrative/delivery capacity to run concurrent programs, timeline urgency relative to each lever's real lead time, equity exposure of the captive-driver segment, and current baseline mode share — determine which lever(s) to deploy, in what sequence, and with what safeguards (chain C5).

**Key insight:** The three options are not competing answers to the same question: each is bounded by a different structural ceiling — a transit-network-coverage gap for free transit, a captive-trip share for congestion pricing, and a too-far-to-cycle share for protected lanes — so the "pick the best one" framing the problem invites is itself the most consequential wrong assumption to carry into the decision. A generic weighted trade-off shows the combination weakly dominates every single-lever option on every criterion assessed, not narrowly on the weighted sum, and the reason it dominates is precisely that it is the only configuration whose reach isn't capped by any single ceiling (chains C3, C2).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The combination's dominance is conditional, not unconditional: it requires giving up simplicity and single-agency execution for cross-program coordination, and it is only durable if toll revenue is legally locked to transit/cycling investment and a captive-driver rebate is built in from the start — without both safeguards, the same institutional and equity risks that have derailed real congestion-pricing programs elsewhere remain live (chain C4). This framework is also necessary-but-not-sufficient guidance: it does not by itself capture the value of an open political window, which can make an off-framework choice the right real-world call even when it scores lower here — no chain — flagged assumption only.

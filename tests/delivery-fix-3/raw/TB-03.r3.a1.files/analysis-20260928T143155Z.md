## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Full/proactive microservices decomposition as the default choice

**What was tried:** Treated "adopt microservices" as the presumptively modern, forward-looking
default and tried to justify it on general scalability and best-practice grounds.

**Why abandoned:** The trade-off in chain C5 scores full microservices 33 points below the
status quo (46 vs. 79 of 100) under every weighting tested in the flip test; chain C4 shows
the team's sustainable operational capacity for additional services is small and likely
already exceeded by a full decomposition; and GT-7 states directly that "the majority of
software systems should be built as a single monolithic application."

**What it ruled out:** Re-litigating "microservices are the default modern choice" without
first checking scale, team topology, and operational capacity against this specific team's
numbers.

### Dead End: DAU count alone determines architecture need

**What was tried:** Treated the 1,200 DAU figure as itself informative about whether more
architecture is needed — "more users implies more architecture."

**Why abandoned:** Chain C1's Fermi estimate shows 1,200 DAU produces a peak-load bracket of
roughly 0.3 to 20 requests per second, one to three orders of magnitude below typical
single-node capacity at both bracket ends — decisively contradicting the notion that this
user count alone signals a scaling need.

**What it ruled out:** Using DAU as a proxy for architectural necessity without first
converting it into an actual load estimate.

### Dead End: A single deployable is inherently technical debt

**What was tried:** Treated "one deployable" as itself evidence of neglect or an unaddressed
problem that the analysis should explain away or apologize for.

**Why abandoned:** GT-7, read at source, explicitly recommends a single monolithic
application as the majority-case default; chain C2 further shows the current one-deployable
state is exactly what Conway's Law predicts for a single, undivided six-person team — the
predicted output of the org's structure, not a deviation from it.

**What it ruled out:** Treating "one deployable" as a problem statement requiring
justification, rather than as this team's unremarkable starting condition.

### Dead End: Proactive decomposition now to avoid a costlier future rewrite

**What was tried:** The strongest rival to the recommendation — decompose now, while the
system is small, to avoid paying a larger migration cost later once the system and team have
grown.

**Why abandoned:** Chain C3 shows decomposition introduces new, CAP-theorem-grounded failure
modes with no offsetting benefit today (chain C1 finds no compute need driving them); chain
C4 shows the team's sustainable operational capacity for new services is limited without
prior platform investment; and the trade-off gap in chain C5 (33 points) is large under every
weighting tested. The underlying concern — a costlier future rewrite — is real and is not
dismissed; it is addressed instead by the "modular monolith plus tracked flip signals"
compromise in chains C5 and C6, which captures most of the future-optionality benefit at a
small, bounded present cost rather than paying the full migration cost now on a
not-yet-justified bet.

**What it ruled out:** Paying the full cost and risk of decomposition today as insurance
against a growth trajectory that GT-4 explicitly states was not supplied.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not adopt microservices now. Keep the single deployable, invest
specifically in enforced internal module boundaries (a "modular monolith") plus baseline
deployment/monitoring hygiene, and track three named flip signals before revisiting (chain
C5).

**Key insight:** The two variables that conventionally drive this question — user count and
"we're a monolith" — are not the load-bearing variables here: at 1,200 DAU, compute load sits
two to three orders of magnitude below where a single deployable would struggle (chain C1),
and a single deployable is the Conway's-Law-predicted output of a single, undivided
six-person team, not a defect (chain C2). The variables that actually gate the decision are
organizational (does more than one team need to ship independently) and operational (can the
team fund the deployment/monitoring automation microservices presuppose) — and neither is
established by the given facts (chains C2, C4).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This recommendation accepts a larger extraction cost if a real
trigger arrives without warning, mitigated by explicitly tracking the three flip signals
(chain C6); it accepts continued single-process blast-radius risk in exchange for avoiding
the CAP-theorem-grounded distributed failure modes microservices would introduce with no
offsetting benefit today (chain C3); and it spends a modest, bounded amount of engineering
time now on internal-modularity discipline rather than zero, which only pays off if that
discipline is actually funded and not skipped under feature-delivery pressure (chain C5).

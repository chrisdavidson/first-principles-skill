  serverless/microservices design with a monolith after the original design "hit a hard scaling
  limit at around 5% of the expected load," and the change "saved them a staggering 90% on
  operating costs, and simplified the system too." — cited to: Amazon's Prime Video Tech Blog,

### Derivation notes: GT-6? (estimate + theoretical-limit)

**Target quantity:** peak instantaneous requests/sec the web tier must serve. **Unit-factor
decomposition:** (users) × (requests/user/day) × (peak-hour fraction) × (intra-hour burst factor)
÷ (3,600 s/hr) → requests/sec. **Optimistic scenario:** 1,200 users × 20 req/user/day × 15%
peak-hour concentration × 5× burst ÷ 3,600 = **5.0 req/s**. **Pessimistic scenario:** 1,200 ×
200 req/user/day × 25% peak-hour concentration × 8× burst ÷ 3,600 = **133.3 req/s**.
**Decision-resolution check:** both ends of the bracket land inside "trivially handled by a
horizontally-scaled monolith, no decomposition required" — the bracket is tight enough for the
decision. **Theoretical-limit framing (governing constraint = single-node commodity-hardware
CPU/network I/O throughput):** the ideal ceiling for HTTP-serving throughput on a single modern
instance is directionally many multiples of even the pessimistic 133 req/s figure; this framing
is corroborative, not independently load-bearing, and is not carried as its own ground truth,
since GT-6?'s bracket already stands on its own unit-factor arithmetic.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End 1: Adopt microservices now via the Inverse Conway Maneuver, to pre-shape a future multi-team org

**What was tried:** Considered recommending an upfront service split specifically to force the
org toward smaller, autonomous sub-teams later (the "Inverse Conway Maneuver"), using GT-3 and
GT-4 as justification for shaping future structure rather than reflecting current structure.

**Why abandoned:** The maneuver only pays off when a specific future multi-team structure is
already planned with reasonable certainty; nothing in the problem statement indicates this team
has a stated plan to grow into separate sub-teams with independent ownership boundaries within
the analyzed horizon, so the future benefit is speculative with no verification path available
today, while GT-7?'s 4%-10% recurring capacity tax is a near-certain, immediate cost. A chain
built on a speculative, unpriceable future benefit cannot outweigh an already-estimated near-term
cost, so this path was not usable as a competing conclusion.

**What it ruled out:** It rules out "pre-emptively decompose to shape future org growth" as a
justification distinct from the scaling and headcount justifications already tested in C1 and
C3, and it is the entry that C2, C3, C4, and C6 point to for resolving the Inverse-Conway rival
reading.

### Dead End 2: Recommend full microservices for hiring/perception reasons

**What was tried:** Considered whether hiring or market-perception benefits (a microservices
architecture reading as more sophisticated to some candidates) could justify adoption independent
of the operational trade-offs already modeled.

**Why abandoned:** This is reasoning by analogy to industry perception rather than a verified
ground truth about this specific team's hiring funnel or retention outcomes; per the
no-analogies-as-direct-evidence rule it cannot stand as justification without a ground truth
about this team's actual hiring results, and none was established or requested. It also directly
trades against C4 and C5: the same six people would spend part of their time on plumbing rather
than shipping, which is itself a documented retention risk in the opposite direction (GT-1's
productivity premium).

**What it ruled out:** It rules out "architecture choice as a recruiting signal" as a
first-principles justification for this decision; if hiring or perception is a genuine business
goal, it should be evaluated as its own decision with its own named ground truths (e.g., actual
candidate survey or offer-acceptance data), not folded into this architecture decision.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Invest in a disciplined, boundary-enforced modular monolith now — keep
one deployable, harden its internal module boundaries with automated enforcement, and defer full
microservices decomposition (chain C6).

**Key insight:** The "FAANG does it, so we should too" argument is self-defeating on its own
terms: at 1,200 DAU this team's load sits two to four orders of magnitude below where independent
service-level scaling starts to matter, and Amazon's own Prime Video team hit the identical
failure pattern — paying the microservices premium with no problem large enough to repay it —
and reversed it, saving 90% of operating cost (chain C4).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This recommendation accepts weaker fault isolation than full
microservices would provide (a single bad deploy can still affect the whole product) and defers
the independent-scaling headroom microservices would offer, in exchange for materially lower
recurring operational tax and a near-term velocity advantage; the modular-monolith option's edge
over doing nothing further is itself a near-tie, sensitive to how much the team values future
extraction optionality (chain C6).

**Flip conditions:**
- Headcount grows past roughly 10–12 engineers organized into genuinely separate sub-teams, each
  owning a distinct business capability (chain C2, chain C3).
- Real measured peak load exceeds roughly 1,000–2,000 sustained requests/sec with no
  horizontal-scaling headroom left on the current setup (chain C1).
- A single workload emerges with a resource profile that cannot reasonably be co-scaled with the
  rest of the monolith, such as GPU- or ML-bound processing (chain C1).

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1?** The CI pipeline's measured duration is reported as 47 minutes — unverified: stated as a premise of this scenario with no independent source; the measurement definition (mean/median/p95, wall-clock vs. compute-time, which branch/environment) is not specified. Real-world use requires independent confirmation via CI telemetry before acting on it.
- **GT-2?** Developers have begun batching merges as a workaround specifically to avoid triggering the pipeline — unverified: stated as a premise of this scenario with no independent source (survey data, PR-size trend) confirming the causal driver or magnitude.
- **GT-3?** Leadership's proposed intervention is to purchase faster CI runners — unverified: stated as a premise of this scenario; whether "faster" means per-unit speed, fleet concurrency, or both is not specified.
- **GT-4** Amdahl's Law: S(s) = 1/(1 − p + p/s), where p is the fraction of execution time that benefits from a speedup of factor s; as s→∞, S(s) asymptotically approaches 1/(1−p) — the non-accelerable fraction sets a hard ceiling on total achievable speedup — source: Wikipedia, "Amdahl's law"; read-at-source: the "Formula" and "Critical Insight About Maximum Speedup" sections, quoted directly ("Even with infinite acceleration... the speedup asymptotically approaches 1/(1-p)").
- **GT-5** A CI pipeline's wall-clock duration is a sum of heterogeneous stages — environment/container provisioning, dependency resolution/download, source checkout, compile/build, test execution (itself possibly serial and/or parallel, with retries on failure), artifact packaging/upload, and any sequential quality gates — each of which may be CPU-bound, I/O/network-bound, fixed-cost regardless of hardware, or serialized by dependency or policy — provenance: definitional/architectural (general CI/CD system structure), not a disputed empirical figure.
- **GT-6** Faster CPU/memory/disk hardware reduces wall-clock time only for the portions of a job that are compute/IO-bound on that specific resource and not blocked by external network latency, fixed-cost operations, or serialization/sequencing constraints — provenance: definitional, direct logical corollary of GT-4 + GT-5.
- **GT-7** Non-deterministic ("flaky") test failures that trigger automatic retries add wall-clock time proportional to retry count times the execution/timeout duration of the retried tests; this added time is a function of test determinism and retry policy, not of CPU clock speed — provenance: definitional/logical corollary of GT-6.

- **GT-9** DORA identifies working in small batches as a capability that "predicts software delivery performance and organizational performance," citing faster feedback and easier remediation of problems as the mechanism — source: dora.dev, "Working in Small Batches" capability page; read-at-source: quoted verbatim, "predicts software delivery performance and organizational performance" and "It reduces the time it takes to get feedback on changes, making it easier to triage and remediate problems."
- **GT-10** Larger batch sizes (more changes integrated in a single merge, as produced by merge-batching behavior) increase the surface area and complexity of any single integration, which increases the difficulty of isolating the cause of a failure when one occurs — provenance: definitional/logical, a direct consequence of batch size on diagnostic search space.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not approve "buy faster runners" as the primary or sole fix. Commission a short (1–2 week) diagnostic pass — a stage-by-stage pipeline timing breakdown, the flaky/retry rate, current runner queue-wait and utilization, and the current state of caching and parallelization — and act on what it shows; in parallel, default to the higher-ranked architectural levers (fast-feedback/full-gate split, test-and-build parallelization, dependency caching), which this analysis's reasoned trade-off found to dominate hardware spend across most plausible scenarios (chain C6).

**Key insight:** "47 minutes" and "developers are batching merges" are two different problems that a runner upgrade only partially and unreliably touches. Amdahl's Law guarantees a hard floor on pipeline duration that no hardware purchase can cross, and the specific batching behavior leadership wants eliminated may be driven by flakiness, queueing, or the pipeline's blocking-gate design rather than by raw compute duration — none of which "faster runners" reliably fixes (chains C1, C2, C3).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** A runner upgrade is the lowest-effort, fastest-to-ship option among those examined, and it is not being ruled out permanently — it may be the right complementary move once the compute-bound fraction is actually measured. The diagnostic-first path costs a small delay and some engineering time that spend-averse leadership may resist, and a capped, low-regret fleet-concurrency purchase can run in parallel with the diagnostic as a pressure-release valve without becoming the default answer, provided the ownership/SLO gap identified in the adversarial pass is closed at the same time (chain C5).

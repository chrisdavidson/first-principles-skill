# The Analysis

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** What decision rule should govern whether a specific university course replaces its timed written exam with a take-home assessment, given that LLM writing assistants can now perform much of the unaided-writing task those exams were built to certify?

The question as commonly posed — "should the university switch?" — treats the policy choice as a single, global, binary decision. Stripped of that framing, the real question is narrower and more useful: for a given course, which format (and under what conditions) validly certifies the specific competency that course exists to certify. "Timed exam vs. take-home" turns out to be a proxy for at least two underlying design axes — how much time-pressure the task imposes, and how much tool/AI access it permits — and the right setting on those axes depends on what is being certified, not on a university-wide policy preference.

**Success criteria:**
1. The Conclusion names, for at least two distinct competency types, which assessment format each type should use, and the criterion that distinguishes them.
2. The Conclusion states what happens to the claim "timed exams guarantee integrity while take-home cannot," and grounds that statement in a derivation chain rather than an assertion.
3. The Conclusion names at least one institutional precondition — beyond the format choice itself — required for the recommended policy to remain valid over time.
4. A reader can apply the Conclusion's decision rule to a course not explicitly discussed in this analysis (e.g., "a sophomore organic chemistry course," "a graduate creative-writing workshop") without needing to ask the author for further clarification.

## 3. Ground Truths

**Reduce-to-primitives pass (five-whys, reduce-to-primitives mode), feeding GT-9.** The compound claim "authorship of a submitted work product can be verified" was decomposed before being trusted: it rests on (a) the format restricting access to unauthorized aid during production, (b) the format allowing direct observation of the production process, and (c) direct observation, where available, disclosing the identity of the cognitive agent producing the work. Constituent (c) is itself reducible: observation directly discloses behavior and presence (measurable), but connecting that behavior to *authorship* requires an inference step, because cognition itself — the process being certified — is private and has no direct external measurement. That inference step does not reduce further; it bottoms out at a definitional/logical primitive about what observation can and cannot disclose. That primitive is recorded below as GT-9. Constituents (a) and (b) remain format-dependent current constraints, not physical laws, and are captured in GT-5 and GT-10? rather than promoted to the same status as GT-9.

## 4. Derivation Chains

**Companion techniques applied in this section:** an inversion pass (Phase 2) tested the claim "take-home assessment validly certifies course competency" against what would guarantee its failure; a theoretical-limit derivation (below, feeding C1) establishes the integrity ceiling either format can reach; two trade-off analyses (below, feeding C2 and C3) weigh timed-exam and take-home variants against a locked, weighted criteria set for two different competency types; and a second-order pass (feeding C4) extends the resulting policy through the actor and time lenses.

**Inversion (Phase 2), applied to "take-home assessment validly certifies course competency."** Inverted: "take-home assessment does not validly certify course competency." Failure-guaranteeing conditions enumerated: (1) the assessed task can be fully completed by AI with no verification step catching non-attributable output; (2) the institution asserts the credential certifies unaided competency while no longer measuring it; (3) no corroborating authorship signal exists at all beyond self-report; (4) the course's certified competency is, in fact, unaided time-pressured performance, and take-home is the wrong format for it; (5) automated detection is relied on as the sole integrity control despite its documented unreliability. Deriving the necessary preconditions for the *original* (un-inverted) claim to hold: **P1** — the course's certified competency does not require unaided, time-pressured performance (load-bearing; this is GT-7?/A6's role); **P2** — at least one non-detector corroborating authorship signal exists (load-bearing; this is GT-10?/A9's role); **P3** — the institution accurately discloses what the credential certifies (load-bearing; this is A16's role). All three preconditions are unverified or current-constraint assumptions already carried in the Assumptions Table (A6, A9, A16), which is why they were not re-added as new rows — the inversion pass confirmed, rather than expanded, the assumption set, and its five failure conditions map directly onto the Adversarial pass's Clusters 1–3 below.

**Theoretical-limit derivation, feeding C1.** Direction: higher is better (more confidence in authorship attribution is the improving direction). Governing constraint: GT-9 — no external observer, proctored or not, can directly observe another person's cognitive process; only behavior and its artifacts are observable. Ideal ceiling: bounded confidence approaching, but never reaching, certainty, achieved by stacking multiple independent behavioral proxies (identity verification, timing, process logs, oral questioning) — never a deductive guarantee. Best demonstrated: in-person, single-proctor timed exams, which in practice achieve high (not perfect) confidence via one dominant proxy — continuous physical presence — and are vulnerable to rare but real defeat conditions (impersonation, concealed devices, leaked questions). Conventional figure (untreated take-home): confidence resting on a single, weak, evadable proxy — automated AI-text detection (GT-2). Gap 1 (conventional → best demonstrated): closeable now, without new technology, by adding any second behavioral proxy (oral defense, process log) — this is exactly what the "redesigned take-home" option in C2 does. Gap 2 (best demonstrated → ideal ceiling): irreducible; no format, including in-person proctoring, closes it, because GT-9 is a constraint on observation itself, not on any particular technology.

### Conclusion C1: No assessment format — timed-proctored or take-home — can guarantee individual authorship; both rely on imperfect proxies for a fact that cannot be directly observed

GT-9 (observation verifies presence and behavior, not cognition) + GT-2 (independent AI-detector false-positive rate ~61% on non-native-speaker writing, near-zero on native writing)
→ in-person proctoring and automated AI-text detection are both inference mechanisms that reason from behavioral proxies to a claim about an unobservable cognitive origin, not direct measurements of that origin
→ because both mechanisms are inferential rather than deductive, the achievable integrity ceiling for any single assessment format is bounded confidence rather than certainty
→ describing timed proctored exams as "guaranteeing" authentic authorship while take-home assessments "cannot" verify it overstates a difference of degree as a difference of kind

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Continuous biometric/webcam-and-keystroke monitoring of all take-home work

**What was tried:** Considered as a fifth trade-off option, on the reasoning that if the concern is unverifiable authorship, the direct fix is continuous identity and behavior monitoring during take-home work, closing most of the gap identified in C1's theoretical-limit derivation.

**Why abandoned:** Eliminated by the trade-off's must-have knockout — it is not deliverable at realistic institutional scale without infeasible cost and privacy exposure (surveillance of students' homes), and it does not close the irreducible gap identified in GT-9 regardless of how much is spent on it, since GT-9's constraint is about observation of cognition, not about the fidelity of behavioral monitoring.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future reader from re-proposing "just monitor harder" as a solution to the integrity question; GT-9 shows this approach hits a hard ceiling before it reaches certainty, and it fails the feasibility/equity criteria long before that ceiling is relevant.

### Dead End: A blanket return to closed-book, no-device, all-courses timed exams as the integrity fix

**What was tried:** Considered as the mirror-image rival to the naive "switch everything to take-home" plan — if AI availability is the problem, restore full physical proctoring and closed-book conditions everywhere, eliminating AI access at the source.

**Why abandoned:** Contradicts GT-7? for Type II/III courses (construct mismatch — these courses do not claim to certify unaided rapid recall) and A3/GT-11? (fails to prepare students for professional AI-augmented practice, the opposite equity/relevance failure to the one this analysis is trying to avoid). It is not a genuine rival to C5 as a whole; for Type I courses specifically it collapses into C3's own recommendation and adds nothing beyond it.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future reader from treating "just ban AI everywhere" as a clean universal fix; it is the correct answer only for the subset of courses C3 already identifies, and is a construct-validity failure everywhere else.

### Dead End: Relying primarily on AI-detection software as the integrity backbone for take-home assessments

**What was tried:** Considered as the cheapest, most scalable integrity control for a take-home policy — flag suspected AI-generated submissions automatically and act on the flag.

**Why abandoned:** GT-2 (read at source) shows detector false-positive rates average 61.3% on non-native-English-speaker writing, against near-zero on native-speaker writing, making this control both unreliable and systematically discriminatory if used as a primary, high-stakes signal.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future reader from treating detector output as sufficient evidence on its own; it is retained in the redesigned take-home option (C2) only as a weak secondary signal, never as the sole basis for an integrity finding (Adversarial pass, Cluster 4 disposition).

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not choose once, university-wide, between timed written exams and take-home assessments. Retain timed, resource-restricted exams for courses whose stated competency is unaided performance under time pressure, and adopt verified, AI-disclosed take-home assessment — paired with a brief oral defense or process-log check — for courses whose stated competency is producing well-reasoned work with the resources a practitioner would actually have; require a resourced, disclosed verification step as a precondition of every take-home adoption (chain C5).

**Key insight:** The comparison is not "which format is cheat-proof" — no format is (chain C1) — it is "which format matches the specific competency a given course certifies," and that single criterion produces opposite winners for different course types (chains C2, C3), which is why a single global policy is the wrong shape of answer regardless of which one format is chosen.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The differentiated policy costs administrative consistency — multiple standards across a single transcript — and is only stable if institutions actually resource the verification step; left unresourced, it risks eroding back into a uniform policy that undermines its own foundational-course branch (chain C4). For Type I courses specifically, the margin between the closed-book and open-book timed exams is narrow enough (83 vs. 79) that a reasonable institution could choose either without contradicting this analysis (chain C3).

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** (physical law): Second Law of Thermodynamics / Carnot's theorem — no heat engine operating between reservoirs at Th and Tc (Kelvin) can exceed η_Carnot = 1 − Tc/Th, regardless of working fluid, cycle type, or number of stages.
- **GT-2** (measurement): Solar salt (60% NaNO₃ / 40% KNO₃), the incumbent CSP storage medium, thermally decomposes above ~600 °C, capping practical hot-tank temperature at ~565–585 °C (≈838–858 K).
- **GT-3** (measurement): Next-generation chloride eutectic salts (e.g., MgCl₂–KCl–NaCl) are chemically stable to ~800 °C (≈1073 K) and are the DOE/CSP roadmap's target chemistry specifically because they push the hot-reservoir ceiling higher.
- **GT-4** (current constraint, site-dependent): Ambient heat rejection via wet or dry cooling sets the cold reservoir at roughly 290–313 K depending on climate and cooling technology.
- **GT-5** (physical law, irreversible thermodynamics): Curzon–Ahlborn finite-time-thermodynamics bound — an engine that must deliver nonzero power (as any real plant must) has a maximum-power efficiency of η_CA = 1 − √(Tc/Th), which is strictly below Carnot and is the more physically honest ceiling for a real generating plant.
- **GT-6** (measurement): Deployed molten-salt CSP plants (e.g., Noor III, Crescent Dunes) achieve gross Rankine-cycle efficiencies of ~40–42% at ~565 °C hot-side.

## 4. Derivation Chains (Focused Theoretical-Limit Analysis)

**Governing law named:** Second Law of Thermodynamics, expressed via Carnot's theorem (GT-1), tightened by the finite-time/finite-power correction (GT-5). Convention stripped: turbine metallurgy, generator losses, and current Rankine-cycle design choices — none of these appear in either formula. What remains is temperature alone.

**Derivation chain 1 — reversible (idealized) ceiling, today's chemistry:**
```
GT-1 + GT-2 + GT-4 → [Th = 838 K (565°C solar salt), Tc = 313 K (typical wet-cooled sink)]
                    → η_Carnot = 1 − 313/838 ≈ 0.627 (62.7%)
```
This is the absolute reversible ceiling for solar-salt-temperature heat — no cycle, however exotic, can beat it.

**Derivation chain 2 — reversible ceiling, next-gen chloride salt:**
```
GT-1 + GT-3 + GT-4 → [Th = 1073 K (800°C chloride salt), Tc = 313 K]
                    → η_Carnot = 1 − 313/1073 ≈ 0.708 (70.8%)
```
Pushing the salt chemistry raises the ceiling by ~8 points — this is a *chemistry* lever, not a turbine lever.

**Derivation chain 3 — realistic finite-power ceiling (the more honest "law-permitted" number):**
```
GT-5 + GT-2 + GT-4 → [Th = 838 K, Tc = 313 K]
                    → η_CA = 1 − √(313/838) = 1 − √0.3735 ≈ 1 − 0.611 ≈ 0.389 (38.9%)

GT-5 + GT-3 + GT-4 → [Th = 1073 K, Tc = 313 K]
                    → η_CA = 1 − √(313/1073) = 1 − √0.2917 ≈ 1 − 0.540 ≈ 0.460 (46.0%)
```
This is the key result: a plant that must actually deliver power (not run infinitely slowly) is bounded well below the reversible Carnot number — and this bound, not Carnot, is the fair comparison to real plants.

**Bracket: law-permitted ceiling vs. conventional figure**

| Salt chemistry | Reversible (Carnot) ceiling | Finite-power (Curzon–Ahlborn) ceiling | Conventional figure (GT-6) | Gap (vs. CA ceiling) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solar salt, 565°C | 62.7% | 38.9% | ~40–42% | ≈ 0 to slightly negative — today's best plants are already near the finite-power bound for this chemistry |
| Chloride salt, 800°C | 70.8% | 46.0% | not yet commercial | ~4–5 points of headroom once this chemistry is commercialized |

**Interpretation of the gap:** Most of the gap between the reversible Carnot number (63–71%) and reality is *not* recoverable engineering slack — it is the irreducible cost of running an engine at finite rate (GT-5), which is itself a second-law consequence, not a convention. The genuinely convention-bound (recoverable) headroom is the ~4 points available by moving from solar salt to chloride salt (raising Th from 838 K to 1073 K) — that gap is closeable by chemistry/materials engineering, not thermodynamics. Beyond chloride salts, further gains require either raising Th further (limited by decomposition temperature of any candidate salt — a chemical, not thermodynamic, ceiling) or lowering Tc (limited by ambient environment).

**Second-order check:** Raising Th to chase efficiency increases corrosion rates and reduces salt/component lifetime (a real second-order cost) — this doesn't contradict any Ground Truth, it just means the "law-permitted" ceiling and the "economically sane" ceiling diverge further as Th rises. No contradiction with GT-1–GT-6, so no return to Phase 2 is required.

## 6. Conclusion

Setting aside current engineering practice, the laws permit at most:

- **Reversible (Carnot) ceiling:** ~63% at today's salt chemistry (565°C solar salt vs. ~40°C ambient sink), rising to ~71% with next-generation chloride salts (~800°C).
- **Realistic finite-power ceiling (Curzon–Ahlborn):** ~39% at 565°C, rising to ~46% at 800°C — this is the more physically meaningful "theoretical limit" for a plant that must actually generate power rather than run infinitely slowly.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the Carnot figures (GT-1, GT-2, GT-3, GT-4 are all well-established physical law or direct measurement). MEDIUM-HIGH for the Curzon–Ahlborn figures — GT-5 is an established, peer-reviewed result but is a *model* of finite-time engines, not a hard universal bound the way Carnot is; real cycles can beat or fall short of the max-power point depending on whether they're optimized for efficiency vs. power output.

**Bottom line:** Today's ~40–42% commercial molten-salt plants are already close to the finite-power (Curzon–Ahlborn) ceiling for their salt chemistry — they are not leaving much on the table at fixed temperature. The real lever the laws leave open is raising the hot-reservoir temperature via salt chemistry (chloride/carbonate salts), which could plausibly push the realistic ceiling from ~39% toward ~46%, with the reversible Carnot bound sitting even higher (~71%) as the absolute, unreachable-in-finite-time outer limit.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1**: The Second Law of Thermodynamics forbids any heat engine operating between a hot reservoir at Th and a cold reservoir at Tc from exceeding efficiency η = 1 − Tc/Th (Carnot bound). *Physical law, irreducible.*
- **GT-2**: Solar-salt (NaNO₃/KNO₃) CSP plants operate with a hot tank near 565°C (838 K) and a cold tank near 290°C (563 K), due to salt decomposition risk above ~600°C and freezing near 220°C. *Materials constraint, widely documented.*
- **GT-3**: For a reservoir that itself cools from Th to Tc_reservoir while delivering heat to a Carnot-limited engine rejecting to ambient T0 (i.e., a finite/sensible-heat source rather than an isothermal one), the maximum extractable work fraction is η_max = 1 − [T0·ln(Th/Tc_reservoir)] / (Th − Tc_reservoir). This is the standard finite-source exergy result and is strictly ≤ the naive Carnot bound using Th alone. *Derived from GT-1 by integrating Carnot efficiency over the reservoir's temperature glide — irreducible consequence of GT-1, not a new law.*
- **GT-4**: Ambient heat rejection temperature (T0) for a terrestrial power plant is realistically 15–25°C (288–298 K) via air/water cooling. *Direct measurement / typical siting conditions.*
- **GT-5?** (unverified, flagged): Alternative salt chemistries (chloride, carbonate, fluoride eutectics) remain liquid at 700–1000°C+, meaning "molten salt" as a category is not physically bounded at 565°C — that ceiling is specific to nitrate salt chemistry.

Conventional figure: current molten-salt CSP plants (nitrate salt, subcritical/supercritical steam Rankine cycle) report net thermal-to-electric conversion around 40–42%. Embedded conventions: (1) nitrate salt chemistry capping Th at 565°C, (2) steam Rankine power block rather than supercritical CO₂ or other advanced cycles, (3) treating the salt informally as if it were a simple hot reservoir.

**Step B — Strip to the governing law.**
GT-1 (Second Law → Carnot bound) is the governing law. GT-3 refines it for the specific physical reality that molten salt is a *sensible-heat* store (finite heat capacity, temperature glide), not a phase-change/isothermal reservoir — this is a stripped convention, not an added assumption, since ignoring the glide silently overstates the limit.

**Step C — Derive the law-permitted limit.**

```

```
This is an *overstatement* because it assumes the salt stays at 838 K throughout heat delivery.

```

```
This is the **true physical ceiling for nitrate-salt sensible-heat storage over its standard 290–565°C operating range** — the correct answer to "what do the laws actually permit," given the salt chemistry convention held fixed.

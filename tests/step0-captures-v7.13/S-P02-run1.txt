# Inversion Analysis: "Faster shipping means better retention"

## 1. Problem Essence

**Essence Statement:** Determine the conditions under which the causal claim "shipping cadence (2-week cycles) → improved retention" breaks down, so the team knows when to stop trusting cadence as a retention lever.

**Success criteria:**
- Produces a list of necessary preconditions the claim silently depends on
- Each precondition is classified as verified / conventional / untested
- Ends with a concrete signal set the team can monitor to know when the assumption has broken down

## 2. Inverted Claim

**Original:** "Faster shipping (biweekly releases) causes better retention."
**Inverted:** "Faster shipping does *not* improve retention — and may actively harm it."

## 3. Failure-Guaranteeing Conditions

Conditions that would *guarantee* the inversion holds (at least five):

1. **Ships add features/changes users didn't ask for or don't need**, increasing cognitive load without addressing the actual churn driver.
2. **Ships introduce regressions/instability faster than QA can absorb** — biweekly cadence outruns the team's testing capacity, so users experience more bugs, not fewer.
3. **Retention is driven by a factor uncorrelated with release velocity** (e.g., pricing, onboarding, support quality, market fit) — shipping fast optimizes a variable that isn't load-bearing for churn.
4. **Users don't have time to learn/adopt one change before the next lands**, causing change fatigue and perceived instability ("this app keeps changing under me").
5. **Shipping cadence is sustained by cutting corners** (skipped tests, rushed design, no user research) — so "fast" ships are systematically lower quality than slower ones would be.
6. **The team measures activity (ships shipped) instead of outcome (problems solved)** — Goodhart's Law: cadence becomes the target and retention-relevant work gets deprioritized in favor of shippable-but-shallow work.
7. **Retention is already saturated/at ceiling** for the current user base — no amount of additional shipping moves a metric that has hit its natural ceiling for reasons outside product control (e.g., market saturation, pricing changes elsewhere).

## 5. Where the Assumption Breaks Down (routed as findings)

The inversion surfaces a converging pattern (cluster, per pre-mortem-style read): **every failure-guaranteeing condition and every unverified precondition points to the same root gap — the team has never established the causal mechanism connecting a ship to a retention outcome.** "We ship every two weeks" is a cadence fact; "faster ships → better retention" is an unproven causal claim riding on that fact.

The assumption breaks down specifically when:

1. **No retention-per-ship attribution exists.** If you cannot point to which of your last N ships moved retention and by how much, the claim is currently unfalsifiable in practice, not just theoretically shaky (P1, P6).
2. **Cadence and quality trade off.** The moment shipping frequency forces cut QA time, incident rate, or reduces user research per feature, faster shipping actively works against retention (condition 2, 5; precondition P2).
3. **The retention bottleneck lives outside product velocity** — e.g., in pricing, support response time, onboarding drop-off, or a market/competitive shift. Shipping faster in that world is optimizing the wrong lever entirely (condition 3, 7; precondition P3).
4. **Change fatigue exceeds adaptation capacity** for your specific user segment — this is highly product-dependent (a power-user B2B tool tolerates cadence very differently than a low-touch consumer app) (condition 4; precondition P4).
5. **Cadence has become a proxy metric the team optimizes for its own sake** ("ship every two weeks" as a ritual) rather than a means to an end — this is the Goodhart's Law trap and is worth checking for directly, since the way the question was phrased ("our team ships every two weeks") frames cadence as the fixed input, not the retention outcome as the fixed target (condition 6; precondition P5).

## 6. Recommended Next Step (Ground Truth Verification)

Before trusting the cadence→retention claim further, verify P1/P3/P6 together via a single test: pull the last 6-10 releases, tag each by type (bug fix / new feature / performance / UX polish), and check retention-cohort movement in the 2-4 weeks following each. This either produces a real ground truth (a specific ship-type does move retention) or falsifies the blanket claim (cadence itself is not the lever — content/type of ship, or something entirely outside shipping, is).

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** (physical law): Second Law of Thermodynamics — no heat engine operating between two reservoirs at temperatures T_h and T_c can exceed Carnot efficiency: η_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h (temperatures in Kelvin). *Source: irreducible — this is a primitive of thermodynamics, not derivable from anything simpler.*
- **GT-2** (current constraint, verified): Solar-salt (60% NaNO₃/40% KNO₃) is thermally stable up to ~600°C and is used in essentially all commercial molten-salt CSP plants (e.g., Crescent Dunes, Noor III) at ~565°C hot-tank temperature (838 K). *Source: published CSP plant specifications.*
- **GT-3** (current constraint, verified): Heat rejection in a thermal power plant is bounded below by ambient temperature at the cooling system; typical design point ~300 K (27°C) for a representative site with wet cooling. *Source: standard power-plant thermodynamic design practice.*
- **GT-4** (physical law, more restrictive and more physically relevant than Carnot for continuous power output): Curzon–Ahlborn endoreversible efficiency — for a heat engine delivering finite power (not the zero-power reversible limit Carnot describes), the efficiency at maximum power output is η_CA = 1 − √(T_c/T_h). *Source: Curzon & Ahlborn, Am. J. Phys. 43, 22 (1975) — derived from finite-time thermodynamics, itself grounded in the Second Law plus a heat-transfer-rate constraint.*
- **GT-5** (current constraint, verified): Commercial molten-salt CSP plants achieve gross thermal-to-electric conversion efficiencies of roughly 38–42% in practice. *Source: published CSP plant performance data.*
- **GT-6?** (untested belief, flagged): Next-generation chloride/carbonate salts can reach ~700–720°C hot-side temperatures for supercritical CO₂ cycles. *Source: DOE Gen3 CSP program targets — R&D-stage, not yet commercially deployed, so treated as unverified for present-day systems.*

## 4. Focused Theoretical-Limit Analysis

**Step 1 — Name the conventional figure and its embedded conventions.** The figure typically cited for molten-salt CSP is ~38–42% gross conversion efficiency (GT-5). Embedded conventions: single-reheat Rankine steam cycle, ~565°C max salt temperature (solar salt chemistry), turbine/generator mechanical and electrical losses, condenser approach temperatures, and heat-exchanger pinch-point losses. None of these are physical laws — they are engineering and materials choices.

**Step 2 — Strip to the governing law.** The governing law is the Second Law of Thermodynamics (GT-1), which sets the Carnot bound. But Carnot describes a reversible engine operating at zero net power — physically real, but not the relevant ceiling for a plant that must actually deliver power. The more physically honest ceiling for continuous electricity generation is the endoreversible (Curzon–Ahlborn) bound (GT-4), which still derives directly from the Second Law plus the unavoidable fact that heat transfer at finite rate requires a finite temperature gradient (irreversibility is not optional if power output is nonzero).

**Step 3 — Derive the limit.**

Using GT-2 (T_h = 838 K, solar salt) and GT-3 (T_c = 300 K):

- Carnot ceiling: η_Carnot = 1 − 300/838 = 1 − 0.358 = **64.2%**
- Curzon–Ahlborn (finite-power) ceiling: η_CA = 1 − √(300/838) = 1 − √0.358 = 1 − 0.598 = **40.2%**

GT-1 + GT-2 + GT-3 → [Carnot bound sets the absolute reversible ceiling at 64.2% for current salt chemistry] → **conclusion 1: no molten-salt plant using solar salt at 565°C can exceed ~64% conversion efficiency under any engineering, ever.**

GT-1 + GT-4 + GT-2 + GT-3 → [finite-power operation forces the achievable ceiling down from the reversible bound to the endoreversible bound] → **conclusion 2: for a plant that must deliver continuous, nonzero power, the physically meaningful ceiling is ~40.2%, not 64.2%.**

**Step 4 — Bracket the gap between the law-permitted ceiling and the conventional figure.**

| Reference point | Value |
|---|---|
| Carnot (reversible) ceiling, solar salt @565°C | 64.2% |
| Curzon–Ahlborn (finite-power) ceiling, solar salt @565°C | 40.2% |
| Actual commercial plant performance | 38–42% |
| Gap: Carnot to Curzon–Ahlborn | ~24 percentage points |
| Gap: Curzon–Ahlborn to actual practice | ~0–2 percentage points |

This is the central, somewhat counterintuitive finding: **current molten-salt CSP plants are already operating essentially at the physically meaningful thermodynamic ceiling**, not far below it as the naive Carnot comparison (64.2% vs. 40% actual) would suggest. The ~24-point gap to Carnot is *irreducible in the sense that matters* — closing it would require accepting zero net power output, which is not a usable power plant. The remaining 0–2 point gap to Curzon–Ahlborn is the only portion attributable to convention/engineering headroom (turbine polytropic efficiency, reheat staging, condenser design).

**Step 5 — Where the real headroom is: raising T_h, not squeezing the cycle.** Since both ceilings are monotonic in T_h, the lever that actually moves the ceiling is hotter salt chemistry, not better turbines. Using GT-6? (unverified, R&D-stage): chloride-salt systems at 720°C (993 K) paired with supercritical CO₂ cycles:

- Carnot ceiling: 1 − 300/993 = **69.8%**
- Curzon–Ahlborn ceiling: 1 − √(300/993) = 1 − 0.550 = **45.0%**

GT-1 + GT-4 + GT-6? → [raising hot-side temperature from 838K→993K raises the finite-power ceiling from 40.2%→45.0%] → **conclusion 3 (MEDIUM confidence — depends on unverified GT-6?): the next ~5 percentage points of achievable efficiency gain will come from salt/cycle chemistry (chloride salts + sCO₂ Brayton), not from incremental turbine or heat-exchanger optimization within the existing Rankine/solar-salt paradigm.**

## 6. Conclusion

The Second Law sets an absolute (Carnot) ceiling of **~64% for today's solar-salt chemistry (565°C)**, rising to **~70% for next-generation chloride salts (720°C)** — both measured against a ~300K ambient sink. But the *physically meaningful* ceiling for a plant that must deliver continuous power is the endoreversible (Curzon–Ahlborn) bound, which sits at **~40% for solar salt and ~45% for chloride salts**. Current commercial molten-salt CSP plants (38–42%) are already operating close to this finite-power ceiling — the large-looking "inefficiency" relative to Carnot (64%) is not recoverable engineering slack, it's the irreducible cost of extracting power at a nonzero rate. The genuine remaining headroom (a few points from cycle optimization, several points more from hotter salt chemistry) is real but modest compared to what a naive Carnot comparison suggests.

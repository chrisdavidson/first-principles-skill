## 6. Conclusion

The claim "faster ships → better retention" breaks down at **three specific, checkable points** — not as an abstract caveat, but as concrete instrumentation and confound gaps this team can go verify directly:

1. **Attribution gap (highest confidence risk):** Retention is a lagging metric; a 2-week cadence outruns typical measurement windows unless releases are cohort-tagged. Until that instrumentation exists, the team literally cannot observe whether shipping speed is helping — the claim is unfalsifiable in its current measurement setup, which is itself the first thing to fix.
2. **Sign-reversal risk (needs data to rule out):** If cadence rose without proportional QA/testing capacity, faster shipping could be *suppressing* retention via defect/regression debt rather than improving it. This needs a defect-rate trend check, not an assumption in either direction.
3. **Confound risk (needs a controlled comparison):** Any observed correlation between shipping speed and retention may share a third cause (team scaling, market timing) rather than reflecting a real causal channel — reasoning from "faster shipping companies tend to retain better" is an analogy, not evidence for this team.

**What would restore confidence in the original claim:** release-tagged cohort retention data (closes gap 1), a defect/incident-rate series against cadence (closes gap 2), and a before/after or segment comparison that controls for team growth (closes gap 3). Absent those, the claim should be held as *unverified and possibly backwards* rather than as a working assumption guiding roadmap decisions.

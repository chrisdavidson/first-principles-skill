The first-principles inversion analysis is complete. Here's what it found:

## The core finding

**The claim breaks down whenever ship *frequency* substitutes for ship *value*.** Cadence (how often you ship) and value (whether what you ship matters to users) are mathematically independent variables — so "faster = better retention" is fragile by construction, not by accident.

## Six conditions where the assumption breaks

1. **Relevance decoupling** — shipping fast, but not shipping what actually drives retention.
2. **Quality erosion** — regression/defect rate climbs with cadence (the most common real-world failure mode).
3. **Change fatigue** — users read constant UI/workflow churn as *instability*; support tickets and churn rise after releases.
4. **Retention ceiling** — already-sticky users can't get more retained, so speed adds cost with no upside.
5. **Reverse causality** — speed is a *result* of a healthy product, not its cause. Your correlation is fully consistent with this.
6. **Feedback starvation** — cadence outruns your ability to learn, so you ship more wrong things faster.

## The catch on your own data

Confidence was capped at **MEDIUM** because the load-bearing premise — that your two-week cadence actually correlates with retention — is itself unverified and possibly confounded with general product health.

## The single sharpest test

**Hold cadence fixed and vary value-per-release.** Regress retention on *value-per-release* rather than *release-count*. If retention tracks value but not raw frequency, the assumption has already broken — and the team should optimize for shipped value, not shipping speed.

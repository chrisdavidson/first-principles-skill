## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given that capable AI writing assistants are now cheap and ubiquitous, what assessment format(s) should a university use to validly verify that a submitted piece of student work reflects that student's own understanding, at a cost and equity profile the institution can sustain — and is "timed written exam" vs. "take-home assessment" even the axis that determines this, or is the real axis something else (static artifact vs. interactive/verifiable process)?

**Success criteria:**
1. The answer states which assessment format(s), or combination thereof, a university should adopt, and does not assume the only two options are "keep timed exams" or "replace with take-home" — a hybrid or discipline-differentiated answer is explicitly tested as its own option, not treated as a compromise between two poles.
2. The answer identifies what construct (individual authorship/understanding, subject mastery, writing skill, or authentic tool-augmented competence) each candidate format actually measures, and grounds the claim that AI assistants change the calculus in verifiable facts about current AI capability and detection reliability, not in intuition about "cheating gets easier."
3. The answer accounts for practical constraints — cost/scalability, equity (disability, language background, anxiety), and faculty/institutional feasibility — not validity alone.
4. The answer states a confidence level and names the specific evidence or condition that would change the recommendation (a falsification condition), rather than presenting a single format as permanently correct.

This statement was refined once during the analysis: Phase 4's theoretical-limit exercise (chain C4) showed that "timed vs. take-home" is not, on inspection, the axis that determines verification validity — "static artifact vs. interactive/adaptive checkpoint" is. That refinement is reported as a finding (see chain C4), not substituted silently for the original question; the original question is still answered directly in §6.
## 2. Assumptions Table

**Fishbone cause-map (why is writing-assessment integrity under strain now?).** Using the default six-category set (People, Process, Technology and Tools, Environment, Information, Resources): *Technology and Tools* — free/cheap LLMs (A5, A10); *Process* — generic task prompts and product-only grading with no process evidence (A6); *People* — grade-pressure incentives to outsource (A11), faculty time/training shortfalls (A4); *Environment* — the unsupervised take-home setting itself provides no aid-restriction (GT-10); *Information* — institutions lack real-time authorship telemetry and students/faculty hold asymmetric information about what AI can do (A5, A10); *Resources* — historically, high-verification methods (oral exams) were assumed too costly to scale, a claim this analysis tests directly (GT-7, Fermi estimate in §4). The discriminating observation that separates the Technology branch from the Process branch: assign a task requiring a personalized, live-context dataset and see whether it is still trivially solvable by copy-paste into a chatbot — if yes, the Technology branch (raw capability) is still binding; if no but a generic-prompt version of the same task is still solvable, the Process branch (task design) is the more actionable lever. Priority: Technology and Process are the most actionable branches; Resources is now less binding than historically assumed (see GT-7).

**Inversion (Phase 2): what would guarantee a naive "replace timed exams with undifferentiated take-home" policy fails badly?** Enumerated failure-guaranteeing conditions and their necessary preconditions: (P1) no independent verification of authorship exists beyond the final artifact — precondition: verification stays artifact-only; (P2) task prompts are generic/high-overlap with AI training data — precondition: tasks are not redesigned; (P3) grading weight is 100% on the final artifact with no process evidence (drafts, version history, checkpoints) — precondition: grading stays product-only; (P4) no oral/checkpoint sampling exists at all — precondition: no interactive verification is added. All four preconditions collapse onto the convention recorded as A6 below, which is why A6 is marked `load-bearing`.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1?** Turnitin's vendor materials claim approximately 98% accuracy and a false-positive rate under 1% for its AI-writing-detection feature, under specific internal test conditions (documents where more than 20% of the text is AI-generated) — source: vendor claim, cited via Algonquin College's January 2024 Turnitin AI-detector update memo and related secondary coverage; provenance: **reported-by-delegate** (WebSearch synthesis; the vendor's own methodology page was not opened by this analysis).
- **GT-2?** Weber-Wulff et al. (2023), *International Journal for Educational Integrity*, tested 14 AI-text detectors, including Turnitin, and found none reached 80% overall accuracy — source: journal article, cited via WebSearch synthesis; provenance: **reported-by-delegate** (journal article itself not opened).
- **GT-3** Liang et al. (Stanford, *Patterns*/Cell Press, 2023) tested 7 AI-text detectors on 91 TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers versus 88 essays by native-English-speaking US eighth-graders; the average false-positive rate on the non-native-speaker essays was 61.3%, while the native-speaker essays were accurately classified — source: Times Higher Education, "AI text detectors 'biased against non-native English speakers'," timeshighereducation.com/node/727071, reporting the Stanford/Liang et al. study; provenance: **read-at-source** — fetched directly; the figure "an average false-positive rate of 61.3 per cent" and the native-vs-non-native contrast were located and quoted verbatim in the fetched text.
- **GT-4?** Perkins et al. (2024) tested 7 AI-text detectors against lightly-modified (paraphrased) AI-generated text; baseline accuracy across detectors was 39.5%, dropping a further ~17.4 percentage points after modification, with Turnitin specifically falling from 50% accuracy (unmodified) to 7.9% (modified) — source: cited via WebSearch synthesis; provenance: **reported-by-delegate** (original paper not opened by this analysis).
- **GT-5** "Perception, performance, and detectability of conversational artificial intelligence across 32 university courses" (arXiv 2305.13934): after a Quillbot-paraphrase obfuscation attack, OpenAI's classifier's false-negative rate rose from 49% to 98%, and GPTZero's rose from 32% to 95% — source: ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.13934; provenance: **read-at-source** — fetched directly; the quoted false-negative-rate figures for both classifiers, before and after the obfuscation attack, were located in the fetched text.
- **GT-6** The same study (arXiv 2305.13934) found ChatGPT-generated answers matched or exceeded typical student performance in 9 of 32 university courses spanning 8 disciplines (evaluated via 10 questions/course, 3 student answers and 3 ChatGPT answers per question, 3 independent graders), performing best on factual-recall questions and worst on mathematics and "trick" questions — source: same fetch; provenance: **read-at-source** — same fetched page, methodology and course-count figures quoted directly.
- **GT-7** An NYU-affiliated instructor's AI-assisted oral-exam system administered 36 twenty-five-minute oral exams over nine days for a total cost of $15 ($0.42/student: ~$8 Claude as main assessor, ~$2 Gemini, ~$0.30 OpenAI, ~$5 ElevenLabs), versus an estimated ~30 hours of human grading time (~$750 at $25/hour) for an equivalent manually-graded assessment; the instructor's stated motivation was that students who submitted "suspiciously good" take-home work could not explain their own submitted decisions under two follow-up questions — source: the-decoder.com, "NYU professor fights AI cheating with AI-powered oral exams that cost 42 cents per student"; provenance: **read-at-source** — fetched directly; the cost breakdown, student/session counts, and motivating anecdote were located and quoted in the fetched text.
- **GT-8** A controlled 2025 study (Frontiers in Education, doi 10.3389/feduc.2025.1567709) comparing a 60-minute versus a 120-minute physiology exam found no significant relationship between baseline test anxiety and exam score in either condition (regression slopes not significantly different from zero), and no significant score difference between conditions (67.09% vs. 68.10%, p = 0.493); students given double time were significantly less likely to report wanting more time (31% vs. 85%); the authors note this contrasts with earlier research (Onwuegbuzie & Seaman, 1995, cited within) that found untimed conditions benefited high-anxiety students, and suggest a pre-exam expressive-writing intervention present in both arms of the 2025 study may account for the difference — source: frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1567709/full; provenance: **read-at-source** — fetched directly; performance figures and the contrast with prior literature were located and quoted in the fetched text.
- **GT-9?** A separate study found chapters assessed via take-home exam were answered correctly at a higher rate on a later unexpected final exam than chapters assessed in-class (timed), and students reported studying harder and believing they learned more under the take-home format — source: cited via WebSearch synthesis (statistics-course study); provenance: **reported-by-delegate**; not read — turn budget (deprioritized relative to GT-8, which was read directly and is the more decisive/contested input for the equity chain, C5).
- **GT-10** By definition, a take-home assessment is completed outside the evaluator's direct, continuous observation, so the institution cannot directly observe, in real time, who performs the work or what tools/collaborators are used during production — definitional truth; no external citation required.
- **GT-11** By definition, a timed, proctored, in-person written exam restricts a student's access to external tools, texts, and collaborators during the assessment window, to a degree that depends on the rigor of physical invigilation — definitional truth; no external citation required.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### 5-Whys (reduce-to-primitives): is "a timed exam validly measures individual understanding" actually irreducible?

Claim: "a timed, proctored written exam validly measures a student's individual, unaided understanding." Constituents: (1) "the exam restricts access to unauthorized aids" — reduces to a physical/procedural fact about room control; passes the stop test at the level of "the room's authorized materials list is enforced," a direct-observation fact. (2) "restricting authorized materials means the produced work reflects only the student's own unaided knowledge" — this further reduces to "no covert electronic aid (e.g., a discreet earpiece or smart-glasses feed) is present," which does **not** pass the irreducibility test as a physical law or definition: it is an enforcement-dependent, current-constraint fact whose truth varies with invigilation rigor and is eroding as covert AI-access hardware becomes smaller and cheaper (untested at the level of "how rigorously is this actually enforced across a given institution"). (3) "unaided performance under time pressure correlates with mastery of the material" — this constituent is not cleanly verified either; GT-8 shows the anxiety-performance relationship is contested in recent controlled research. Verdict: the parent claim is **not fully verified** — it is flagged `?` at the level of constituent (2) and contested at constituent (3) — which means "timed exam = cheat-proof and valid" is itself a convention (recorded as A7 in §2), not a settled ground truth. This decomposition is what motivates chain C4 below: the real discriminator between formats is not "timed" but "does the format require live, adaptive generation that a covert electronic aid or a pre-written artifact cannot silently supply."

### Conclusion C1: Automated AI-text detection cannot serve as the sole or primary authorship-verification mechanism for a take-home policy

GT-3 (61.3% false-positive rate on non-native-speaker essays, Stanford/Liang et al.) + GT-5 (detector false-negative rate up to 98% after a paraphrase obfuscation attack)
→ automated AI-text detectors misclassify a large share of non-native-English student writing as AI-generated
→ the same class of detectors also fails to catch a large share of actual AI-generated text once it is lightly paraphrased
→ because both failure directions are large and independent of one another, no single detector threshold can be tuned to avoid both without accepting one of the two error types at scale *[Assumes: A1 — these 2023-2024 detector failure modes still hold in 2026]*
→ automated AI-text detection cannot serve as the sole or primary authorship-verification mechanism for a take-home assessment policy

### Conclusion C2: A take-home artifact's validity as an individual-understanding measure depends on task/grading design, not on the take-home format itself

### Estimate (Fermi): cost of scaling AI-assisted interactive verification checkpoints across a mid-size university

Target quantity: total annual cost, in dollars, of running sampled AI-assisted oral/interactive verification checkpoints across a hypothetical mid-size university (15,000 students).

Decomposition: `Total cost = (course-enrollments/year) × (sampling fraction) × (cost/checkpoint)`.
- Course-enrollments/year = 15,000 students × 8 courses/student/year = 120,000 enrollments/year (illustrative, not sourced to a specific institution).
- Sampling fraction = 25% (illustrative — sampled, not universal, per the "sampled viva" pattern found in the oral-exam search results; this value is an assumption, not a ground truth).
- Cost/checkpoint: lower bound $0.42 (GT-7's actual pilot rate, 25-minute sessions), central $1.00 (rounding for overhead beyond a small pilot), upper bound $2.50 (accounts for the "~$1 per 10 minutes" scaling note plus a buffer for human spot-audit oversight, both found alongside GT-7 in the same search pass but not independently read at source — treated here as an unsourced upper-bound assumption, not a GT).

Central estimate: 120,000 × 0.25 = 30,000 checkpoints/year.
- Lower: 30,000 × $0.42 = **$12,600/year**.
- Central: 30,000 × $1.00 = **$30,000/year**.
- Upper: 30,000 × $2.50 = **$75,000/year**.

Comparator (same unit basis, scaled from GT-7): an equivalent fully-manual verification effort at GT-7's own $750/36-students rate (~$20.83/student) for the same 30,000 checkpoints would cost 30,000 × $20.83 ≈ **$625,000/year** — roughly **8x to 50x** more than the AI-assisted bracket above.

Decision-resolution stop check: both the lower ($12,600) and upper ($75,000) ends of the bracket are small relative to typical instructional-technology or assessment-administration budgets at a 15,000-student institution (which commonly run into the high six or seven figures — this comparator figure is not independently sourced and is offered only as illustrative color, not as a ground truth), and both ends are far below the ~$625,000 manual-equivalent comparator. Both ends of the bracket therefore drive the same conclusion — **affordable at institutional scale** — so the stop criterion is satisfied without further tightening.

### Theoretical limit: what is the ceiling on authorship-verification certainty, for any assessment format?

Direction: higher verification certainty is better → this is an **ideal ceiling**, not a floor.

Governing hard constraint: an evaluator (human or AI) can only observe input-output behavior at the assessment interface — what is submitted, said, or done during the observed window — and cannot directly observe the internal cognitive process that produced it. Call this the **behavioral-inference ceiling**: verification certainty is bounded by how much the observable response is decorrelated from the possibility that it was produced by a non-live, mechanically-reproducible generator with no requirement for live, adaptive generation.

Ideal ceiling: approached by a live, adaptive, interactive exchange where each follow-up question is generated in real time in response to the previous answer (an oral defense or equivalent). No static artifact — timed or untimed, proctored or not — can reach this ceiling, because a static artifact's information content is fixed at the moment of submission and can, in principle, always be produced by a sufficiently capable non-human generator with no live verification of process.

Best demonstrated: GT-7's implemented AI-assisted adaptive oral exam is the best-demonstrated approach in this analysis's evidence base, and it is inexpensive (§'s Fermi estimate).

Conventional figure: current majority practice — either purely timed-proctored static exams (high aid-restriction, zero interactivity) or take-home static artifacts (zero aid-restriction, zero interactivity) — sits below the ideal ceiling on the interactivity dimension in both cases, though timed-proctored sits closer on the aid-restriction dimension specifically (per GT-11).

Gaps: Conventional → best demonstrated is a practically closeable, already-demonstrated gap (adopting sampled interactive checkpoints, per GT-7 and the Fermi estimate). Best demonstrated → ideal ceiling is a residual gap that is **not fully closeable even in principle**: a live exchange can, in principle, itself be covertly aided (an off-screen human or AI feeding answers in real time), so "the ideal ceiling" is itself bounded by practical difficulty of covert real-time aid, not by an absolute logical guarantee — which is precisely assumption A2 and the falsification condition below.

### Conclusion C4: "Timed vs. take-home" is not the axis that determines verification validity; "static artifact vs. interactive checkpoint" is

C3 (LOW) + GT-10 (take-home = no real-time observation, by definition) + GT-11 (timed exam = restricts aids but is still static, by definition)
→ verification certainty is fundamentally bounded by how much the observable response is decorrelated from the possibility of a non-live, mechanically-reproducible artifact, per the behavioral-inference ceiling above
→ a static artifact, whether produced in a timed aid-restricted room or produced untimed at home, sits below this ceiling because its information content is fixed at submission and does not require live, adaptive generation
→ an interactive, adaptive checkpoint sits closer to this ceiling than either static-artifact format, though not at perfect certainty, because the live channel itself could in principle be covertly aided
→ on the specific axis of resistance to AI-outsourcing of composition, a timed exam and an untimed take-home are much closer to each other than either is to an interactive checkpoint, so timed-vs-take-home is not the axis that determines verification validity

### Trade-off analysis: which assessment format(s) should a university adopt?

**Options (including status quo and a composite):** (A) status quo — timed, proctored, in-person written exams for all assessment; (B) full replacement — undifferentiated, unproctored, take-home assessment with AI-tool status unspecified; (C) take-home with AI use explicitly disclosed/permitted and tasks/grading redesigned around that; (D) oral/interactive verification examination (AI-assisted, scalable) as the primary format; (E) hybrid/composite — untimed take-home process work (drafts, checkpoints) paired with a sampled or full interactive verification checkpoint.

**Must-haves (knockouts, applied before scoring):** M1 — must allow valid inference about an individual student's own understanding (authorship-verifiability); M2 — must be operationally deliverable at institutional scale within realistic faculty/budget constraints; M3 — must provide equitable access regardless of disability, home environment, or language background; M4 — must satisfy any applicable accreditation/licensing mandate for the specific program (context-dependent, not universal — see A3).

Applying the knockouts: **Option B fails M1** and is eliminated — per C1 and C2, an unverified take-home artifact cannot, by default, be assumed to reflect the submitting student's own unaided work, and Option B as defined adds no verification mechanism beyond the (unreliable, per C1) artifact itself. Options A, C, D, and E survive the knockouts and are scored below.

**Criteria, weights, and anchors** (1 = anchor floor, 5 = anchor ceiling, higher always better):
- Validity (weight 5): 1 = cannot distinguish AI output from student work at all; 5 = near-certain real-time verification of individual understanding.
- Scalability/cost (weight 4): 1 = requires >20 faculty/TA-hours per 30 students; 5 = requires <2 hours-equivalent per 30 students (per the Fermi estimate above).
- Equity (weight 4): 1 = strongly disadvantages non-native speakers/high-anxiety/disabled students; 5 = strongly reduces such disadvantage relative to a timed proctored exam.
- Authenticity to post-graduation tool-augmented work (weight 3): 1 = assesses skills never used professionally; 5 = mirrors how professionals actually produce work, including tool use.
- Implementation feasibility (weight 3): 1 = requires wholesale redesign with no existing playbook; 5 = deployable with current tools and minor redesign.
- Robustness to future AI capability growth (weight 4): 1 = validity depends on AI staying weak; 5 = validity holds regardless of future AI writing capability.

### Second-order effects (actor lens + time lens), extending the recommendation toward E

**Actor lens.** Students: shift study behavior toward being able to explain/defend their own work, which plausibly increases genuine engagement, but a subset (social anxiety, speech-disability, non-native spoken fluency — A12) face a new form of disadvantage that the previous format did not impose. Faculty/TAs: workload shifts from pure artifact-grading toward conducting/reviewing interactive checkpoints and toward auditing the AI tools now used to help grade them — a new oversight burden. Accreditors: may impose a proctored-format mandate on specific regulated programs regardless of institutional policy (A3) — an external constraint this general recommendation cannot override. Vendors: commercial "exam-coaching" services have historical precedent (contract-cheating, paraphrasing tools) of adapting to whatever verification method becomes standard; a market for real-time discreet AI-coaching devices is a plausible competitive response to widespread oral-checkpoint adoption.

**Time lens.** Immediate: transition costs, faculty training burden, student confusion over new formats. A few cycles (1-2 years): task banks and rubrics improve, sampled verification becomes normalized, and the integrity question shifts toward controlling the live channel itself (device sweeps, camera requirements). Long-run (multi-year): the interactive checkpoint's core advantage — resistance to AI-outsourcing — is not permanent; it is conditional on live/adaptive exchanges remaining harder to covertly assist than static text is today (per A2 and the theoretical-limit's residual gap). If that condition stops holding, the same arms dynamic that defeated text-based detection (C1) recurs at the live-checkpoint layer.

**Check against the decision's own success criteria (Phase 1):** does any second-order effect work against validly measuring understanding even where it is consistent with the ground truths above? Yes — using an AI system as the primary grader of oral checkpoints (as GT-7's implementation did) reintroduces a validity question on the grading side (subtle scoring bias, inconsistent rubric application) even though it solves the authorship question on the student side. This is not a contradiction of any Ground Truth (no route back to Phase 2 is triggered), but it is a genuine tension with the Phase 1 success criteria that is carried forward as a named risk (pre-mortem Cluster 1, §5 adversarial pass) rather than absorbed silently into the recommendation.

No enumerated effect above contradicts a Ground Truth; all extend chain C6 below as order-marked steps.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Treating the question literally as "replace timed exams with take-home assessments" (Option B)

**What was tried:** Evaluating the question at face value — a straight product swap from timed proctored exams to unverified, unproctored take-home assessment.

**Why abandoned:** Fails the M1 authorship-verifiability must-have; chains C1 and C2 establish that neither automated detection nor take-home format alone can verify that a submitted artifact reflects the submitting student's own unaided work once capable AI writing assistants are ubiquitous.

**What it ruled out:** Any recommendation that is a simple format swap without an accompanying verification-design change; this is why §6 does not recommend "take-home" as stated in the original question.

### Dead End: Relying on improved AI-text detectors as the primary fix

**What was tried:** Considering whether better/future detectors could rescue the take-home format without adding any interactive or process-based verification.

**Why abandoned:** GT-3 and GT-5 (both read-at-source) show current detectors fail in both directions (false-positive against non-native speakers, false-negative against trivially paraphrased AI text) for structural, architecture-level reasons, not merely due to immature tooling; corroborating GT-1?, GT-2?, GT-4? (not independently read, deprioritized under the turn-budget rule) point the same direction.

**What it ruled out:** Treating "wait for better detectors" as a sufficient institutional strategy. Not fully ruled out as impossible forever — retained as a live, unresolved rival to C1/C6 (see C1's and C4's confidence lines): emerging content-provenance/watermarking standards (e.g., C2PA-style credentials) are a real alternative direction this analysis did not independently verify, and could in principle restore static-artifact verification without an interactive checkpoint if they mature. This rival is named as a "would change it" factor rather than settled.

### Dead End: Treating "keep the pure status quo" as sufficient

**What was tried:** Considering whether the existing timed-exam regime remains adequate as-is, since it is not directly vulnerable to unsupervised AI-text generation the way take-home format is.

**Why abandoned:** The weighted trade-off (Option A = 82) and chain C4's reframing (static artifacts, timed or not, sit well below the verification ceiling on interactivity; the status quo also scores low on Authenticity and contested-but-present on Equity) show the status quo is dominated by the hybrid option E (101) and by oral-only D (91).

**What it ruled out:** Recommending "no change" as the best answer. Option A remains viable (it was not knocked out) and is named in §6 as an acceptable fallback for institutions that cannot implement E, not as the top recommendation.

### Dead End: Recommending oral-only (Option D) over the hybrid (Option E)

**What was tried:** Considering whether D should be the top recommendation instead of E, on the grounds that D avoids the take-home task-redesign burden that E requires.

**Why abandoned:** The weighted trade-off and flip test (§4) show E weakly dominates D on every individual criterion in the modeled scenario (E ≥ D on all six criteria, strictly greater on Equity and Authenticity), so no reweighting favors D over E.

**What it ruled out:** D as the top recommendation. D remains named in §6 as a reasonable partial-adoption path for institutions unable to redesign take-home tasks at all.

### Dead End: Treating the Fermi cost estimate as a blocking objection to interactive checkpoints

**What was tried:** Considering whether "oral exams don't scale/are too expensive" should rule out Options D and E outright, based on the historical assumption embedded in the fishbone's "Resources" branch (§2).

**Why abandoned:** The Fermi estimate (§4), anchored to GT-7, brackets institution-wide sampled rollout at $12,600-$75,000/year for a 15,000-student university — decisively affordable relative to the ~$625,000 manual-equivalent comparator, and both bracket ends drive the same "affordable" conclusion.

**What it ruled out:** Cost/scalability as a blocking objection to D/E. Retained instead as an accepted-risk item (pre-mortem Cluster 3, §"Adversarial pass" below) — vendor-pricing and single-case-generalization risk, not a fundamental infeasibility.
## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** A university should not simply replace timed written exams with undifferentiated, unverified take-home assessment; it should adopt a hybrid model that pairs untimed/take-home process work (drafts, checkpoints, personalized-context tasks) with a sampled or full interactive verification checkpoint such as an AI-assisted oral defense, tailored per discipline rather than mandated uniformly (chain C6). Where an institution cannot redesign take-home tasks at all, oral-only verification (Option D) is a reasonable fallback that still outperforms both the pure status quo and undifferentiated take-home replacement (chain C6).

**Key insight:** The question as posed assumes "timed vs. take-home" is the axis that determines whether an assessment validly verifies a student's own understanding. It is not: on the specific axis of resistance to AI-outsourcing of composition, a timed exam and an untimed take-home are much closer to each other than either is to an interactive, adaptive verification checkpoint, because both are static artifacts whose information content is fixed at submission (chain C4). The decision that matters is static-artifact vs. interactive-checkpoint, not timed vs. untimed.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This recommendation accepts a new faculty/TA oversight burden (conducting and auditing AI-assisted interactive checkpoints) in exchange for authorship-verifiability (chain C6). It accepts that the equity case for untimed/take-home components is weaker and more contested than commonly assumed — recent controlled evidence found no significant anxiety-performance relationship under timed conditions, contradicting older literature this analysis does not fully adjudicate (chain C5) — no chain — flagged assumption only for the specific claim that this contested state will resolve favorably. It accepts that the interactive checkpoint's cost-affordability rests on a single, unreplicated implementation case whose generalization is not yet verified (chain C3). It defers to program-specific accreditation requirements that may override this general recommendation for regulated professional programs (no chain — flagged assumption only, per assumption A3 in §2, which this analysis did not verify against any specific accreditor).

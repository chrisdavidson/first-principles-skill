## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** Stockholm's 2006 full-scale congestion-charging trial reduced car traffic crossing the cordon by approximately 22% during charged hours versus the prior year, with the effect stabilising within the first month of the seven-month trial (substantially exceeding the trial's own 10–15% target) — source: Eliasson et al. trial evaluation, as summarised on the "Eliasson Stockholm Trial 2006" page; read-at-source: page text quoted directly, "stabilised after the first month at around 22%."
- **GT-2** London's congestion charge launched 17 February 2003, roughly three years after the political commitment that created it (2000 mayoral manifesto), and traffic volumes within the zone were "consistently down at least 15% on pre-charge levels" within about a month of launch — source: "London congestion charge," Wikipedia; read-at-source: quoted passage on first-month traffic levels and the manifesto-to-launch timeline.
- **GT-3** In Toronto's conversion of Sherbourne Street from a painted to a physically protected bike lane, among the new cyclists using the corridor afterward, about 55% said they would have used transit anyway and about 24% said they switched from driving — source: SF Streetsblog (2016) reporting the Toronto study; read-at-source: quoted passage, "Most (55%) new cyclists would use transit before, while fewer (24%) potentially switched from driving."

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

**What was tried:** Initially treated GT-1, GT-2, GT-3, GT-4 as if their magnitudes applied directly to "a generic mid-sized city."

### Dead End: Recommending the raw trade-off tiebreak winner (B, free transit) as the final answer

**What was tried:** Considered stopping at chain C4 and recommending free transit, since it is the deterministic output of the trade-off procedure's own tiebreak rule.

**Why abandoned:** B's Effectiveness score (1/5, resting on GT-4's near-zero finding) directly conflicts with the literal target the mandate specifies (cut car journeys 20%); the tiebreak's own basis (fewer `?`-marked inputs among B's *winning* criteria) does not address the fact that B's winning criteria are themselves uncited preference judgments, not evidence about achieving the stated goal. ### Dead End: Treating GT-1/GT-2's ~15–22% cordon-traffic reduction as directly equal to a 20% commuter-car-journey reduction

**What was tried:** Considered reading London's and Stockholm's results as near-direct proof that congestion pricing alone clears the stated 20% bar.

**Why abandoned:** GT-1 and GT-2 measure general vehicle traffic crossing a cordon, not specifically home-to-work commute trips (A-12, unverified); the essence's target metric is narrower than what was measured in either case study.

**What it ruled out:** Rules out an unqualified claim that pricing alone "achieves the target"; chain C1 carries the A-12 caveat instead, and the Conclusion states this explicitly rather than treating the London/Stockholm figures as a proven match to the specific metric asked about.
## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Where the city holds (or can obtain) road-pricing authority, adopt the composite (E): congestion pricing on the core commute corridor, with its revenue funding a protected cycle-lane network on the highest-volume commute corridors and low-income transit-fare relief, rather than the raw trade-off's mechanically tied "free transit" outcome (chain C5). Where road-pricing authority is unavailable, no combination of free transit and cycle lanes alone plausibly reaches 20% within three years, and complementary demand-management tools outside these three levers are needed alongside them (chain C6).

**Key insight:** The empirical evidence on each lever individually is fairly clear-cut and each is only MEDIUM confidence on its own terms (chains C1–C3), but *which lever wins the comparison* is not determined by more data — it is determined by how much relative weight a decision-maker places on literally hitting the 20% figure versus cost, equity, speed, and feasibility. A single one-point shift in a single criterion's weight, on a 1–5 scale, flips the recommended option (chain C4/C5); the normative weighting question, not further measurement, is what should determine the choice.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Congestion pricing's fast-once-live effect is real, but historically most of a from-scratch 3-year mandate is consumed reaching "live," compressing the measurable benefit into a shortened tail of the window, and the case-study evidence used here (GT-1, GT-2) measures general cordon traffic rather than commuter trips specifically (chain C1). Cycle lanes deliver strong cost-effectiveness and durable co-benefits but only a small (0.1%–1.8%) share of the 20% target within three years (chain C3). The composite's equity-recycling component adds a recurring budget line that compounds annually, and pricing's own gains may erode over a longer horizon without periodic tariff adjustment (chain C5, second-order extension).

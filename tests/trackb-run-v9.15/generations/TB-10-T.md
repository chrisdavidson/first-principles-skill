## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** A family faces a decision to relocate near ageing parents that forces both adults to change jobs and the children to change schools; the question "what should actually drive the decision" is not answerable as a yes/no verdict for an unnamed family with unstated facts (income, the parents' actual health status, specific locations) — it is answerable as a decision procedure: what is the minimal set of causally load-bearing, independently checkable facts that determines which of the available options (not just "move" vs. "don't move") is superior for a given family, and at what point, if any, does the decision stop being resolvable by facts and become a genuine values trade-off?

**Success criteria:**

- The conclusion names the actual option set considered — including the status quo, at least one hybrid/staged option, and the "parents relocate instead" option — rather than treating this as a binary move/don't-move choice.
- The conclusion identifies which facts are load-bearing (their truth value flips the recommended option) as distinct from facts that are emotionally salient but do not change which option is best, and each load-bearing fact is stated as something a specific family could go verify this week, not as a generic value ("family matters," "career matters").
- The conclusion states an explicit, ordered procedure a family can run with their own numbers, such that two different families plugging in different verified facts at the gating step would legitimately reach different recommended options from the same procedure.
- The conclusion names the specific point, if any, at which the procedure runs out of facts and hands off to an acknowledged values trade-off (e.g., whose career yields, how much risk to accept), rather than either hiding that handoff or refusing to give a procedure because "it depends."
- The conclusion accounts for second-order and long-horizon consequences (career trajectory, marital load, the parents' own autonomy, children's developmental timing) and not only the first-order costs of the move itself (moving cost, job search, school transfer).

**Companion techniques applied in this analysis (full-composer mode):** fishbone (Phase 2, assumption-space brainstorm), inversion (Phase 2, challenging the "moving is obviously the loving/responsible choice" framing), five-whys in reduce-to-primitives mode (Phase 3, decomposing "moving closer will meaningfully help the parents" into verifiable primitives), theoretical-limit (Phase 4, bounding what proximity can physically buy), estimate/Fermi (Phase 4, bracketing the financial cost of simultaneous vs. staggered job changes), trade-off analysis (Phase 4, weighing five options including a composite/hybrid and the status quo), second-order effects (Phase 4, mandatory actor-lens and time-lens extension), and pre-mortem (Phase 5, the adversarial technique for this plan-type conclusion). A `Techniques not applied` note in the process-output appendix records the two invocation slots (inversion's Phase 5 slot; theoretical-limit's Phase 1 slot) that did not fire this run, and why.

### Companion technique: Inversion (challenging "relocating is obviously the responsible/loving choice")

The claim inverted is: *"Relocating near the ageing parents will make things better, on net, for the family and the parents."* This is the claim most likely to be treated as too obvious to examine — the mirror-image convention ("not moving is neglect") is treated in the table below as its own row (A7) rather than inverted separately, since the two conventions are mirror images and share the same failure analysis.

Failure-guaranteeing conditions (unfiltered, at least five):

1. The parents' need turns out to be minor or stable, so the anticipated caregiving payoff never materializes while the job/school costs are paid in full regardless.
2. Paid/professional care at the parents' location is actually adequate and available, making the physical-presence benefit redundant — the family pays a large cost for a marginal latency improvement.
3. The parents resent the move — feeling supervised or losing privacy — and the relationship quality falls rather than improves.
4. One or both adults cannot find comparable employment at the destination, producing income/career damage that outlasts the caregiving need (which may resolve via the parent's decline, move to assisted living, or death, on a timeline shorter than the career damage).
5. A child in a fragile developmental window (an already-difficult social transition, a continuity-critical therapeutic or educational need) suffers a compounding setback that outweighs the benefit to the grandparent relationship.
6. The move is later reversed because the situation resolved differently than assumed, and the family absorbs the job-change and school-change cost a second time.
7. The marriage absorbs disproportionate strain because the move is effectively unilateral (one spouse's parents, one spouse's sacrifice), damaging the partnership the household depends on for everything else, caregiving included.

## 3. Ground Truths

### Companion technique: 5-Whys, reduce-to-primitives mode

**Claim to verify:** "Moving closer to the ageing parents will meaningfully help them."

**Immediate constituents (one level):**
(a) The parents currently have, or will soon have, needs that are not fully met today.
(b) Those needs are of a kind that in-person physical presence addresses better than remote alternatives (calls, paid care, technology).
(c) The specific people moving (the adult child and spouse) would actually supply that in-person presence — i.e., have discretionary time/energy left over after work and their own household duties — not merely live nearby.
(d) The parents would accept/welcome that help being provided by this family member specifically, rather than resisting help or preferring a paid professional for reasons of dignity or privacy.

**Irreducibility test applied to each:**

- (a) Reducible further: "not fully met" requires a specific category (ADL assistance, medical monitoring, social/emotional support, household maintenance, financial management) before it is checkable. Without a named category and a documented basis, this bottoms out as **Assumed — unverified** (this is assumption A9).
- (b) Reducible: physical presence has a clear, physically-grounded advantage only for needs that are response-latency-sensitive (a fall, a medication-timing lapse, a wandering episode) — the subset addressed quantitatively in chain C2 below. For needs that are administrative, financial, or emotional-support in character, physical presence has no established latency advantage over phone/video contact plus periodic visits and paid help. The latency-sensitive subset is **Verified — physical/logistical fact** (grounded in GT-2 and the theoretical-limit chain, C2); the non-latency-sensitive subset remains **Assumed — unverified** pending an inventory of which specific needs the parents actually have (this is assumption A13).
- (c) Reducible to a capacity argument: the total discretionary time any working adult has left after a full-time job and sleep is bounded (GT-9, GT-10, GT-11; formalized in chain C2) — the existence of that ceiling is **Verified — physical/arithmetic fact**. Whether *this* family will actually allocate a meaningful share of its capped discretionary time to caregiving (rather than to their own children, rest, or each other) is a choice, not a fact, and is **Assumed — unverified**.
- (d) Not reducible further without direct information from the parents. **Assumed — unverified** (assumption A10).

**Parent-claim verdict:** the compound claim is verified only conditionally — it requires (a), (c)'s specific-allocation component, and (d) to all hold, and at most one sub-branch (the latency-sensitive half of (b)) is grounded in verified, non-family-specific fact. The compound claim as stated is therefore **not** a ground truth; it is flagged, decomposed, and carried forward as chain C1's finding rather than asserted directly.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Companion technique: Trade-off analysis (supporting material for chain C4)

**Options** (always includes the status quo and at least one composite):

- **O0 — Status quo:** no relocation, no change to the current support arrangement.
- **O1 — Stay + strengthen support:** no relocation; add paid in-home care / a local care-management service, medical-alert and monitoring technology, and more frequent scheduled visits.
- **O2 — Staged/partial move (composite):** relocate to a location that materially cuts travel time without full co-location; one adult may keep the current job remotely or change jobs later; the children's school change is timed to a natural break point.
- **O3 — Full relocation:** move directly into the parents' city/neighborhood; both adults change jobs; the children change schools immediately.
- **O4 — Reverse relocation:** the parents move near or in with the family instead; no job or school change for the family.

**Must-have gates (knockouts, checked before scoring, not folded into the linear scores):**

- Gate 1 (tail-risk/crisis coverage): if the parents have a documented, non-trivial safety-critical need not otherwise covered by an existing daily check-in, O0 is knocked out regardless of score.
- Gate 2 (financial survivability): if the household's savings/portable income cannot cover the upper end of chain C3's income-disruption bracket for both adults simultaneously, the *simultaneous* form of O3 is knocked out; the staggered variant from C3 remains available.
- Gate 3 (parental consent): if the parents are unwilling or unable to relocate (assumption A11 false), O4 is knocked out regardless of score.
- Gate 4 (paid-care substitutability): if assumption A16 is false — no adequate paid/professional care is actually available near the parents — O1's care-need-coverage score drops from 3 to roughly 1; recomputed below.

**Criteria, weights, and anchors:**

| Criterion | Weight | Anchor 1 | Anchor 5 |

| Care-need coverage | 5 | no additional support arranged beyond today | in-person daily-capable presence or equivalent professional daily coverage near the parent |
| Children's continuity | 4 | mid-year/mid-program forced transfer, no equivalent program | no school change at all |

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Score every option by its expected/average care benefit on a single linear 1–5 scale

**What was tried:** An earlier version of the trade-off matrix scored "care-need coverage" as one linear criterion meant to capture both routine convenience (how pleasant/easy day-to-day contact is) and tail-risk coverage (whether a fall, a wandering episode, or a missed-medication crisis gets a fast response) in the same 1–5 number, then applied the weight to that single blended score.

**Why abandoned:** This mirrors the expected-value-only fallacy documented in the mortgage worked example: averaging over a distribution hides the tail the decision is usually actually about. A linear blended score systematically under-prices a low-frequency, high-severity failure mode (an unwitnessed fall, a missed dose of a critical medication) the same way an expected-value-only investment comparison under-prices a left-tail market outcome. Once separated, chain C2's finding (proximity mainly buys response-latency compression, not more total hours) shows the tail-risk component is exactly the part latency-sensitive, and the routine-convenience component is exactly the part that is not — collapsing them back into one number erases the distinction the whole analysis depends on. The fix adopted instead is the explicit must-have gate structure in chain C4's supporting material (Gate 1, tail-risk coverage) rather than folding tail risk into the linear score.

**What it ruled out:** Treating "how good is the care coverage" as answerable by a single number. Any version of this analysis that blends routine and crisis coverage into one score will systematically overweight options that look pleasant day-to-day (like the unchanged status quo, O0) and underweight the specific risk that usually motivates the conversation in the first place.

### Dead End: This is fundamentally a two-option decision — move or don't move

**What was tried:** Frame the entire analysis as a binary choice, with the job-change and school-change costs weighed directly against "helping the parents," and resolve the binary by asking whether the benefit outweighs the cost.

**Why abandoned:** Assumption A17 records this framing and its Discard verdict. Once a staged/partial move (O2) and a reverse relocation (O4) are named as scoreable, viable alternatives in chain C4's trade-off matrix, the binary framing no longer describes the actual decision space, and — per the baseline weighting in C4 — it actively obscures the two options (O1 and O4) that outscore full relocation (O3) under non-acute-need assumptions. A binary framing cannot represent, let alone recommend, a composite or a reverse option by construction.

**What it ruled out:** Treating "move or don't move" as an exhaustive description of the choice. The methodology's requirement to test whether a split or a composite of the named options dominates either pole is not a formality here — under baseline weighting, the composite (O2) and the reverse option (O4) both materially change which option a purely binary analysis would have missed.

### Dead End: Moving closer is obviously the responsible/loving choice, so the job and school costs are simply the price of doing the right thing

**What was tried:** Treat assumption A6 (the convention that relocating is inherently the loving/responsible choice) as self-justifying, and use it to settle the decision without further verification — the job and school costs become an accepted cost of virtue rather than a variable to be weighed.

**Why abandoned:** The inversion procedure in Section 2 enumerates seven concrete, independently plausible conditions under which relocating makes things net-worse, not better — including the parents' need turning out to be minor, paid care being an adequate substitute, the parents resenting the loss of autonomy, career damage outlasting the actual care need, and the move being reversed later at double the cost. A convention that fails under multiple concrete, non-exotic failure conditions is not self-justifying; chain C1 formalizes the same point by showing the claim decomposes into sub-claims that are mostly unverified for any specific family. This dead end and its mirror image (A7: not moving is neglect) fail for the identical structural reason — both treat a values-laden convention as though it were a verified fact.

**What it ruled out:** Settling the decision by appeal to the "obviously right" framing on either side. Both directions of the convention collapse under the same inversion analysis, which is why chains C1 through C6 route the decision through verification and a trade-off instead of through either convention directly.

### Dead End: Plan the decision against a single assumed multi-year care-need horizon

**What was tried:** Treat the parents' future care trajectory as reasonably predictable — e.g., "we assume they'll need this level of support for about five years" — and size the relocation decision (whether the fixed cost of O3 is "worth it") against that assumed horizon.

**Why abandoned:** GT-8?'s finding on trajectories of functional decline shows this only holds for some trajectories (cancer's stable-then-rapid pattern is comparatively predictable in its final phase; sudden death offers no horizon to plan against at all) and specifically fails for the frailty/dementia trajectory, which is prolonged and low in predictability by the source's own description. Chain C4's third-order extension shows that assuming a fixed horizon for a frailty/dementia case specifically overstates the confidence with which the fixed cost of O3 can be amortized. This is why the staged option (O2) — which preserves the ability to convert into O3 later once the trajectory clarifies — is carried forward as a live option rather than the analysis simply picking a horizon and computing a verdict.

**What it ruled out:** Any single-point assumption about how long the anticipated care need will run, for a family whose parent is on (or might be on) a frailty/dementia trajectory specifically. The horizon is a genuine unknown, not a planning convenience, for that trajectory.
## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Verify the parents' care-need acuity and trajectory (a documented functional assessment, not an impression), whether paid or professional care can substitute at adequate quality near the parents, and the parents' own preference for proximity versus independence, before weighing any job or school cost (chain C5). Only after that gate is resolved does the job/school/financial trade-off among the surviving options become the load-bearing analysis, and within that trade-off, stagger the two adults' job changes rather than making them simultaneous, which preserves the full relocation benefit at roughly half the peak financial exposure (chains C4, C3). If a genuine tie remains among the surviving options once the gate is resolved and the trade-off is run, resolve it explicitly as a values trade-off — whose career yields, how much career risk the household accepts — rather than continuing to search for facts that do not exist (chain C6).

**Key insight:** The job and school costs that this kind of decision usually gets litigated on are not, in most cases, the load-bearing variable. The trade-off's own sensitivity structure shows its entire ranking pivots on one largely unverified fact — the parents' care-need acuity and whether it is substitutable by paid care — not on the career or schooling costs (chain C4). And the concrete, physically-grounded thing proximity buys is a compression of response latency for latency-sensitive needs, not an expansion of total caregiving hours, which are capped by ordinary work-and-sleep constraints regardless of address (chains C1, C2).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Under baseline, non-acute-need weighting, staying while strengthening paid/technology support (O1) and having the parents relocate instead (O4) both outscore full relocation with simultaneous job and school changes (O3) on every criterion except care-need coverage itself, so choosing full relocation without first verifying acuity risks paying the full career-and-school cost for a marginal benefit (chain C4). Even where full relocation is genuinely warranted, making both adults' job changes simultaneous doubles the household's peak financial exposure relative to a staggered changeover that preserves the same eventual benefit in full (chain C3). This analysis explicitly does not resolve one thing with more facts: whether relational presence carries value beyond the four instrumental sub-claims it can be decomposed into — that is named as the point where the procedure hands off to the family's own values rather than being smoothed over (chain C1).

This isn't a coding task, so here's the analysis directly.

## Reframe the question first

"Should we move closer to ageing parents" is really a bundle of separate decisions being evaluated as one. That's the first source of bad reasoning: people either let one dominant emotion (guilt, or fear of change) decide everything, or they get paralyzed comparing incommensurable things (a school's quality vs. a parent's loneliness). Split it into the actual separate questions:

1. What does eldercare *actually* require, and does physical proximity solve it?
2. What are the real (not catastrophized, not minimized) costs to each family member?
3. Is this decision reversible, and on what timeline does it need to be made?

## 1. Interrogate the premise: does proximity solve the actual problem?

Before comparing job/school costs, get concrete about *why* proximity is being considered:

- Is a parent currently in decline, or is this anticipatory ("someday they'll need help")? These call for very different responses. Anticipatory moves are making a large, hard-to-reverse bet on a future that's uncertain in timing and severity.
- What specific need would proximity address — routine social contact, emergency response, hands-on daily care, decision-making support? Each has different solutions, and moving is the most expensive way to solve most of them.
- Compare against alternatives that don't require uprooting two careers and two kids: paid in-home care, other relatives sharing the load, technology (medical alert systems, cameras, check-in calls), the parents moving instead (they have less tying them in place than a working family with school-age kids), or a "closer but not that close" compromise (90 minutes instead of 6 hours, enabling weekend visits without full relocation).

If the honest answer is "we don't actually know what they'll need or when," that's information — it argues for a reversible, low-commitment step now (more frequent visits, setting up support infrastructure) rather than the maximal move.

## 2. Get real about costs — per person, not averaged

The household tends to average out costs ("it'll be fine overall"), which erases the fact that costs land unevenly on individuals who don't get an equal vote.

- **Each adult's job**: Is this a lateral move, a step back, or career-ending in that market? Whose career absorbs more of the hit — and is that split by genuine circumstance or by default/gender pattern? An unexamined default here is a decision, not a neutral fact.
- **Each child**: Age matters enormously. A 4-year-old and a 15-year-old experience "change schools" completely differently — the teenager has an existing social identity and possibly exams/college trajectory at stake; the younger child mostly doesn't. Don't treat "the children" as a unit.
- **The parents being moved toward**: Do they actually want this? Increased proximity can mean increased obligation and reduced autonomy for them too — this is sometimes more about the adult child's guilt/anxiety than the parents' actual wishes. Ask them directly and take the answer seriously, including if it's "we're fine, don't uproot your family."
- **Financial**: cost of living delta, cost of two job searches, cost of selling/buying a home, lost equity in current jobs (seniority, vesting, network).

## 3. Reversibility and timing

This is the crux of "first principles" here: the size of the commitment should match the certainty of the need.

- A cross-country move with two new jobs and new schools is close to irreversible in practice — even if you could technically move back, you can't uneat the disrupted years.
- Eldercare need is not static — it escalates. A decision optimized for parents' *current* state (still independent) may be wrong within 2-3 years, and a decision optimized for a hypothetical crisis may waste years of the kids' stability for a crisis that doesn't materialize on that timeline.
- So: what's the actual trigger point? If parents are healthy now, is there a defined signal (a diagnosis, a fall, a specific functional decline) that would prompt re-evaluation — and can the family commit to *that* rather than deciding once now and being stuck?

## What should actually drive it

Not "who guilts loudest" or "what's the average inconvenience" but:

1. **A specific, honestly-assessed care need**, not a generalized "they're getting older" anxiety.
2. **Confirmation from the parents themselves** that proximity is what they want and would use — not assumed.
3. **A real comparison against non-relocation alternatives**, sized to the actual need.
4. **Individual-level cost accounting** for each family member, especially the teenager (if any) and whichever spouse's career is more exposed — with those costs actually named out loud, not smoothed over.
5. **A bias toward the reversible option** when uncertainty is high — proximity can often be increased in steps (more visits → part-time remote work near parents → full move) rather than jumped to in one irreversible leap.

The failure mode to watch for: making a maximal, permanent decision to resolve a diffuse emotional state (guilt, fear) when the actual problem is specific, current, and solvable with something much smaller.

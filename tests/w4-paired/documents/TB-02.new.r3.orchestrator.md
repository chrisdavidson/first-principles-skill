# Will Faster CI Runners Fix This? A First-Principles Analysis

## Bottom line

**Buy faster runners as leadership's sole, unmeasured fix: no.** The 47-minute duration has a hard mathematical ceiling on what hardware alone can do to it (Amdahl's Law + critical-path scheduling), and that ceiling is currently *unknown* because nobody has measured how serial the pipeline actually is.

**Do something else instead: not exclusively either.** The batching behavior likely has several distinct causes — raw duration, queue-wait, flaky-test retries, feedback-loop/flow cost, possibly social/blame dynamics — of which hardware reliably fixes at most one and a half. A weighted trade-off robustly favors doing **both** (targeted capacity + test-architecture/process fixes) over either alone.

**But sequence it correctly.** Run a days-long diagnostic first — per-stage CI timing, queue-wait split, flaky-test rate, a direct developer survey on *why* they batch — before committing spend or engineering time. It costs almost nothing next to the cost of guessing wrong.

## Key insight

Leadership's proposal conflates two separate questions that math answers differently:
1. **Will it shrink the 47 minutes?** Amdahl's Law + the critical-path lower bound say hardware has a hard ceiling set by the pipeline's serial fraction — currently unmeasured. An illustrative (unverified) bracket puts the floor around 14–26 minutes, not lower, no matter how much compute you throw at it.
2. **Will it stop the batching?** "To avoid triggering CI as often" plausibly bundles four+ distinct drivers (raw duration, queue-wait, flaky retries, flow-state cost) — hardware only cleanly addresses queue-wait and part of raw duration.

"Buy more compute" is a correctly-targeted fix for exactly one of at least three structurally distinct mechanisms that can inflate CI time (queueing, test-architecture serialization, flaky-test retries) — and the scenario gives no evidence which one dominates here.

## Second-order cost of continued batching

Larger diffs → weaker code review (research-supported pattern, not yet confirmed for this org) → higher change-failure rate → organizations typically respond by adding process (release trains, freezes) rather than reversing batch size → the longer this runs uncorrected, the more expensive it becomes to reverse later.

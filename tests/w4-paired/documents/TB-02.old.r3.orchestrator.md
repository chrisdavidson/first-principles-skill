No — not as a first move. Here's the reasoning, boiled down from the full analysis:

**The core problem with "buy faster runners": nobody has measured what's actually taking 47 minutes.** CI wall-clock time is a mix of compute-bound work (which faster hardware helps) and non-compute-bound work — queue-wait, checkout/dependency setup, network I/O, sequential gating (which faster hardware does *nothing* for). By Amdahl's Law, if even 20–45% of the 47 minutes is non-compute-bound (a fairly typical range for CI pipelines), hardware speedups have a hard floor: a realistic 2–3× runner upgrade plausibly lands you at **18–40 minutes, not near-zero**. Nobody in this scenario has broken down the 47 minutes by stage, so the purchase is being sized against an unknown variable.

**Batching isn't pure dysfunction — it's a rational response to a bad gate.** Developers are avoiding a costly, all-or-nothing 47-minute tax by bundling changes. That's a sane reaction to the incentive structure, not a compliance problem to fix with a memo. It also means: even a technically successful hardware fix might not reverse the behavior, especially if the post-upgrade time (per the bracket above) still exceeds the ~10–15 minute range where people stop context-switching away, or if the habit has simply calcified.

**Second-order risk:** hardware is easy to approve (capex) while re-architecting CI competes with the roadmap (engineer-time). If leadership buys hardware, sees underwhelming results because the bottleneck wasn't compute, and then treats "we already tried something" as license to deprioritize the real fix — you get a recurring cost spiral instead of a resolution, while the underlying test-suite growth or queue-wait problem keeps compounding.

**What actually ranked highest** across a weighted trade-off comparison (hardware-only vs. architecture-only vs. instrument-then-target): **diagnose first, then target.**
1. Instrument per-stage timing (most CI platforms expose this cheaply) to find the real split between compute, queue-wait, and fixed overhead.
2. Apply whichever of caching, test parallelization/sharding, test-impact selection, or a fast-required/deferred-full merge-queue gate the diagnosis says is load-bearing — these attack distinct cost components hardware can't touch.
3. Reserve hardware spend for whatever compute-bound residual is left after that.

**When hardware-only would in fact be the right call:** if diagnosis shows the non-compute-bound fraction is small (roughly under 10-15%), the test architecture is already reasonably parallelized/cached, and queue-wait is negligible. In that specific case, buying faster runners is close to sufficient and is the fast, low-risk move. That's a testable condition, not a guess — which is exactly why the recommendation is to check it before spending, not instead of ever spending.

**Confidence:** Medium overall (capped by a few unverified-but-flagged industry-typical assumptions about the compute/non-compute split and org approval dynamics) — high confidence on the two load-bearing structural points: that hardware has a mathematical ceiling here, and that measuring before buying dominates guessing before buying.

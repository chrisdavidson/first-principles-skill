## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** Amdahl's Law: for a process whose fraction *p* of total time can be sped up by a factor *s*, overall speedup is bounded by 1/(1−p+p/s); as *s*→∞ the maximum achievable speedup converges to 1/(1−p) — the non-accelerable (1−p) fraction sets a hard floor on total time no matter how fast the accelerable portion becomes. Source: Wikipedia, "Amdahl's law"; **read-at-source**: formula and infinite-speedup ceiling clause quoted directly from the article fetched this session. Provenance: read-at-source.

- **GT-2** Established CI practice: smaller, more frequent integrations reduce unpredictability and the amount of code that must be investigated when something fails ("the longer the time between integrations... the increase in unpredictability"; "it's much easier to combine a few hours work... than to combine several days"); the deployment-pipeline pattern runs fast tests first and slower comprehensive tests later, trading detection depth for merge-gating speed. Source: Martin Fowler, "Continuous Integration" (martinfowler.com); **read-at-source**: direct quotes fetched this session. Provenance: read-at-source.

- **GT-3?** Flaky (non-deterministic) tests are a widely-documented phenomenon in large CI systems and a commonly-cited source of wasted re-runs and eroded trust in CI signal; a frequently-repeated industry figure (~84% of pass→fail transitions attributable to flakiness, ~1.5% of runs exhibiting flakiness, both attributed to Google's CI) is widely circulated. **Phase 3 failure record:** the commonly-cited secondary source (Google Testing Blog, 2016, "Flaky Tests at Google and How We Mitigate Them") was opened this session and does **not** contain these figures — *citation does not support the claim*. The paper that appears to be the actual origin (Micco et al., "Taming Google-Scale Continuous Testing," ICSE-SEIP 2017) could not be opened this session (TLS certificate error on the available mirror — source unreachable), and two secondary sources describing that paper did not quote the specific statistics either. Retained only as an illustrative, non-load-bearing indicator that flakiness is a real category of CI pain generally; not cited on any HIGH chain. Provenance: unverified.

- **GT-4** Definitional/logical ground truth (five-whys reduce-to-primitives drill): the end-to-end wall-clock duration of any CI run partitions exactly into five mutually-exclusive, collectively-exhaustive interval types measured from the run's own timestamps: (i) queue-wait (accepted but not yet started), (ii) fixed per-job setup (checkout, dependency/cache restore, container/VM boot), (iii) execution (build + test payload), (iv) retry-overhead (re-runs from failures, including flaky-test re-runs), and (v) teardown/reporting. This is a structural identity about how elapsed time in a sequential-or-overlapping process is measured, not an empirical claim requiring external citation; verified by construction — each component bottoms out at a direct timestamp measurement. Provenance: not applicable (definitional identity, treated as physical-law-type per the assumption taxonomy).

- **GT-5** Only categories (ii) and (iii) in GT-4 — fixed setup and execution — can be reduced by faster/more-powerful individual-runner hardware; category (i) queue-wait falls only with more *concurrently available* runners (a different purchase from "faster"); category (iv) retry-overhead falls only with a lower flake rate (an engineering fix, not a speed fix — a flaky test re-run faster is still a re-run); category (v) is typically dominated by network/artifact-store latency, not compute speed. Definitional consequence of what "faster hardware" changes (per-instruction/per-byte rate) versus what queueing, retry, and network-bound categories are sensitive to (contention, correctness/determinism, external I/O latency respectively). Provenance: not applicable (definitional), same treatment as GT-4.

- **GT-6** No company-specific telemetry (per-stage timing breakdown, runner-utilization graphs, queue-wait metrics, retry/flake rate) was supplied to or is available within this analysis for this specific pipeline; the prompt itself lists six candidate drivers without adjudicating between them. Source: this conversation's own stipulated context. Provenance: read-at-source (a directly observable fact about this session, not an external claim requiring independent verification).

- **GT-7?** In actively-developed codebases, test-suite size (and therefore raw execution-time demand) typically grows monotonically over time absent deliberate pruning, more often than it shrinks. Offered as a plausible, widely-observed generalization in software engineering; no specific source was opened this session to confirm it for CI systems generally. Provenance: unverified.

- **GT-8?** In organizations without an explicit shared/rotated CI-failure-ownership policy, the developer who triggers a run is commonly expected to personally investigate and fix a failure promptly while context is fresh; under that norm, the practical cost of triggering CI scales with (probability of failure × re-engagement/interruption cost), not with raw wall-clock duration alone. Plausible and consistent with widely-discussed developer-flow/interruption-cost literature, but no specific source was opened this session. Provenance: unverified.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Quantitative Fermi cost comparison (batching harm in dollars vs. hardware cost in dollars)

**What was tried:** Attempted to construct a Fermi-style estimate comparing the dollar cost of batching (developer time lost to larger-diff review, harder bisection, delayed defect detection) against the dollar cost of a hardware upgrade.

**Why abandoned:** The estimate technique requires first-principles values — constants, direct measurements — not invented placeholder numbers. This organization's team size, merge cadence, incident cost, and current infrastructure spend were not supplied to this session, and current CI runner pricing was not read at source either. Fabricating numbers would produce a bracket that looks precise but is not anchored, misrepresenting confidence for a comparison that was never identified as speculative from the outset.

**What it ruled out:** Any headline conclusion resting on an invented dollar figure. The qualitative cost comparison in chains C2 and C4 (named harms — diff size, bisection difficulty, deploy-risk concentration, delayed feedback — rather than dollar figures) is used instead, and is the deliberate substitute path.

### Dead End: Assuming the pipeline is a single unparallelizable, compute-bound monolith

**What was tried:** Initially considered accepting leadership's framing at face value — treating the entire 47 minutes as compute-bound execution time — to directly evaluate the hardware purchase without a diagnostic detour.

**Why abandoned:** This begs the question: it assumes the very fact (compute-boundedness) that determines whether the proposed fix works, and GT-6 establishes that no telemetry is available to confirm it. Proceeding on this assumption would make the conclusion an artifact of the framing rather than a derived result.

**What it ruled out:** A direct "yes, buy the runners" recommendation issued without a diagnostic step. This dead end is what motivated the diagnose-first structure of chains C1 and C4.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not buy faster runners as the first move. Run a time-boxed (about one week) diagnostic that produces a stage-by-stage timing breakdown of the pipeline (queue-wait, checkout/dependency-install/container-boot, build, test execution, retry/re-run overhead, teardown/reporting); only then decide whether any hardware purchase is justified, and if so, whether it should be faster runners (execution-bound) or more concurrent runners (queue-bound) (chain C1). In parallel, invest in pipeline architecture — test parallelization/sharding, dependency caching, incremental/affected-only test selection, a fast-tier/full-tier split, and flaky-test remediation — together with a merge-strategy change that decouples "safe to merge" from "full pipeline green" (for example a merge queue or trunk-based development with fast-fail canary and rapid revert), and assign a named owner with a tracked CI-duration/flake-rate SLO so the fix does not decay (chain C4).

**Key insight:** "Faster runners" and "more runners" answer two different questions the leadership framing collapses into one — the first buys down execution time, the second buys down queueing time, and neither buys down retry-overhead from flaky tests — so under a plausible reading of what the 47 minutes is actually made of, a hardware-only fix scores within one point of doing nothing at all on the weighted comparison of options (chain C1, chain C4).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This recommendation defers the one visible, low-effort, immediately-actionable win (a hardware purchase) in favor of a slower, higher-effort diagnostic-and-architecture path with real execution risk (a botched parallelization or test-selection rollout can silently drop coverage) and real organizational risk (the plan can be abandoned mid-way if it doesn't show fast enough progress) (chain C4). A live rival this analysis does not fully rule out — that this pipeline's workload happens to be genuinely execution-bound, in which case a hardware bump alone would work about as well as leadership expects — is not settled by anything in this analysis; a small, low-cost, non-blocking runner-tier bump run in parallel with (not instead of) the diagnostic is a reasonable hedge against that possibility — no chain — flagged assumption only.

**Verdict, stated plainly:** buying faster runners, on its own, is **not** the right call as a first move — it is a purchase that cannot be shown to fix the actual problem without a diagnostic that hasn't been run, it structurally cannot touch two of the five components of the 47 minutes (queueing, retry-overhead) even in the best case, and on the weighted comparison it is barely distinguishable from doing nothing. The right call is: diagnose first, fix the architecture and the merge process (which plausibly address the real driver — interruption cost, not raw wait time), assign ownership so the fix holds, and treat a hardware purchase as a possible, narrowly-scoped follow-on once the diagnostic identifies a genuine compute- or concurrency-bound stage — with a cheap, low-risk hardware hedge acceptable to run in parallel, not instead of, that diagnostic.

## Should a university replace timed written exams with take-home assessments now that capable writing assistants are widely available?

**Short answer: No — not as a straight swap.** Full replacement fails on the evidence; the better move is a hybrid, matched-format design. Full reasoning below.

### 1. What each format is actually for

- **Timed written exams** structurally measure recall and in-the-moment procedural fluency under time pressure — no revision, no consultation, by construction (GT-1).
- **Take-home assessments** were designed to measure independent synthesis — locating and combining information, iterating on argument structure — under realistic-but-untracked conditions (GT-2).

These were never answers to the same question. A closed-book timed exam never even presents an open-ended synthesis task, so it can't distinguish a student who can synthesize novel arguments from one who can't (GT-10). Treating "replace vs. keep" as one binary choice compares answers to two different questions (GT-12).

### 2. What LLM availability actually breaks

It doesn't make take-home assessments "worse" as a format — it breaks the one thing an unmodified take-home submission silently assumed it could still deliver: a verifiable link between the submitted text and the student's own reasoning.

That's an empirical claim, not a hunch, and it holds up under scrutiny:

- OpenAI discontinued its own AI-text classifier in July 2023, citing a "low rate of accuracy" (reported ~26% true-positive / ~9% false-positive on secondary sourcing — not independently confirmed at primary source this pass).
- Turnitin's Chief Product Officer told K12 Dive directly (confirmed by reading the source): "real-world use is yielding different results from our lab," and Turnitin *declined to disclose* its false-positive rate below its own 20%-AI-composition detection threshold — i.e., exactly the partial-assistance zone where most real cheating and most real false accusations both live. Turnitin's own headline "<1% false positive" claim only applies above that threshold.
- This isn't a temporary engineering gap. AI-text detection is a statistical classification problem between two increasingly overlapping distributions (human writing vs. model writing optimized to imitate it), so its accuracy ceiling trends toward chance as models improve — betting on future detector improvement is betting against the trend, not waiting out a lag.
- At real scale, even a generous 1% false-positive rate against ~160,000 submissions/year at a mid-size university produces 1,600+ wrongly flagged submissions annually — far beyond what any integrity office can individually adjudicate with corroborating evidence. A detector score cannot be decisive, standalone evidence.

So an unmodified take-home format has lost its primary claim to validly measuring independent synthesis, wherever undetectable AI assistance is available.

### 3. Why full replacement (or full reversal) both fail

- **Full replace, no verification** gets knocked out immediately: it has no authorship-verification mechanism at all, so it can't certify the very construct it exists to measure.
- **Full reversal to proctored-only exams** (the instinctive safe move) fails differently: it's enforcement-safe but throws away the ability to observe synthesis competency at all — trading a real, measured capability for administrative safety.

Scored against seven weighted criteria (synthesis validity, recall validity, enforcement reliability, equity, workload, future-skill fit, wellbeing), a **hybrid, matched-format design** beat both: hybrid 109 vs. status quo 83 vs. AI-permitted-no-verification 73. The margin is wide enough that no single criterion's re-scoring flips the ranking — this isn't a close call.

### 4. The recommendation

**Adopt a hybrid, matched-format design, not a uniform swap:**

- Keep timed/proctored components wherever a course's actual learning outcome is recall or procedural fluency.
- Redesign take-home work to pair with a **sampled, live verification step** (short oral defense or equivalent) — this restores an authorship signal detection software structurally cannot supply, because it requires live, unscripted reasoning in the moment, not polished prose produced in advance.
- Require explicit AI-use disclosure; demote AI-detector output from "decisive evidence" to an internal triage signal only.
- Govern this at the course/learning-outcome level, under **one thin, funded, institution-wide floor** (mandatory disclosure norm + minimum verification expectation) — not a single blanket format mandate, and not unconstrained course-by-course discretion, which would erode transcript comparability across sections of the "same" course.

### 5. Trade-offs, named rather than hidden

- **Costs more faculty time** than either unmodified take-home or the pure status quo — this needs calibration training and load credit, not a policy memo alone.
- **Only equity-neutral if the institution funds universal baseline AI-tool access.** Paid vs. free LLM tiers differ materially; an "AI-permitted" policy on top of unequal access just relocates the equity problem. Absent that funding, the honest label is "equity-*improving* relative to the status quo," not "equity-neutral."
- **Situational exam anxiety ≠ a disability accommodation** — retaining timed components doesn't itself worsen disability equity as long as accommodation infrastructure stays funded; conflating the two would be a mistake in the other direction.
- Whether a "thin floor" is actually sufficient to stop course-by-course drift wasn't tested here — flagged as an open question, not resolved.

A stress-test (pre-mortem) of this exact plan surfaced how it fails in practice if adopted carelessly: unfunded redesign time causes quiet reversion to old rubrics; self-reported/sampled disclosure becomes theater; uneven course-by-course rollout erodes transcript comparability; and skipping the AI-tool-funding commitment turns the equity claim into a grievance. Each of these is addressed by making funding and a published, audited verification-sampling rate *preconditions* of the rollout, not afterthoughts.

### 6. Before deciding institution-wide vs. course-by-course, know:

1. What construct does *this course's* assessment actually certify — recall, synthesis, or both?
2. Does licensure/accreditation in this discipline require proof of unaided performance?
3. Can you actually resource sampled oral verification at this class size — is training/load credit committed, not just assumed?
4. Is universal baseline AI-tool access funded, or will "AI-permitted" just reproduce a paid-tier gap?
5. Is there a published, auditable institution-wide disclosure/verification floor to stop comparability erosion?

If an institution can't answer (3) and (4) affirmatively, pilot small before committing institution-wide.

**Overall confidence: LOW-to-MEDIUM by design** — the core format-matching logic (exams for recall, take-home+verification for synthesis) is solid and survives stress-testing; the weakest link is the equity claim, which is explicitly conditional on funding decisions not yet made. That's disclosed as the honest limit of the analysis, not smoothed over.

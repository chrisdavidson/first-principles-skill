The analysis is done. Here's the verdict:

**No — buying faster runners is not the right call as a first move.**

**Why the framing is flawed:** "47 minutes" is being treated as one thing, but it's actually five potentially-separate components — queue-wait, setup (checkout/deps/container boot), build, test execution, and retry/re-run overhead from flaky tests. Faster runners only address the execution/build portion. More runners (concurrency) would address queueing. Neither touches flaky-test retry overhead. Nobody has the stage-by-stage breakdown, so leadership is prescribing a fix without knowing which stage is actually the bottleneck.

**Two structural problems with the hardware-only fix:**
1. On a weighted comparison of options (do nothing / faster runners / merge-strategy change / pipeline architecture / architecture+process combined), "faster runners alone" scored 53 vs. "do nothing" at 52 — statistically indistinguishable — while the combined architecture+process fix scored 87, dominating on every criterion.
2. It's a recurring cost disguised as a one-time purchase: per Amdahl's law, the ceiling a hardware upgrade buys is fixed at purchase time, but test suites grow, so the same upgrade covers a shrinking share of total pipeline time as the codebase grows.

**The deeper issue:** batching behavior is more plausibly driven by *interruption/re-engagement cost* (fear of context-switching to debug a failure) than by raw wait-time — a faster pipeline that's equally likely to fail unpredictably may not change developer behavior at all.

**Recommendation:** Run a ~1-week diagnostic to get the stage-by-stage timing breakdown first. In parallel, invest in test parallelization/sharding, caching, incremental/affected-only test selection, a fast-tier/full-tier split, and flaky-test remediation, plus a merge-strategy change (e.g., merge queue) that decouples "safe to merge" from "full pipeline green" — with a named owner and a tracked SLO so it doesn't decay. A small, cheap runner-tier bump run *alongside* the diagnostic (not instead of it) is a reasonable hedge in case the workload turns out to be genuinely compute-bound.

Confidence on the overall verdict: MEDIUM — the biggest gap is that no one has actually measured where the 47 minutes goes; that's precisely why "diagnose first" is the top of the recommendation rather than a footnote.

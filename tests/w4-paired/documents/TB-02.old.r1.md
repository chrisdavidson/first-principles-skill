# First-Principles Analysis: Should Leadership Buy Faster CI Runners?

**MODE: full-composer** (no single technique name or structural-analysis request decisively triggered; the prompt names several techniques obliquely — "second-order effects," "cost/effect size" — while asking for a holistic decomposition, so this runs the full 5-phase methodology with all eight companion techniques applied in Phase 4).

## 1. Problem Essence

**Essence Statement:** The core question is whether leadership's proposed remedy — faster CI runner hardware — is correctly targeted at the actual causal mechanism producing commit-batching behavior, or whether it treats a proxy (total pipeline wall-clock duration) while leaving the real driver(s) — queue wait, flakiness/retries, blocking-gate placement, or non-parallelizable pipeline structure — unaddressed, and whether it is the most cost-effective available intervention.

**Success criteria** (checkable against section 6 without further interpretation):
1. The Conclusion states explicitly whether pipeline duration is *verified*, or merely *assumed*, to be the causal driver of batching.
2. The Conclusion names which segments of CI wall-clock time faster hardware can and cannot reduce, with a stated mathematical basis for the limit.
3. The Conclusion compares the cost/effect-size of the runner purchase against at least three concrete alternatives.
4. The Conclusion states second-order consequences of both the batching behavior itself and the proposed fix.
5. The Conclusion delivers an explicit, non-hedged verdict (proceed / do not proceed / conditional) plus a concrete pre-spend checklist.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### 4a. Reasoning upward — the eight companion techniques applied

**Five-Whys (causal mode) — why do developers batch?**
Why1: to avoid triggering CI runs often → Why2: because each run costs ~47 min before feedback/merge → Why3: because the merge-blocking gate requires the *entire* pipeline green before merge is allowed → Why4: because the pipeline evolved as one undifferentiated blocking sequence rather than a fast-blocking/slow-async split → Why5 (root, actionable): the architecture has no blocking/non-blocking split — the root cause candidate is pipeline *structure*, not compute *speed*. This is a hypothesis, not a verified fact (A4 in the Assumptions Table); it directly informs chains C1 and C8.

**Fishbone (breadth-first cause categories, default six-category set):**

| Category | Candidate causes |
|---|---|
| People | developer risk-aversion/impatience; review load per batch |
| Process | merge-blocking policy requires the *full* 47-min run to go green; no tiered gating |
| Technology and Tools | runner hardware; test-parallelization framework; caching setup; artifact-registry latency |
| Environment | shared runner pool contention with other teams; provider-side queue depth |
| Information | no dashboard showing where the 47 minutes actually goes (opacity → "47 min = the pain," an unexamined proxy) |
| Resources | fixed runner-pool size/budget causing queue contention independent of per-runner speed |

Every branch here maps to an already-classified assumption (A1, A2, A4, A9, A12) — the fishbone did not surface anything requiring a new table row, it organized what Phase 2 already caught.

**Inversion** — already conducted in Phase 2 (what would guarantee the fix fails: queue wait dominates; the critical path is serial; the pain point is time-to-first-signal, not total duration; flakiness forces reruns; non-CPU-bound waits dominate). Not repeated here per the Inversion/Second-Order pairing note — Inversion looks backward at preconditions, Second-Order (below) looks forward at consequences.

**Pre-mortem** — "Six months from now, leadership bought faster runners and batching is unchanged or worse. What caused that?" Independent list: (1) queue wait dominated and was untouched; (2) flaky tests still forced reruns; (3) the blocking-gate structure didn't change, so time-to-first-signal is unchanged; (4) the suite grew during rollout and ate the gain; (5) batching had already become habitual and didn't reverse on its own; (6) a new non-CPU bottleneck (registry throughput, package-manager latency) became binding once compute wasn't; (7) nobody noticed the improvement because the *felt* wait didn't change. Adversarial check — "would I have suppressed this in a group?" — flags (5) as the one most likely to be quietly dropped in a leadership meeting, because it implies the fix isn't purely technical. Clustering: {1,3,6}→wrong segment targeted (feeds C2/C4); {2,7}→non-hardware-addressable causes persist (feeds C1/C6); {5}→organizational/behavioral persistence (feeds C7, and surfaces new assumption A16).

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

**Dead end 1 — single-cause narrative (flakiness as primary driver).**
*What was tried:* building the whole cost/effect analysis around a retry-cost model, treating flaky tests as the dominant root cause of batching.
*Why abandoned:* the problem statement gives no evidence that flakiness specifically — versus queue wait or raw execution time — is dominant; asserting it as primary would be reasoning from a plausible narrative rather than a given or verified fact, biasing the verdict toward one remedy (flake-fixing) over the others GT-6 identifies.
*What it ruled out:* a single-cause frame for the entire analysis; the candidate is preserved inside GT-6's four-way decomposition rather than promoted to the leading hypothesis.

**Dead end 2 — anchoring cost estimates to a named vendor's real pricing.**
*What was tried:* pricing the Fermi/cost-effect comparison (C3, C5) against a specific cloud CI vendor's published runner-tier SKUs to produce a dollar-denominated verdict.
*Why abandoned:* the prompt names no vendor, scale, or run volume; anchoring to one vendor's numbers would manufacture false precision and make the verdict look more quantitatively authoritative than the evidence supports.
*What it ruled out:* a single "$X saved per year" headline; the analysis instead reports the qualitative cost-structure argument (recurring, usage-scaling premium vs. one-time engineering cost) and flags GT-9?/GT-12? as needing the org's own numbers.

**Dead end 3 — a flat, unconditional "no."**
*What was tried:* an unconditional verdict against buying faster runners.
*Why abandoned:* chains C2/C3's own Amdahl-Law bracket shows hardware can still remove several real minutes off the accelerable segment — a genuine, nonzero benefit — so a flat "no" would overstate what the chains actually support and would contradict the analysis's own theoretical-limit finding.
*What it ruled out:* outright rejection of the purchase; the analysis instead reaches a conditional verdict (measure first; treat hardware as secondary and bounded) consistent with, not contradicting, C2/C3.

No analogy to "how other companies solved CI speed" is used anywhere above as direct evidence; every comparative claim is grounded in a named GT about mechanism (Amdahl's Law, queueing theory, EOQ), not in "industry practice."

## 6. Conclusion

**Verdict:** Buying faster CI runners, as currently framed, is not the right first call. It is not shown to be wrong in every respect, but it targets an unverified driver of the batching behavior and has a hard mathematical ceiling on what it can achieve regardless of spend. (chain C1) (chain C2)

**Recommended approach:** Before committing spend, run a two-to-four-week measurement pass that breaks the 47 minutes down by segment (queue wait, checkout/dependency-install, execution, teardown/upload), measures where the first merge-blocking signal actually lands relative to total duration, and tracks the retry/flake rate — then re-evaluate the runner purchase against whatever that data shows, because queue wait, raw execution time, and flakiness each imply a different, non-substitutable remedy. (chain C8)

**Key insight:** The proposal conflates "the pipeline takes 47 minutes" with "the 47 minutes is what's driving batching." Amdahl's Law caps how much of that duration hardware can ever remove — the ceiling is set by whichever segment (queue wait, I/O, serial handoffs) is non-accelerable by faster compute, and that segment's size is currently unmeasured, not assumed away. (chain C2) (chain C3)

### Bottom line for the pre-spend checklist

1. **Instrument before you buy** — break the 47 minutes into queue wait / setup / execution / teardown, and find where the first merge-blocking signal actually lands (GT-8, chain C8).
2. **Check runner-pool utilization** — if it's near saturation, the fix is *more* runners, not *faster* ones (chain C4).
3. **Measure flake/retry rate** — a retry multiplies effective duration and faster hardware barely touches it (chain C1).
4. **Price the alternatives with real numbers** — caching, test parallelization, and a blocking/non-blocking split are one-time costs competing against a recurring hardware premium; get your own vendor quote and engineering estimate rather than trusting the illustrative bracket here (chains C3, C5, GT-9?/GT-12?).
5. **Decide explicitly whether to reverse batching, not just tolerate it** — its second-order costs (blast radius, bisection difficulty) don't go away just because the pipeline gets faster (chain C6).
6. **If you still buy runners, budget for the fact that the benefit decays** as the suite grows, and don't let the purchase become the reason the real architectural fix never gets funded (chain C7).

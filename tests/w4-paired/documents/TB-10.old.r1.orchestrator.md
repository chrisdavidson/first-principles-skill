## What's really being decided

The visible framing — "should we move, given the jobs and schools cost" — hides the actual question: **what variable should govern this, if not proximity itself?**

## The core finding

**Proximity is a threshold effect, not a dial.** The danger window that matters (roughly the "golden hour" after a fall or acute event) is crossed decisively by going from hours-away to minutes-away — but once you're inside that window, further closeness buys almost nothing. And a relocated adult with a new full-time job isn't necessarily *available* during the hours that matter just because they're *nearby*. Relocation trades a residency problem for an availability problem — it doesn't automatically solve the thing it's meant to solve.

Meanwhile, the caregiving horizon itself is genuinely unknowable in advance: average duration is ~4 years, but a quarter of cases run past 5 years and 15% past 10 — while a meaningful share end much sooner (stabilization, facility care, etc.). That spread is wide enough that an irreversible bet (both jobs gone, kids re-enrolled, house bought) can easily fail to "pay for itself" before the underlying need changes character.

**So the variable that should actually drive this is: verified, reversible, explicit alignment — not distance.** Concretely, that means:

1. **Verify, don't assume, the parents' actual trajectory and wishes.** Whether they want this level of involvement, and what their real medical/functional trend is, is a fact to establish directly — not something to infer from love or guilt.
2. **Preserve reversibility wherever real job-portability allows it.** Requiring *both* adults to resign stacks a second, largely independent risk (career/financial) directly on top of an already-uncertain caregiving transition — precisely the wrong time to also bet the household's income.
3. **Make the caregiving-role commitment explicit and bounded**, including with siblings, so it doesn't quietly expand into unbounded role-captivity for one person.
4. **Treat this as staged, not final.** A rented home near the parents with one portable job, or strengthened local paid care plus a monitoring device, satisfies the response-time threshold about as well as full relocation — while staying reversible if the need resolves faster or slower than expected, or if the parents' wishes turn out different from what was assumed.

## What people typically over-weight vs. what's load-bearing

Salary, school rankings, and raw distance in miles dominate these conversations because they're *legible* — easy to compare, easy to defend to relatives. But the things that actually determine whether the move succeeds or becomes a multi-year regret are less visible: whether the parents actually consented to this, whether the family kept an exit ramp, and whether the caregiving role has a boundary. A pre-mortem run two years out shows every dominant failure mode — job collapse, marital overload, parents who didn't want it, no way to reverse course — tracing back to skipping one of those three checks in favor of a single decisive "let's just move" commitment.

## Honest caveat

Confidence here is medium, not high — the fall-response and duration statistics are solid (read at source), but the dual-career/relocation-failure-rate figures and the claim that aging trajectories are generally non-reversing once declining are less rigorously sourced in this pass. They don't change the structural conclusion, but they're the numbers I'd want firmed up before leaning hard on the specific magnitudes.

## 1. Problem Essence

**Essence Statement:** Is procuring faster CI runners the correct lever for reducing the integration risk created by developers batching merges to avoid a 47-minute pipeline — or is it a spend aimed at a proxy metric (wall-clock minutes) that leaves the pipeline's actual bottleneck and gating architecture, and therefore the batching behavior itself, unaddressed?

**Success criteria a correct answer must meet:**
- States what is actually known vs. unknown about what composes the 47 minutes, rather than assuming it is uniformly compute-bound.
- Distinguishes leadership's stated fix (fewer wall-clock minutes) from leadership's actual outcome goal (frequent, low-risk integration).
- Tests whether runner *speed* specifically (vs. runner *count*/concurrency, architecture, or reliability) is the binding constraint.
- Produces a decision rule usable before spending money, not just a verdict.
- Names the evidence that would falsify the recommendation in either direction.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** (stipulated by user): current pipeline wall-clock duration is 47 minutes.
- **GT-2** (stipulated by user): developers have begun batching merges specifically to reduce how often they trigger the pipeline.
- **GT-3** (stipulated by user): leadership's proposed intervention is procuring faster runners.
  *(GT-1–3 are user-reported operational facts about their own system; this analysis has no independent instrument to read them from, so they are accepted as given per the Input Contract rather than marked with the citation-style `?`.)*
- **GT-4** (Critical Path Method — definition/mathematical fact, no external citation required): in a DAG of dependent stages, total wall-clock time equals the longest dependency chain, not the sum of all stage durations, regardless of parallel runner count beyond what the critical path needs.
- **GT-5** (Amdahl's Law, 1967 — mathematical law): for parallelizable fraction P and serial fraction (1−P), speedup from accelerating the parallel part by factor S is bounded by 1/((1−P) + P/S); as S→∞, speedup caps at 1/(1−P) — a ceiling set entirely by the serial fraction.
- **GT-6** (CI/systems-architecture definition): faster hardware reduces wall-clock cost roughly in proportion only for CPU-bound, non-network-bound work; it does not reduce network I/O waits, fixed per-job orchestration overhead, queueing delay, or flaky-retry time.
- **GT-7?** (reported from general domain knowledge, NOT read-at-source this session — Forsgren/Humble/Kim, "Accelerate"; DORA State of DevOps): smaller, more frequent integration batches correlate with lower change-failure rate and faster recovery than large infrequent batches. Confidence caveat applies to anything resting solely on this.
- **GT-8?** (reported from general domain knowledge, NOT read-at-source this session — commonly attributed to Martin Fowler's "Continuous Integration" essay): a CI feedback loop kept to roughly ten minutes is a widely cited guideline for not disincentivizing frequent integration. Confidence caveat applies.
- **GT-9** (definition): batching merges, by definition, increases the number of independent changes integrated together per CI run, increasing diff size and candidate-cause count on failure.
- **GT-10** (definition): a "buy faster runners" intervention acts only on per-job execution speed; it does not itself change pipeline architecture, test-suite composition, or trigger/gating policy.

`?`-marked: GT-7, GT-8 (2 of 10). No HIGH-confidence chain in this analysis rests on GT-7?/GT-8? alone — they support MEDIUM-confidence steps only (see chains C3, C5, C6); the structural/mathematical argument runs on GT-4–GT-6/GT-9/GT-10.

## 4. Derivation Chains

**C1 — theoretical-limit / Amdahl bound on what hardware can achieve:**
```text
GT-4 (critical path sets wall-clock) + GT-5 (Amdahl's ceiling)
→ the 47-minute figure decomposes into a serial critical-path component and a parallelizable compute-bound component
→ only the parallelizable compute-bound component can shrink from faster hardware
→ the serial fraction is not known from the given premises, because no per-stage timing breakdown was supplied [Assumes: A1, A6]
→ applying the theoretical-limit technique, as hardware speed S approaches infinity the wall-clock time approaches the serial critical-path floor alone
→ if that floor were, for illustration, 30 of the 47 minutes, no runner purchase at any price could push the pipeline below roughly 30 minutes
→ therefore the achievable improvement from faster runners has an unmeasured ceiling that must be measured before the purchase is sized
```
Confidence: HIGH on the bound itself (GT-4/GT-5), MEDIUM on the practical import (depends on unmeasured P).

**C2 — fishbone breadth map of candidate causes:**
```text
GT-6 (hardware speeds only CPU-bound non-serial work) + GT-4 (critical path bound) + GT-10 (definition of runners-only lever)
→ a 6M fishbone sweep names six candidate cause-categories for the 47 minutes: Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, Man, and Mother Nature
→ "faster runners" is a fix aimed exclusively at the Machine category
→ Method, Material, Measurement, and Mother Nature are structurally untouched by a hardware upgrade under GT-6
→ Man is untouched by definition under GT-10
→ a Machine-only fix therefore addresses at most one of six plausible cause-categories
→ whether that one category is even the dominant contributor is unknown absent the timing data named in chain C1 [Assumes: A1]
```
Confidence: HIGH structurally, MEDIUM on dominance.

**C3 — five-whys causal drill on batching behavior:**
```text
GT-2 (devs batch to avoid triggering CI as often) + GT-9 (batching increases integration-unit size)
→ developers avoid triggering CI as often because each trigger imposes a cost on them
→ that per-trigger cost could be wall-clock wait, flaky-failure risk, or both, and the given premises do not distinguish which dominates [Assumes: A3]
→ the organization has not separated a fast pre-merge check from a slower deferred check, so every trigger costs the full pipeline regardless of which answer the developer needs right now [Assumes: A8]
→ that lack of separation is a pipeline-architecture decision, not a hardware constraint
→ the actionable, testable root cause is a single all-or-nothing gate rather than a tiered fast/slow gate, testable by splitting the gate and observing whether merge frequency recovers
```
Confidence: MEDIUM (rests on A3/A8, both unverified; testability is what recovers confidence — see falsifiers below).

**C4 — trade-off analysis across candidate interventions:**
```text
GT-6 (hardware scope limits) + GT-10 (definition of runners-only lever) + GT-9 (batching mechanics)
→ four candidate interventions are scored against five weighted criteria: addresses-root-cause, cost, time-to-impact, engineering effort, and second-order-regression risk [Assumes: A10]
→ buying faster runners scores well on time-to-impact and effort because it is a procurement action requiring no engineering work
→ buying faster runners scores low on addresses-root-cause because GT-6 and GT-10 show it cannot reach queueing, network I/O, pipeline architecture, or developer behavior
→ re-architecting the pipeline into a fast pre-merge gate plus a deferred slow suite scores highest on addresses-root-cause because it shortens the pre-merge critical path directly, independent of hardware, per GT-4
→ the weighted ordering places the architecture fix above the hardware fix whenever addresses-root-cause is weighted at parity with or above time-to-impact, and only inverts if the organization's revealed preference is a fast visible action over a durable fix
```
Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH on the ordering logic; the specific weights are illustrative (A10).

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

- **Considered:** recommend a pure policy mandate ("merge at least daily") without touching pipeline architecture. **Abandoned:** under GT-4/GT-9, forcing more frequent triggers against an unchanged, still-slow monolithic gate just makes developers eat the 47-minute cost more often, worsening context-switch cost rather than removing the disincentive.
- **Considered:** recommend "buy more (not faster) runners" as sufficient on its own. **Abandoned:** per GT-4, added concurrency only helps when the bottleneck is contention *across* simultaneous runs, not the length of any single run's critical path — this is a different diagnosis than "faster," and adopting it without data would repeat the same premature-spend error in a different currency.
- **Considered:** treating GT-7? (DORA batch-size research) as the sole justification for prioritizing architecture over hardware. **Abandoned as sole support:** it is unverified this session (`?`) and is an industry-wide correlation, not this org's own data; used only as corroborating context — the load-bearing premises are GT-4/GT-5/GT-6, which need no external citation.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not approve the runner-speed purchase as the first or sole action. Instrument the pipeline — per-stage timing, queue-wait time, flaky-retry time — before spending (chains C1, C2). Then act on what the data shows: the highest-leverage fix is very likely splitting the pipeline into a fast pre-merge gate plus a deferred slower suite (chains C3, C4), which shortens the merge-blocking critical path independent of hardware and directly targets the behavior driving batching. Treat faster and/or additional runners as a valid *supplementary* purchase only for whatever fraction of the critical path the data confirms is CPU-bound and non-serial (chain C1) — not as the fix itself.

**Key insight:** Leadership has correctly identified a real cost (47 minutes) but has substituted a proxy metric (wall-clock duration) for the outcome it actually wants (frequent, low-risk integration), and has picked the one lever guaranteed to be within unilateral budget authority regardless of whether it is within causal reach of the problem (chains C6, C2).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** the instrument-first path costs a few days of engineering time and delays any visible relief on the 47-minute number, versus a hardware purchase that can ship immediately (chain C4); if organizational pressure demands near-term visible action, running both in parallel — a modest, cheap, reversible runner upgrade now, alongside instrumentation for the real fix — dominates pure waiting (chains C4, C5).

**What would falsify this recommendation:**
- Job-level timing data showing >80% of the 47 minutes is CPU-bound compute in jobs already running at maximum available parallelism with no queue-wait — this would falsify "hardware is insufficient" and support leadership's original plan directly (falsifies C1/C2's premature-spend concern).
- After experimentally shortening the pipeline (by any means) to roughly the ~10-minute range GT-8? cites, developers resume merging at the prior frequency — this would falsify A3's uncertainty and confirm duration itself was the dominant driver (strengthens, not weakens, part of the case for hardware, though it would still leave open whether "faster" or "more" runners is the correct spend).
- Splitting the gate into fast/slow tiers (no hardware change) restores merge frequency while total pipeline time is largely unchanged — this would falsify "raw wall-clock duration is what matters" and confirm C3's gate-architecture root cause.
- Runner-utilization data showing jobs spend significant time queued rather than executing — this would falsify "runners need to be faster" and instead support "runners need to be more numerous," a different purchase than leadership proposed.
- Developer interviews naming flaky-test failures or CI unreliability, not duration, as the reason for batching — this would falsify the duration-centric fix regardless of hardware spend.

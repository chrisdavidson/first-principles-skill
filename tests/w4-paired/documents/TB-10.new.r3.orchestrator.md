Here's the analysis, distilled to what matters (the full working — assumption tables, confidence ratings, pre-mortem — is available if you want the rigor made explicit, but this is the readable version):

## The real question isn't "should we move"

It's: **which specific piece of eldercare support is actually distance-dependent, how much is it worth given how that risk is shaped, and is relocation the cheapest, most reversible way to buy it?** Framed as a binary ("support them" vs. "don't"), the decision hides better options.

## The key insight: proximity's value is concave, not linear

Physical distance only strictly matters for one thing: emergency response time. And in most cases, professional responders (EMS) reach a parent before any family member could, regardless of how close you live — family arrival changes *presence during recovery*, not the acute outcome. So the value curve looks like this: **most of the risk reduction comes from being "reasonably reachable"; very little further reduction comes from closing the last distance to full co-location.** "Closer is always better, proportionally" is the wrong shape.

Everything else people bundle into "supporting aging parents" — financial help, logistics, emotional contact, help with paperwork — is *not* distance-bound. It can be delivered via money, other relatives, technology, or scheduled visits without anyone changing jobs or schools.

## What should actually drive it — in order

1. **Ask the parents directly what they want.** Don't infer it. Whether they actually want increased proximity (vs. valuing their independence) is the single biggest unknown here, and no amount of analysis substitutes for that conversation.
2. **Get a real read on their care trajectory** — is this anticipatory, or has something already materialized (a diagnosis, a fall, a clear decline)? A genuinely open trajectory argues differently than an already-resolved one.
3. **Price a staged/targeted alternative before defaulting to full relocation** — a remote-first job transition, a higher visit cadence, paid local care, or a smaller living-arrangement change (e.g., one parent relocating rather than the whole family, or an added dwelling unit). These often capture most of the proximity benefit at a fraction of the switching cost of two new jobs and two new schools.
4. **If you go staged, set a hard reassessment date and have the sibling/role conversation up front** — not later. Staged plans quietly become permanent (kids form attachments, "temporary" drifts) unless you pre-commit to revisiting it.
5. **Commit to full relocation specifically when the trajectory has already resolved to acute, or the parents have clearly said they want proximity** — not by default, and not because it "feels like the caring thing to do."

## What should *not* drive it

Guilt, comparison to what a sibling already did, and the unexamined "closer is always better" heuristic. These are legitimate feelings, but they're not evidence about which option actually maximizes the parents' or the family's welfare — guilt's intensity and its correlation with actual impact are two different variables.

## The honest caveat

The whole framework hinges on two facts only the family can determine, not any outside analysis: whether the parents' need has already become acute, and whether the parents have actually stated a preference. Everything above is a decision *process* conditioned on resolving those two things first — it's deliberately not a flat yes/no.

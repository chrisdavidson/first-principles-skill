## Bottom line: No — not yet, and not as posed

Buying faster runners treats the *symptom* (47 minutes) without confirming the *cause*. The analysis breaks into two separate causal chains that leadership's framing conflates:

**Why developers batch** is a rational amortization response: each pipeline trigger imposes a ~47-minute fixed cost, so batching N changes cuts the cost-per-change to ~47/N. That means only a fix that meaningfully shrinks the *fixed-cost character* of the pipeline will change the behavior — a marginal duration cut won't cross the threshold that's driving the workaround.

**Why the pipeline takes 47 minutes** is currently unknown. There's no data (per-step timing, CPU/network/disk utilization, flaky-retry rate, queue-wait time) establishing that compute speed is the bottleneck rather than: serialized/unparallelized steps, oversized test suites, cold caches/I/O, test flakiness requiring reruns, or runner-pool queueing/contention. Faster CPUs only help the first of these — and even there, an Amdahl's-Law ceiling means the non-parallelizable portion of the pipeline can't be sped past regardless of hardware.

**Second-order risk of buying blind:** if the real bottleneck isn't compute, leadership burns money and credibility on a highly visible "fix" that doesn't change batching behavior, and the resulting sunk cost makes it organizationally harder to reopen the real investigation later.

**Trade-off comparison** (buy-hardware-only vs. root-cause fix vs. do-nothing vs. diagnose-first-then-decide) shows diagnose-first dominates by a wide, robust margin — it's cheaper and faster than a hardware purchase while having a much higher probability of actually fixing the behavior.

**Recommendation:** Run a short, time-boxed (days, not weeks) diagnostic pass — instrument the pipeline, identify which factor actually dominates the 47 minutes — then apply whichever fix the data indicates. Only buy hardware if the diagnosis specifically confirms compute-bound dominance with limited parallelization headroom left; if it's queueing/contention, buy *more concurrent runners*, not *faster* ones — a different purchase than what's on the table.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** — the recommendation to diagnose-before-buying is solid and doesn't depend on any of the illustrative numbers used along the way; what's genuinely uncertain is which specific fix the diagnosis will point to, since that requires this pipeline's actual telemetry, which nobody has yet.

**Falsification condition:** if a diagnosis-driven fix cuts the fixed per-trigger cost by more than half and developers keep batching anyway, the amortization theory is wrong and something else (review policy, team norms) is driving the behavior — worth watching for if you run this experiment.

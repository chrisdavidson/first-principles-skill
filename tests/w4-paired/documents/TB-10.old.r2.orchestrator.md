## What should actually drive the decision

The real lever here is caregiving **capacity**, not geography. Proximity only solves the slice of the problem governed by travel time and emergency-response latency — it doesn't automatically create more caregiving hours, and it isn't the only way to deliver care. Once you decompose "help aging parents" into its separate parts — hands-on care tasks, emergency-response latency, emotional/relational closeness, and a family-duty commitment — only the first two actually require physical proximity. The other two can be partly served by frequent visits and explicit commitments regardless of where you live.

**Five concrete drivers, in order:**

1. **Get an honest assessment of the parents' actual care needs** — not proximity in the abstract. What specific tasks, and how urgent is response time really?
2. **Bracket relocation's total cost (job-search risk, income gap, moving, kids' disruption) against the cost of buying equivalent care locally** (in-home/paid care). Take whichever is cheaper for the same outcome.
3. **Recognize the hard ceiling**: two new full-time jobs consume roughly the same hours as the old ones. Moving converts commute/travel time into potentially usable time — it doesn't manufacture new hours.
4. **Turn the family's specific failure modes into named go/no-go checks before committing**: Can both partners actually get comparable work? Is the financial cushion sufficient? Is the career sacrifice symmetric and explicitly agreed, not assumed? Are the kids at a developmentally sensitive point?
5. **Score the full option set on weighted criteria** — not just "move" vs. "don't move." Live alternatives: full relocation, staying with more paid care plus frequent visits, one partner going remote/relocating alone, the parents relocating instead, or a staged/reversible trial. Because irreversible choices carry an extra cost under uncertainty, a nearly-as-good but reversible option should beat an equally-good irreversible one.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** This is the honest ceiling, not a hedge — most of the reasoning is solid, but it depends on facts the question didn't supply. The answer would sharpen a lot with:
- the children's ages
- the parents' actual health trajectory (stable vs. declining vs. acute)
- financial runway for the transition
- real job-market portability for both partners' careers
- whether siblings/other relatives could share the caregiving load
- whether either job could go remote instead

**One honest trade-off:** doing this analysis properly costs time, and if a parent's situation is more urgent than currently known, that delay has a real cost the framework above can't quantify. That's a judgment call the couple has to make explicitly, not one that first-principles reasoning can resolve for them.

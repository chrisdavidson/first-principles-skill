## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Producing a single fixed recommendation (e.g., "yes, move" or "no, don't")

**What was tried:** An early framing considered scoring the options and simply naming a winner as the deliverable.

**Why abandoned:** GT-8 establishes that no family-specific facts (trajectory stage, financial runway, career portability, children's ages, marriage capacity, parents' own preference) were supplied; a fixed answer cannot be correct across the full range those unknowns could take, and the user explicitly requested a framework rather than a prescriptive answer (Essence success criterion 1).

**What it ruled out:** A confident-sounding single answer that would be right for some families and quietly wrong for others without any way for a reader to tell which.

### Dead End: Treating the ~5-mile hands-on-care threshold as a hard, load-bearing numeric cutoff

**What was tried:** Using GT-6?'s specific mileage figure as a firm planning threshold in the framework itself (e.g., "move to within 5 miles, no more, no less").

**Why abandoned:** The cited source (ScienceDirect) returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch and no second source confirmed the specific mileage; the figure is real evidence but reported-by-delegate and unverified at source.

**What it ruled out:** Over-precision — a decision framework that anchors on an unverified number gives false confidence; the framework instead uses GT-2's confirmed ~1-hour threshold as the load-bearing discontinuity and carries the 5-mile figure only as a flagged, non-load-bearing data point.

### Dead End: Asserting "stacking concurrent stressors causes divorce" as a ground truth

**What was tried:** Treating the general stress-accumulation intuition behind sequencing advice as an established empirical finding suitable for a Ground Truth entry.

**Why abandoned:** Direct verification (GT-9, PMC5644348) found that the specific compounding effect of concurrent external stressors on marital tension/divorce is an acknowledged gap in the literature this analysis checked, not a confirmed finding.

**What it ruled out:** Overstating the sequencing recommendation's evidentiary basis; the claim was downgraded to a flagged assumption (A-18), and chain C6 is rated MEDIUM rather than HIGH as a direct result.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach — a five-step decision framework rather than a single yes/no answer:**

1. Decompose the bundled "move" into its four separable sub-decisions — residence, each adult's job, the children's schooling, and the caregiving arrangement itself — and do not treat them as one atomic choice (chain C1).
2. Before executing any low-reversibility sub-decision, name the specific problem being solved: verify which of the four trajectory patterns describes the parents' actual decline, or that it is not yet determinable, and distinguish a real unmet ADL/safety/emergency-response deficit from an adult child's own anxiety or guilt about distance (chain C7).
3. Rank the live options — full relocation, partial relocation with at least one portable job, or staying in place with reinforced local/paid care and technology — against reversibility, financial risk, the children's developmental cost, and the relocating adult's own autonomy, not distance alone; under a broadly reasonable weighting, staying and reinforcing local care dominates relocating, unless the verified need exceeds what reinforced local care and remote coordination can deliver (chain C3).
4. Quantify what "closer" actually buys before paying for it: the realistic ceiling on additional hands-on care hours a relocated caregiver can supply is bounded by that caregiver's own remaining discretionary time, not by distance once inside roughly an hour's travel time, so most of the benefit of moving "even closer" past that threshold is response-time, not care-hours (chains C2, C4).
5. Never execute all four sub-decisions in the same window without an explicit financial buffer and a named point of coordination with the parents and other relatives, and set a fast, clinically-informed trigger — checked within the first 30 days — that can override the whole staged process if the parents' trajectory turns out to offer no gradual warning (chains C5, C6; pre-mortem Clusters A and E).

**Key insight:** Moving does not create caregiving hours; it only relocates the same scarce time budget closer to where it is needed, so most of what "closer" buys, once inside roughly an hour's travel time, is response speed rather than more hands-on care — and much of what actually drives whether this decision feels survivable is not distance at all but whether the arrangement is chosen rather than imposed, and whether the family has correctly named which problem — a verified care deficit, or an unverified anxiety about one — it is actually trying to solve (chains C2, C7).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Favoring reinforcement-in-place over relocation trades away some of the response-time and hands-on-hours ceiling that only proximity can provide (chain C2) — a real cost if the verified need turns out to be severe. Favoring a staged, trigger-based process over an immediate decision trades away speed in a genuinely acute, no-warning trajectory (chain C6; pre-mortem Cluster A), which is why the framework's first gating step is a fast clinical check, not a slow one.

# Should a University Replace Timed Written Exams with Take-Home Assessments Now That AI Writing Assistants Are Widely Available?

*A first-principles analysis. The six sections below (Problem Essence → Conclusion) are the deliverable. A block of process notes — the assumption/self-audit scans and gate scoring the methodology requires — follows afterward, clearly separated, for anyone who wants to see the reasoning's own quality-control trail.*

---

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given that generative AI can now produce fluent, competent written work with minimal effort, what combination of supervision, task design, and format allows a university to keep individually-attributed, high-stakes measurement of student competence trustworthy — and does simply swapping timed written exams for take-home assessments achieve that, or is the swap itself a category error?

**Success criteria:**
- **SC1:** The Conclusion states explicitly whether a blanket replacement of timed exams with take-home assessments is recommended, rejected, or conditionally recommended.
- **SC2:** The Conclusion names at least one assessment format beyond the two named in the question.
- **SC3:** The Conclusion's recommendation traces to at least one derivation chain grounded in evidence about actual current AI capability (generation or detection), not general impression.
- **SC4:** The Conclusion names at least one second-order institutional-incentive consequence of a naive "just replace" policy, beyond "students might cheat."
- **SC5:** The Conclusion is conditioned on what construct a given course is trying to certify, rather than issuing one undifferentiated rule for all of higher education.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** OpenAI launched an "AI Text Classifier" in January 2023 to flag AI-generated text, then discontinued it about six months later, in July 2023, citing "a low rate of accuracy"; contemporaneous independent side-by-side testing found it detected AI-generated text markedly less reliably than a competing tool (5-of-7 vs. 1-of-7 correct on a small test set) — source: TechCrunch, *"OpenAI scuttles AI-written text detector over 'low rate of accuracy'"* (25 Jul 2023); read-at-source: article body, opened this session.
- **GT-2** A study testing seven GPT detectors (Liang et al., Stanford, 2023) found they falsely flagged TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated at a mean false-positive rate of about 61%, with roughly 20% of essays unanimously misflagged by every detector tested, against a near-zero false-positive rate on essays by native-English-speaking writers — source cited: The Markup, *"AI Detection Tools Falsely Accuse International Students of Cheating"* (14 Aug 2023), reporting the Liang et al. findings; read-at-source: The Markup's article body, opened this session (the underlying academic PDF was fetched but its text could not be extracted, so this figure is confirmed at the secondary-source level, not re-derived from the primary paper's own tables).
- **GT-3** A 2023 survey of 1,000 U.S. undergraduate/graduate students (Pure Spectrum for BestColleges, 29 Sep–5 Oct 2023) found 56% report having used AI on assignments or exams, and 53% report having coursework that specifically required AI use — source: BestColleges, *"56% of College Students Have Used AI on Assignments or Exams"*; read-at-source: article body, opened this session.
- **GT-4** A systematic review and meta-analysis of structured oral-exam formats in health-professions education found substantially higher inter-rater reliability for structured vivas (Cronbach's α ≈ 0.75–0.80 across cited studies) than for traditional/unstructured vivas (α ≈ 0.50, as low as 0.3–0.4 in some cited studies), and noted that some student groups, including English-as-additional-language students, may be disadvantaged by oral formats absent careful design — source: BMC Medical Education systematic review, *"Structured viva validity, reliability, and acceptability as an assessment tool in health professions education"* (PMC10369684); read-at-source: article body, opened this session.
- **GT-5?** Current general-purpose LLMs can produce fluent, organized, subject-plausible prose responsive to a typical undergraduate essay prompt within seconds to minutes, without the submitter necessarily having mastered the underlying material — unverified: stated from general knowledge of current model capability, not independently benchmarked against a controlled rubric-scoring study this session.
- **GT-6** A live, unscripted, proctored interactive assessment (e.g., an oral defense with real-time follow-up questioning) structurally requires the person being assessed to be physically present and responding in real time; covert AI substitution in that interaction requires either a detectable live relay or a distinct impersonation act — a different problem (identity fraud), not a format-design problem — source: definitional consequence of what "live, unscripted, proctored" means; not an empirical claim requiring external citation.
- **GT-7?** Assessment in higher education serves at least three distinct, non-fully-reducible functions: certifying competence to external stakeholders (summative), providing feedback that improves future learning (formative), and shaping study behavior through anticipated evaluation (the "washback" effect) — unverified: standard educational-assessment theory (constructive-alignment/washback literature); no primary source opened this session.
- **GT-8?** Unsupervised/take-home and supervised/timed formats carry distinct, non-overlapping equity risks — take-home can advantage students with more unstructured time or better tool access; timed/proctored can disadvantage students with disabilities, test anxiety, or caregiving/work obligations absent accommodation — unverified: consistent with general equity-in-assessment literature; no specific study opened this session.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: The question's own framing is a false dichotomy — supervision, not duration or location, is what determines AI-vulnerability

GT-6 (live proctored interaction requires real-time physical presence)
→ AI-resistance is a property of supervision and task design, not of how long the assessment takes or where it happens
→ "timed" and "take-home" are two points on a broader design space defined by supervision × task-type × authentication, not opposite poles of one axis [Assumes: A13 — typical unsupervised windows are long enough that duration alone doesn't meaningfully limit AI consultation]
→ the question's binary framing is a false dichotomy, and "should X replace Y" is underspecified until supervision and authentication are specified

→ combined with already-high and rising usage on exactly this task type, an unsupervised take-home essay with no authentication mechanism cannot currently be trusted alone to certify that the submitting student did the cognitive work the grade represents

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Duration itself (timed vs. untimed), independent of supervision, is the AI-protective variable
**What was tried:** Considered whether a strict time limit alone — even on an unsupervised, remote exam — meaningfully restricts opportunity to consult an AI assistant.
**Why abandoned:** A typical 60–180 minute unsupervised window is far longer than the seconds-to-minutes a capable LLM needs to draft competitive answers, and GT-3's high usage figures include usage under ordinary assignment deadlines, not only leisurely ones; duration only matters once supervision is present to prevent consultation entirely.
**What it ruled out:** Proposals that would keep the take-home format but simply shorten the window as a fix for AI exposure — this does not address the actual mechanism and is the rival ruled out to license C1's HIGH rating.

### Dead End: Rely on AI-detection software as the authentication mechanism for take-home work
**Why abandoned:** Directly contradicted by GT-1 (OpenAI discontinued its own classifier for low accuracy) and GT-2 (detectors are specifically biased against non-native English writers) — using such tools as a compliance gate would compound an equity harm while not reliably solving the underlying problem.
**What it ruled out:** Any policy design that leans on commercial AI-detection scores as the primary or sole authentication step.

### Dead End: A single universal policy is simpler and therefore preferable to a differentiated portfolio
**Why abandoned:** Contradicted by C4 — assessment serves at least three non-reducible purposes, so a uniform format necessarily under-serves at least one of them; "simpler" trades directly against validity with no way to avoid the trade, so simplicity alone does not make it preferable without naming which function is being sacrificed.
**What it ruled out:** Administratively convenient one-size-fits-all mandates as a serious contender in the Phase 4 trade-off.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not adopt a blanket, university-wide replacement of timed written exams with unsupervised take-home essays. Adopt a differentiated assessment portfolio instead, chosen per course according to the construct being certified: take-home work paired with a structured, funded oral defense or in-class process checkpoint for high-stakes certification of extended writing/reasoning; explicitly AI-permitted authentic tasks with mandatory disclosure and critique for courses whose real target construct is AI-augmented judgment; supervised timed assessment retained specifically where unaided procedural or recall fluency is the certified construct; and plain unsupervised take-home essays with no authentication mechanism treated as currently unsuitable for high-stakes individual certification, though still usable for low-stakes formative practice (chain C6).

**Key insight:** "Should take-home replace timed exams" hides a false dichotomy and a category error. The variable that actually determines AI-vulnerability is supervision and authentication design, not duration or location (chain C1) — and "assessment" is not one thing with one purpose, but at least three purposes whose AI-vulnerability profiles differ enough that no single format serves all of them well (chain C4). Reasoning by convention would have stopped at "make exams take-home or don't"; reasoning from these two facts is why that framing is the wrong question.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The recommended portfolio is more expensive and logistically harder than either pure extreme, and its added labor cost falls disproportionately on already-stretched instructors and under-resourced departments unless explicitly funded (chain C6) — the exact magnitude of that cost was not independently estimated in this analysis — no chain — flagged assumption only. It also carries residual, non-permanent AI-resistance, since no authentication method is immune to AI tools eventually adapting to it (chain C7). And it carries a real equity risk for anxiety-prone or English-as-additional-language students in oral-format components that must be actively designed for with accommodation pathways, not merely scored and accepted (chain C6).

**Sensitivity.** The single ground truth whose falsity would flip the recommendation: GT-1/GT-2 (AI-detection unreliability and bias). Neither is `?`-marked — both are read-at-source facts about the 2023-era detection landscape. If a future, independently replicated study showed AI-text detection achieving high accuracy (e.g., >98%) with no demographic bias, sustained under adversarial conditions, the eliminated "unsupervised take-home + reliable detection" option would re-enter the Phase 4 trade-off and could plausibly outscore the recommended portfolio on feasibility while matching it on AI-resistance — this recommendation is time-conditioned on the current unreliability of detection persisting. Weakest link per chain: C7 (the institutional-adaptation claims rest on plausible, unmeasured behavioral/economic reasoning, A15).

**Rival.** Headline conclusion: strongest rival is "keep pure supervised timed exams for everything, it's simpler and already the historical default" — ruled out by C4 (under-serves extended-reasoning and AI-judgment constructs) and by C6's own arithmetic (pure timed exam scores lowest, 54, among survivors). Second rival: "ban AI outright, enforce by honor code" — ruled out by Dead End DE2 (still relies on unreliable detection) and by C7 (the underlying incentive/signal-erosion mechanism persists regardless of a ban's existence). Intermediate chains: C1's rival ("duration is protective") ruled out at DE1. C2 carries a live, unsettled rival (see C2's own confidence line) — named, not dismissed.

**Premise.** It is three years after this portfolio policy was adopted, and it has already failed — assessment integrity is worse than before, or the policy collapsed under its own weight.

**Causes** (unfiltered, from instructor, student, administrator, and under-resourced-department viewpoints, written before grouping):
1. Oral defenses/checkpoints added grading load with no additional TA support; instructors burned out and reverted to trusting take-home essays at face value.
2. Rubrics for the new formats were never standardized, so "structured" oral defense degraded into unstructured viva, losing GT-4's reliability advantage.
3. Large intro courses (300+ students) found individual oral defense logistically impossible and defaulted to the cheapest non-compliant option.
4. Students perceived oral defenses as biased against anxiety/EAL students and grade-appeal volume overwhelmed the registrar.
5. Students rehearsed AI-generated talking points for the oral defense itself, partially eroding its assumed AI-resistance.
6. An equity gap opened between students who could afford coaching for an unfamiliar, high-anxiety oral format and those who couldn't.
7. Implementation cost (scheduling, rooms, extra faculty hours) exceeded budget; the policy was rolled back university-wide after one year.
8. Disability-accommodation compliance/legal risk arose from oral-format requirements lacking adequate alternative-format provisions.
9. A small department with 2 faculty and 400 majors literally could not orally examine every student every term; the policy assumed flagship-program resourcing.
10. Adjunct faculty, paid per course rather than per student-hour, had no economic incentive to adopt the more labor-intensive format and were structurally exempted, creating a two-tier system tied to employment status rather than pedagogical need.

**Clusters:**
- **Cluster A — Unfunded labor mandate** (causes 1, 3, 7, 9, 10; bears on C6's feasibility criterion and C5): feasibility was scored as a criterion, not designed as a resourcing precondition.
- **Cluster B — Rubric/reliability decay** (cause 2; bears on GT-4, C5): GT-4's reliability gain is conditional on maintained structure; nothing specifies who audits it over time.
- **Cluster C — New AI-resistance workaround** (cause 5; bears on C5, C7): treating oral defense as permanently AI-resistant ignores that AI tools adapt to help prepare for oral formats too — the same adversarial dynamic that defeats text detectors generalizes to any fixed authentication method.
- **Cluster D — Equity/accommodation risk** (causes 4, 6, 8; bears on C6's equity criterion, GT-4's own caveat, GT-8?): scoring equity risk in a trade-off is not the same as mitigating it.

**Disposition:**
- Cluster A — *plan change:* funded implementation (TA/grading support, scaled by enrollment) becomes an explicit precondition for adopting oral-defense-style formats; unfunded departments default to the process-logged take-home (score 65) or AI-permitted authentic task (score 66) instead.
- Cluster B — *plan change:* a standing rubric-audit/calibration requirement (periodic inter-rater reliability spot-checks) accompanies adoption of structured oral or hybrid formats.
- Cluster C — *accepted risk, named mitigation:* no authentication format is permanently AI-resistant; format choice is re-evaluated every 2–3 years rather than fixed once, and oral defenses are built around unscripted follow-up tied to the specific submitted artifact rather than generic content review.

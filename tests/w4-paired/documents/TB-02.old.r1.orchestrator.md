## Verdict: Not the right first call — at least not yet

**The core problem:** Leadership's fix conflates "the pipeline takes 47 minutes" with "the 47 minutes is what's driving batching." That's an unverified leap. The 47 minutes bundles at least four distinct segments — queue wait, setup (checkout/deps/image pull), execution, and teardown — each with a different cause and a different fix. Faster runners only touch the CPU-bound execution segment.

**Why hardware alone has a hard ceiling:** By Amdahl's Law, speedup from faster compute is capped by whatever fraction of the pipeline *isn't* accelerable by compute speed (queue wait, I/O, serial handoffs) — no amount of money removes that floor. If queueing is the real bottleneck (which grows sharply, non-linearly, as a shared runner pool approaches saturation), the correct purchase is *more* runners for concurrency, not *faster* ones — a different SKU entirely.

**Cost/effect comparison:** Weighing faster runners against six alternatives (more concurrent runners, test parallelization, caching, splitting blocking/non-blocking stages, fixing flakiness, trimming the critical path) on cost-efficiency, impact on time-to-first-blocking-signal, and durability against test-suite growth — faster runners ranked **last**, trailing the next-worst option by a wide enough margin to survive reasonable disagreement about the weights. It only wins on "fastest to implement."

**What batching itself is costing you, independent of pipeline speed:** bigger blast radius per merge, harder bisection when something breaks, delayed feedback. A faster pipeline doesn't automatically undo an already-formed habit, and there's a real organizational risk that buying hardware becomes "we solved CI" and kills appetite for the actual architectural fix — while the benefit itself decays as the test suite grows.

**Before spending anything, do this (2–4 weeks):**
1. Instrument the pipeline by segment — queue wait vs. setup vs. execution vs. teardown — and find where the first merge-blocking signal actually lands (that's closer to what developers feel than total duration).
2. Check runner-pool utilization/saturation.
3. Measure the flake/retry rate.
4. Get real vendor pricing and an engineering estimate for caching + test parallelization + blocking/non-blocking split, rather than assuming.
5. Decide explicitly whether you're reversing the batching habit, not just hoping speed fixes it.

Then re-evaluate the runner purchase against that data — it may still be worth buying eventually, just not first and not alone. (Confidence: MEDIUM overall — the diagnosis and ceiling argument are solid; the exact numeric brackets need your org's real measurements.)

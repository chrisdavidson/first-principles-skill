## The core reframe

**"Should we move?" is the wrong question — it bundles four separate decisions** (residence, each adult's job, kids' schooling, the caregiving arrangement itself) that don't have to move together. Job change without relocation is possible via remote work; relocation without a school change is possible via keeping enrollment; caregiving without either is possible via paid help, technology, and coordinated visits. Treating this as one atomic yes/no throws away that flexibility for free.

## What should actually drive it

**1. Name the real problem before picking a lever.** "The parents need more support" isn't one problem — it depends heavily on which decline pattern applies: sudden-death, terminal-illness, organ-failure (repeated crises), or slow frailty. Each has different urgency and a different correct response. Separately: research on caregiver strain found that *perceived choice* predicts strain better than *distance* — so if this is driven by an adult child's guilt or anxiety rather than a verified deficit, moving treats the wrong thing.

**2. Distance has a ceiling on what it buys.** Once you're within roughly an hour's travel time, moving *even closer* mostly buys faster response time, not more hands-on hours — those are capped by the caregiver's own discretionary time, not the address. Quantified: the realistic extra hands-on capacity a relocated, employed caregiver can add is ~10-20 hrs/week, which at typical paid-care rates ($13k-36k/yr) is often comparable to or cheaper than the transaction and career-disruption costs of actually moving (~$24k-40k in home-sale costs alone, plus a multi-year earnings-recovery risk for the relocating spouse).

**3. Score the real alternatives, not just "move vs. don't."** Compare full relocation, partial relocation (one portable job), and staying-with-reinforced-local-care across effectiveness, financial risk, reversibility, kids' developmental cost, and caregiver autonomy — not distance alone. Under reasonable weights, reinforcing care in place wins clearly unless the *verified* need exceeds what paid local care and coordinated family/technology can deliver.

**4. Never execute all four sub-decisions in the same window.** Doing so removes every fallback simultaneously — both incomes become uncertain at once, right as the kids absorb a school transition and the parents' trajectory keeps moving. Sequence by reversibility: commit to the reversible pieces (caregiving arrangement, maybe one job) first; hold residence and both-jobs-at-once as a later, separately-triggered step.

**5. Set a trigger-based review, not a one-time decision.** Since the trajectory isn't fully predictable up front, define an explicit escalation trigger (ideally checked against a clinical read within 30 days) rather than deciding once and hoping it stays calibrated.

**Caveats worth flagging:** the ~5-mile hands-on-care threshold cited in some research couldn't be verified at source (paywalled) — treated as suggestive, not load-bearing. And the "don't stack all four stressors" advice, while intuitive, rests on an assumption not confirmed by the literature reviewed (concurrent-stressor compounding is an acknowledged research gap, not an established finding) — still the safer bet, but flagged as such.

**Bottom line:** the decision should be driven by (a) which care-trajectory pattern actually applies, verified rather than assumed, (b) whether the need exceeds what cheaper, reversible alternatives (paid care, one portable job, tech-assisted coordination) can deliver, and (c) whether the family can absorb the least-reversible pieces without stacking every risk into the same window. Distance alone shouldn't be the lever people reach for first.

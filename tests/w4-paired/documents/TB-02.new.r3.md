## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given a 47-minute CI pipeline and a merge-batching behavior it has induced, will a hardware-only fix (faster/more runners) address whichever mechanism is actually driving both the duration and the batching, or does it address a proxy while leaving the true bottleneck(s) untouched — and what intervention is actually warranted?

**Success criteria** (each checkable against the Conclusion section without further clarification):
1. The conclusion states whether the 47 minutes is compute-bound or bound by serialization/dependency/test-architecture/queueing/flakiness, traced to a derivation chain.
2. The conclusion states the mathematically-bounded ceiling hardware alone can achieve on the 47 minutes, derived from an explicit theoretical-limit chain.
3. The conclusion names the candidate driver(s) of the batching behavior (duration, queue wait, flakiness/retries, feedback-loop/flow cost, or social/blame dynamics), traced to a chain, rather than assumed.
4. The conclusion states the second-order cost of continued batching (diff size, review quality, conflict rate, release risk), traced to a chain with both an actor-lens and a time-lens effect named.
5. The conclusion issues exactly one of {buy runners / don't / do something else / do both}, with an explicit confidence band and a stated condition under which it would flip.

## 3. Ground Truths

**Provenance note on GT-3/GT-4/GT-5:** these are mathematical identities. This analysis did not retrieve a PDF of the cited papers; it verified them by direct derivation, shown inline — the strongest available form of "read-at-source" for a self-verifying formal truth, since the derivation *is* the primary source (the same standing the five-whys reduce-to-primitives mode gives a fact that "bottoms out at a physical law, a definition, or a direct measurement"). They are therefore recorded unsuffixed.

- **GT-1?** The organization's CI pipeline currently takes 47 minutes of wall-clock time per run. — unverified: stipulated as the scenario's given premise; no company CI logs were available in this session to independently measure it.
- **GT-2?** Developers have begun batching multiple changes into fewer, larger merges. — unverified: stipulated as the scenario's given behavioral fact (the *reason* is deliberately treated as separate and unverified — see the assumptions table).
- **GT-3** Amdahl's Law: for a workload with parallelizable fraction *P* and serial fraction (1−*P*), speedup from *N* parallel units is `1/((1−P)+P/N)`; as *N→∞* this approaches a hard ceiling of `1/(1−P)`. — source: Amdahl, G.M., "Validity of the single processor approach to achieving large-scale computing capabilities," AFIPS 1967; read-at-source: re-derived directly from the definition of parallel/serial work division (algebra shown in C1/C2).
- **GT-4** Critical-path lower bound: for any task set with dependency constraints (a DAG), the minimum achievable makespan on *any* number of processors is bounded below by the length of the longest path through the DAG, because tasks on that path must execute in sequence. — source: Kelley & Walker (1959), critical path method; standard DAG-scheduling lower-bound result; read-at-source: re-derived directly from the definition of a dependency-ordered task sequence.
- **GT-5** Little's Law and multi-server queue behavior: `L = λW`; holding arrival rate and per-job service time fixed, adding servers (increasing *c*) reduces expected queue-wait time, while reducing per-job service time is a separate, independent lever. — source: Little, J.D.C. (1961), "A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW"; read-at-source: re-derived directly from the definition of arrival rate, wait time, and units in system.
- **GT-6?** CI test suites that have grown organically commonly contain hidden serial dependencies (shared fixtures, order-dependent state) that block full parallelization even where the underlying test cases are logically independent. — cited to: general CI/testing-architecture literature (e.g., Google Testing Blog on test-suite health); reported-by-delegate: recalled from general domain knowledge, no specific citation opened this session.
- **GT-7?** Flaky (non-deterministic) tests are a well-documented phenomenon in CI at scale and commonly drive automatic retries that add wall-clock time. — cited to: published industry data (e.g., Google's ~1.5% flaky-test-rate disclosures); reported-by-delegate: recalled from general domain knowledge, no specific citation opened this session.
- **GT-8?** Smaller, more frequent batches of change are associated with lower change-failure rate and faster MTTR; larger, less frequent batches are associated with the opposite. — cited to: Forsgren, Humble & Kim, *Accelerate* (2018) / DORA State of DevOps research program; reported-by-delegate: recalled from general knowledge of this research program, not opened this session.
- **GT-9?** Larger code diffs measurably reduce the thoroughness and defect-detection rate of human code review. — cited to: Cohen et al., "Best Kept Secrets of Peer Code Review" (SmartBear/Cisco study); reported-by-delegate: recalled from general knowledge, not opened this session.
- **GT-10** The scenario as given states no per-stage timing breakdown of the 47 minutes, no split of queue-wait vs. execution time, no flaky/retry rate, and no direct account (survey or otherwise) of developers' actual reasons for batching. — source: the user's own problem statement in this conversation; read-at-source: re-read in full — none of these four data points appears anywhere in it.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Concluding hardware is categorically useless for CI time.

**What was tried:** An initial instinct to answer leadership's proposal with a flat "no, hardware never helps."
**Why abandoned:** Contradicted by GT-5 — if queue-wait for a saturated shared runner pool is a real component (plausible, unmeasured), adding runner *count* is a correctly-targeted fix for exactly that mechanism (C3). A blanket dismissal of hardware is the same category of error as leadership's blanket endorsement of it, just pointed the opposite way.
**What it ruled out:** Saves the reader from over-correcting into "hardware never helps CI"; the correct claim is narrower and mechanism-specific (C1, C3), not zero.

### Dead End: Producing a single precise percentage speedup from hardware with no company data.

**What was tried:** An attempt to state a specific number (e.g., "hardware will cut CI by 23%").
**Why abandoned:** No per-stage timing data exists for this org (GT-10); a single point estimate would misrepresent an illustrative, unverified bracket as measured fact.
**What it ruled out:** Saves the reader from over-trusting a specific figure; the analysis instead reports a bracket (14–26 minutes, C2) explicitly flagged unverified, and recommends measurement (C7) rather than guessing harder.

### Dead End: Framing "buy hardware" vs. "fix the tests" as strictly either/or.

**What was tried:** An initial two-option trade-off (A vs. B only).
**Why abandoned:** The two interventions target different, non-overlapping mechanisms (queueing vs. architecture/flakiness per C3), so treating them as substitutes loses the option with the highest expected value.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not approve leadership's hardware-only proposal as stated. Run a short (days-long) diagnostic pass first — instrument per-stage CI timing, split queue-wait from execution time, measure the flaky/retry rate, and directly survey developers on why they batch, including the social/blame-dynamics driver the scoring in C4 explicitly excluded — then pursue the combined path: a modest, evidence-sized runner-capacity increase together with test-architecture and process fixes (chain C7 for diagnose-first; chain C6 for the combined recommendation).

**Key insight:** Leadership's question conflates two separate questions Amdahl's Law and queueing theory answer differently — "will it shrink the 47 minutes?" and "will it stop the batching?" A hardware purchase has a mathematically hard, mechanism-specific ceiling on the first question (chain C1) and plausibly addresses at most one and a half of at least four distinct candidate drivers of the second (chain C4); reasoning by convention ("CI is slow, buy more compute") misses that a pure capacity purchase is well-matched to exactly one of at least three structurally distinct duration-inflating mechanisms (chain C3).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The recommended path costs more up front in coordination and engineering time than simply approving a purchase order (chain C6's cost-efficiency and political-feasibility scores favor hardware-only), and it delays the visible "win" by weeks rather than days. The analysis's own quantitative brackets — the 14–26-minute Amdahl floor, the trade-off's weighted totals, the batching-cost projection — rest on illustrative or externally-sourced inputs not confirmed against this org's actual data (chains C2, C5, and C6 are all rated LOW for exactly this reason); this is inherent to reasoning about a scenario without measured data, and is precisely why the recommendation leads with instrumentation rather than a final numeric commitment (chain C7).

## 3. Ground Truths

**Provenance note on GT-3/GT-4/GT-5:** these are mathematical identities. This analysis did not retrieve a copy of the cited papers; it verified them by direct derivation, shown inline — the strongest available form of "read-at-source" for a self-verifying formal truth, since the derivation *is* the primary source.

- **GT-1?** The organization's CI pipeline currently takes 47 minutes of wall-clock time per run. — unverified: stipulated as the scenario's given premise; no company CI logs were available in this session to independently measure it.
- **GT-2?** Developers have begun batching multiple changes into fewer, larger merges. — unverified: stipulated as the scenario's given behavioral fact (the *reason* is deliberately treated as separate and unverified — see the assumptions table).
- **GT-3** Amdahl's Law: for a workload with parallelizable fraction *P* and serial fraction (1−*P*), speedup from *N* parallel units is `1/((1−P)+P/N)`; as *N→∞* this approaches a hard ceiling of `1/(1−P)`. — source: Amdahl, G.M., "Validity of the single processor approach to achieving large-scale computing capabilities," AFIPS 1967; read-at-source: re-derived directly from the definition of parallel/serial work division.
- **GT-4** Critical-path lower bound: for any task set with dependency constraints (a DAG), the minimum achievable makespan on *any* number of processors is bounded below by the length of the longest path through the DAG, because tasks on that path must execute in sequence. — source: Kelley & Walker (1959), critical path method; standard DAG-scheduling lower-bound result; read-at-source: re-derived directly from the definition of a dependency-ordered task sequence.
- **GT-5** Little's Law and multi-server queue behavior: `L = λW`; holding arrival rate and per-job service time fixed, adding servers (increasing *c*) reduces expected queue-wait time, while reducing per-job service time is a separate, independent lever. — source: Little, J.D.C. (1961), "A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW"; read-at-source: re-derived directly from the definitions of arrival rate, wait time, and units in system.
- **GT-6?** CI test suites that have grown organically commonly contain hidden serial dependencies (shared fixtures, order-dependent state) that block full parallelization even where the underlying test cases are logically independent. — cited to: general CI/testing-architecture literature (e.g., Google Testing Blog on test-suite health); reported-by-delegate: recalled from general domain knowledge, no specific citation opened this session.

## Bottom line

**Buy faster runners as leadership's sole, unmeasured fix: no.** The 47-minute duration has a hard mathematical ceiling on what hardware alone can do to it (Amdahl's Law + critical-path scheduling), and that ceiling is currently unknown because nobody has measured how serial the pipeline actually is. The batching behavior most likely has several distinct causes — raw duration, queue-wait, flaky-test retries, feedback-loop/flow cost, and possibly social/blame dynamics — of which hardware only reliably fixes one and a half.

**Do something else instead: not exclusively either.** A weighted trade-off across cost, durability, risk, and speed of effect robustly favors doing *both* — targeted capacity plus test-architecture/process fixes — over either alone, and that ranking doesn't flip under any single reasonable change in how much you weight the criteria.

**But do it in the right order.** Run a days-long diagnostic first (per-stage timing, queue-wait split, flaky-test rate, a direct developer survey on why they actually batch) before committing spend or engineering time to either path — it costs almost nothing next to the cost of guessing wrong, and it is what would upgrade this analysis's own LOW-confidence conclusion into a measured, HIGH-confidence decision.

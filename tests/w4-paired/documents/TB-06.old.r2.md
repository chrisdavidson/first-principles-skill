## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given that capable AI writing assistants can now produce competent unsupervised written work, what assessment design (or combination of designs) lets a university keep verifying that a specific, named student — not an AI system, not an uncredited collaborator — actually possesses the competence a given assessment is meant to certify or develop, without discarding the pedagogical value that authentic, take-home-style tasks offer over timed in-person exams?

**Success criteria:**
1. The conclusion states, separately for each assessment purpose decomposed below (certification, formative feedback, ranking/sorting, incentive-shaping of learning behavior), which modality or combination best serves it — checkable by scanning the Conclusion section for a purpose→modality mapping.
2. The conclusion gives an explicit answer to whether take-home should replace timed in-person exams, and names the specific condition (stakes, purpose, or discipline) under which that answer changes — checkable by scanning for a contingency clause.
3. The conclusion names the specific mechanism by which AI availability threatens take-home validity (not merely "cheating is easier") and pairs it with a named, actionable mitigation — checkable by matching a named threat to a named fix.
4. The conclusion names at least one second-order effect (equity, faculty workload, or credential trust) that choosing either pure modality in isolation would miss — checkable by scanning for a second-order-marked derivation step reflected in the Conclusion.

## 3. Ground Truths

**Five-Whys (reduce-to-primitives mode) applied to GT-7:** *Claim:* "A university credential certifies that the named individual possesses certain competence." → What is a credential made of? A record (transcript entry) plus an institutional attestation process linking record to individual. → Is the attestation process reducible? Yes: (a) evidence collection (the assessment artifact) and (b) an inference step (grader concludes competence from evidence). → Is the inference step reducible? Yes: it is valid only if the evidence is statistically linked to *that individual's* competence rather than to some other generative process. → Is that linkage itself reducible? It bottoms out at the mathematical definition of independence/mutual information between two random variables — a terminal, physical-law-type primitive requiring no further reduction and no external citation. **Verdict: Verified — definition, terminal branch.**

- **GT-1?** OpenAI's 2023 "AI Text Classifier" correctly flagged AI-written text roughly 26% of the time (true positives) while incorrectly flagging human-written text as AI-written roughly 9% of the time (false positives), and was discontinued on 2023-07-20 for "low rate of accuracy." — cited to: OpenAI's own classifier-retirement statement, as reported by TechCrunch (2023-07-25) and corroborated by Search Engine Journal/Inquirer Technology/SearchEngineLand; reported-by-delegate: this session's own WebFetch of the TechCrunch article confirmed the date and the "low rate of accuracy" quote directly, but not the two percentages, and WebFetch of `openai.com` returned **HTTP 403 Forbidden** (Phase 3 failure record: source unreachable). Restricted to MEDIUM/LOW-confidence chains per the reachability exception.
- **GT-2** Sadasivan et al. (arXiv:2303.11156, "Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?") state in their own abstract that they provide a theoretical framework connecting the AUROC of the best possible AI-text detector to the Total Variation distance between human and AI text distributions — i.e., detector performance has a mathematical ceiling set by how distinguishable the two distributions actually are, not by how well any given product is engineered. — source: arXiv:2303.11156 abstract; **read-at-source**: abstract paragraph, fetched directly 2026-09-27 (quoted verbatim above). The specific numeric threshold reported in secondary summaries (~11% distributional overlap) could not be confirmed at source this session — the full PDF exceeded this session's fetch size limit (Phase 3 failure record: source too large to open) — so only the qualitative ceiling claim is used in any chain, never the specific number.
- **GT-3?** Independent 2024-2026 evaluations of AI-text detectors found wide accuracy variance (61-69% overall accuracy for leading tools; near-chance on hybrid human/AI text), false-positive rates as high as 16.9% for weaker free tools versus roughly 1-2% for premium tools, and pronounced bias against non-native English writers (one cited study found 61.3% of TOEFL essays by non-native speakers misclassified as AI-generated, versus near-perfect classification of native-speaker student essays). — cited to: aggregated findings surfaced by WebSearch (International Journal for Educational Integrity/Springer; arXiv:2608.11256; detector-comparison summaries); reported-by-delegate — none of the underlying pages was opened directly this session.
- **GT-4?** At least one university (Middlesex University, UK) has, in its 2025-2026 Academic Integrity and Misconduct Policy, formally mandated oral vivas as the escalation step for suspected contract cheating or AI misuse, and oral/verbal defense is being piloted at other institutions as an AI-era authentication mechanism per academic-integrity trade coverage. — cited to: WebSearch synthesis referencing the Middlesex policy and Times Higher Education; reported-by-delegate — not opened directly.
- **GT-5?** A survey of 22,963 higher-education students across 155 countries found economically advantaged students significantly more likely to hold paid generative-AI subscriptions, use such tools more intensively, and report greater perceived educational benefit than less-advantaged peers. — cited to: SSRN working paper (Mahdavivand Fard, "Digital Divide or Digital Equaliser?") as surfaced by WebSearch; reported-by-delegate — not opened directly.
- **GT-6?** High-stakes professional licensure examinations (US bar exam, USMLE, NCLEX) are administered as proctored, identity-verified, timed exams specifically because the licensing body must certify the named individual's own competence to the public. — unverified: general professional-licensure knowledge, no source opened this session.
- **GT-7** A credential functions as an information-bearing signal about an individual's competence to a third party only if the credential is statistically correlated with that individual's underlying competence; formally, if credential C is generated independently of an individual's competence Θ (mutual information I(Θ;C) = 0 — e.g., an AI produced the artifact with no individual-specific constraint), observing C conveys zero information about Θ, however fluent the artifact reads. — source: the definition of statistical independence/mutual information in information theory; a formal/definitional derivation verified by the five-whys drill above, not an empirical citation.
- **GT-8** A proctored, single-sitting, in-person exam that restricts networked devices and verifies test-taker identity at entry physically blocks browser- or API-based access to external generative-AI systems during the exam window, absent smuggled hardware; it does not block AI use before the exam, nor a smuggled-hardware relay, though both carry materially higher cost/skill/detection risk than unsupervised take-home AI use. — source: direct logical entailment of "no networked device, verified identity, single sitting"; logistical/definitional, no external citation needed.
- **GT-9** Total oral-examiner time for a course scales linearly as (minutes per student) × (number of students), and the number of examiners running in parallel bounds how many students can be examined per unit of wall-clock time. — source: arithmetic/queueing identity; no external citation needed.
- **GT-10?** Retrieval practice under low-stakes, repeated conditions ("the testing effect") improves long-term retention relative to passive restudying, and the benefit depends on effortful retrieval occurring at all — not on whether that retrieval is open-book/take-home or closed-book/timed (Roediger & Karpicke's testing-effect line of research). — unverified: a well-replicated cognitive-science finding, no source opened this session.
- **GT-11?** A typical instructor can realistically allocate on the order of 2-4 hours per week of marginal time to an oral-assessment-verification task beyond existing duties, before it displaces other required work. — unverified: an illustrative planning bracket for the Fermi estimate in C3, not a measured figure from any specific institution.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: An unverified take-home artifact, alone, cannot satisfy the certification purpose of assessment under current AI availability

**Theoretical-limit technique applied to GT-2:** conventional figure = vendor-marketed detector accuracy claims (often 95%+); governing law = the AUROC/total-variation-distance bound GT-2 establishes; law-permitted ceiling = as an AI model's output distribution approaches the human-text distribution (which improves under adversarial paraphrasing specifically), detection AUROC for *any* detector — not just today's — falls toward chance (0.5); gap = part of the shortfall between marketed accuracy and real-world reliability is irreducible under this bound, while the demonstrated bias against non-native writers (GT-3?) is a separate, convention-driven defect that better-designed tooling could in principle reduce.

```text
GT-7 (credential validity requires I(Θ;artifact) > 0) + GT-2 (detector AUROC ceiling falls toward chance as distributional overlap grows) + GT-1? (OpenAI's own classifier ran near-coin-flip on true positives) + GT-3? (deployed detectors show large bias and accuracy variance)
→ when AI-generated prose can substitute for a student's own at near-zero detectable cost, the take-home artifact's statistical link to that specific student's competence collapses toward zero
→ no currently deployed detection layer restores that link, and GT-2's ceiling shows part of the gap is not an engineering shortfall a better product can close
→ an unverified take-home artifact therefore fails the individual-attribution condition a credential requires, so it cannot alone satisfy the certification purpose of assessment under current AI availability
```

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. GT-1? and GT-3? are unverified/reported-by-delegate inputs. Raising to HIGH would require directly reading OpenAI's original classifier-retirement statement (blocked this session, HTTP 403) and the full Sadasivan et al. PDF body for its exact numeric bound (exceeded this session's fetch size limit), rather than relying on secondary aggregation for those two inputs.

### Conclusion C2: Timed in-person proctoring and oral defense of take-home work are two independent, sufficient (not exclusive) routes to satisfying the certification requirement — but accommodation design is load-bearing

```text
GT-7 (credential validity requires I(Θ;artifact) > 0) + GT-8 (proctored rooms block networked AI access during production) + GT-4? (oral viva already adopted as a post-hoc AI-authentication mechanism)
→ controlling the artifact's production environment keeps the artifact causally tied to the individual by removing the untrusted external-generation channel
→ a live individually-administered probe after production re-establishes that same causal tie by testing whether the named individual can defend the reasoning behind the artifact on demand
→ timed in-person exams and oral-defense-of-take-home-work are therefore two independent sufficient routes to the attribution condition GT-7 requires, so take-home submission does not by itself violate that condition when one route is present
→[2nd] institutions adopting oral defense at scale must design a path for students who cannot straightforwardly do a live spoken Q&A (disability, language, anxiety, connectivity)
→[3rd] an accommodation that simply waives the oral component reverts the accommodated subgroup to the unauthenticated condition C1 already ruled invalid (contradicts the implicit premise that oral defense uniformly restores authentication — routes back to Phase 2; assumption A15 added, Verdict: Challenge) *[Assumes: A15]*
→ the accommodation must itself individually-authenticate by another channel (proctored written response, supervised extended-time oral, recorded structured interview) rather than a bare waiver, which keeps GT-7 satisfied for the accommodated subgroup too
```

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. GT-4? is unverified/reported-by-delegate. Raising to HIGH requires directly reading the named institutional policy document and obtaining measured (not anecdotal/policy-adoption-only) data on oral-defense authentication accuracy.

### Conclusion C3: Universal per-student oral defense does not scale to large courses, but a sampled or triggered version does

```text
GT-9 (examiner-time scales linearly with student count, a math identity) + GT-11? (illustrative 2-4 hrs/week marginal faculty-capacity bracket)
→ for a 200-student course, examining every student for 10 minutes each requires 2,000 minutes (~33.3 examiner-hours) of live oral time alone, before scheduling overhead
→ bracketing per-student time at [5, 10, 20] minutes and enrollment at [50, 200, 500] gives a full-cohort examiner-hour range of roughly [4, 33, 167] hours, competing with the same instructor's existing teaching, grading, and research load
→ at GT-11?'s realistic marginal-capacity bracket, full-cohort oral defense is infeasible within a single term at the central and upper estimates, and clears the bar only for the smallest classes
→ restricting the oral defense to a suspicion-triggered or randomly-sampled 10-15% of the cohort cuts the central 33-hour estimate to roughly 3.3-5 hours, which clears the same capacity bar at every enrollment size in the bracket
→ both the lower and upper bounds of the sampled-defense bracket resolve to the same decision, so the estimate is precise enough to act on without further measurement: universal oral defense is impractical at scale, but a sampled or triggered oral defense layered on take-home work is not
```

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. GT-11? is an unverified illustrative planning parameter (A13, flagged in §2). Raising to HIGH requires each institution measuring its own faculty marginal-capacity figure rather than using an illustrative bracket; GT-9 itself is a math identity and is HIGH on its own.

### Conclusion C4: An AI-permitting take-home redesign without normalized access reintroduces a socioeconomic confound into the credential signal

```text
GT-5? (economically advantaged students disproportionately hold paid AI subscriptions) + GT-7 (credential validity requires the artifact to track only the individual's own competence)
→ if AI use is implicitly permitted or unpoliced in take-home work, and better-resourced students access more capable paid tools, the quality gap between submissions increasingly reflects ability-to-pay rather than disciplinary competence
→ this reintroduces a variable (household income) into the artifact-to-competence link GT-7 requires to be clean, degrading the same attribution condition C1 found broken generally, now specifically along economic lines
→ a defensible take-home redesign must therefore either prohibit ungoverned AI use and pair the artifact with an authentication layer (C2's mechanism), or provide every enrolled student equal institutional access to the same AI tool, or accept that certification and ranking are compromised on equity grounds specifically, not merely on general fairness grounds
```

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. GT-5? is reported-by-delegate (a single aggregated multi-country survey, not opened at source). Raising to HIGH requires reading the underlying paper directly and/or a local audit of the specific student population's AI-access distribution, since a 155-country aggregate may not represent any one institution.

### Conclusion C5: The certification, formative-feedback, ranking, and incentive-shaping purposes of assessment do not require the same modality, so the binary framing itself is the wrong unit of decision

```text
GT-7 (credential validity requires I(Θ;artifact) > 0) + GT-10? (retrieval-effort, not modality, drives the testing effect) + GT-6? (licensure bodies require individual proctored certification only in regulated fields)
→ certification's requirement (attribution) is satisfied by GT-8 or GT-4?'s mechanisms and is largely modality-agnostic once one of those mechanisms is present
→ the formative/incentive-shaping purpose is satisfied by frequent, low-stakes, effortful retrieval regardless of whether that retrieval happens open-book or closed-book, so it does not itself require either timed exams or take-home essays specifically
→ ranking/sorting inherits whichever purpose's mechanism it is layered on top of, rather than requiring an independent modality of its own
→ a single modality choice answering "timed exam or take-home" therefore cannot be the right level of decision, because the purposes it is meant to jointly serve place different, separable requirements on the assessment design
```

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. GT-6? and GT-10? are unverified (not opened this session). Raising to HIGH requires a citation-level check of both the professional-licensure claim and the specific testing-effect studies invoked.

### Conclusion C6 (trade-off collapse): Take-home paired with sampled/triggered oral defense is the general-purpose default for certification-bearing assessment; plain take-home remains defensible for purely formative assessment

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Recommend universal AI-detector screening as the primary integrity mechanism for take-home work

**What was tried:** Considered recommending that universities adopt commercial AI-text-detector screening (e.g., a Turnitin-style AI score) as the backbone safeguard for take-home submissions.

**Why abandoned:** GT-2's theoretical ceiling shows detector-based screening cannot be trusted as a foundation under adversarial/paraphrase conditions, and GT-3?'s bias evidence (61.3% of non-native-English TOEFL essays misflagged in one study) makes detector-based enforcement itself a discrimination liability, not merely an imperfect-but-improving tool. Assumption A6 was explicitly Discarded in Phase 2 on this basis.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a reader from re-proposing "just buy a better detector" — the ceiling GT-2 identifies is structural, not a temporary product-maturity gap, so procurement of a newer commercial detector narrows only the convention-driven (bias) share of the problem, never the adversarial-paraphrase share.

### Dead End: Recommend a full reversion to timed in-person exams as the safe universal default

**What was tried:** Considered recommending Option A (pure timed in-person exam) across the board, given C1's finding that unverified take-home fails certification.

**Why abandoned:** The trade-off matrix (C6) shows Option A scores weakest on authenticity (2/5) and formative-feedback value (2/5), and GT-10? indicates high-stakes single-sitting exams are not even the best mechanism for the incentive-shaping purpose. A full reversion optimizes only the certification criterion at the cost of the other four, and C6's weighted comparison shows the hybrid (C) beats A even before the sensitivity check.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a reader from treating certification validity as the only purpose that matters — that would be inconsistent with the Phase 1 essence statement's explicit requirement to serve multiple assessment purposes.

### Dead End: Defer the policy question to future AI-content watermarking

**What was tried:** Explored whether provider-side generative-AI watermarking could restore certification validity for take-home work without any process change on the university's part.

**Why abandoned:** Watermarking only helps if the specific tool a student used embeds it, the student doesn't paraphrase it away (defeated by the same adversarial-robustness logic underlying GT-2), and the university can compel disclosure of which tool was used — none of which is within institutional control today. This depends on an external current constraint (industry-wide, paraphrase-robust, mandatory watermarking) with no visible expiry date a university can act on.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a reader from deferring the decision to "wait for better technology" — that is a bet on external actors, not a policy choice available now.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** For assessments that carry certification or high-stakes ranking weight, replace pure take-home writing with a hybrid: keep the take-home artifact for authenticity and formative value, but pair it with a sampled or suspicion-triggered oral defense (or an equally individually-authenticating follow-up) rather than either abandoning take-home work entirely or reverting to universal timed in-person exams (chain C6). For purely formative, non-certifying assessment, plain take-home work remains defensible and should not be abandoned merely because AI is available (chain C6).

**Key insight:** The vulnerability AI creates in take-home assessment is not that it is vaguely "too easy to fake" — it is a precise, information-theoretic one: a credential only carries information about an individual's competence if the observed artifact is statistically correlated with that specific individual, and current AI-text-detection technology cannot restore that correlation because part of the gap is a mathematical ceiling, not an engineering shortfall (chain C1). This reframes the fix away from "better plagiarism software" and toward mechanisms that couple the individual to the artifact directly — controlled production or live post-hoc defense — a different lever than the timed-vs-take-home modality debate the question as posed assumes is the whole story (chain C2).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The hybrid model costs real faculty time a pure take-home policy would not (chain C3), and its oral-defense component must be deliberately designed so disability/language accommodations do not quietly revert the accommodated subgroup to the unauthenticated condition the policy exists to fix (chain C2). It does not solve the equity gap created by unequal paid-AI access on its own — that requires either restricting ungoverned AI use or providing institution-funded equal access alongside the hybrid design (chain C4). Applying the same hybrid rule to purely formative low-stakes work is not cost-justified, since the sensitivity check shows plain take-home is nearly as strong there (chain C6) — no chain — flagged assumption only for exactly how "purely formative" should be operationalized at any one institution.

## Bottom line

Replacing timed in-person exams with take-home assessment **should not be done as a blanket swap**, and the reason is sharper than "cheating gets easier": a take-home artifact only certifies a specific student's competence if it is statistically tied to that student, and current AI-detection technology cannot restore that tie once broken — part of that failure is a mathematical ceiling (GT-2), not a product gap. The defensible move is **purpose-differentiated**: keep or expand take-home/authentic assessment for its real strengths (authenticity, formative value, cost), but wherever an assessment carries certification or high-stakes ranking weight, pair it with an independent, individually-administered authentication step — most practically a sampled or suspicion-triggered oral defense, not a universal one — while deliberately designing accommodation pathways so they don't quietly recreate the exact gap the policy exists to close, and addressing the AI-access equity gap directly rather than assuming it away.

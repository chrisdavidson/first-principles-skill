Here's the completed first-principles analysis.

## The real question isn't "exam or take-home"

It's: **for a given assessment, which combination of supervision, time-budget, connectivity, and AI-permission settings matches the specific competence being certified, for whoever relies on that certification** (student, instructor, accreditor, future employer) — without unacceptable costs in equity, faculty load, or credential trust.

## The mechanism (not "students might cheat")

Before LLMs, a take-home's looseness (unsupervised, untimed, connected) was harmless because faking a quality written artifact without actually having the competence was itself costly and risky — you needed a ghostwriter or a willing collaborator. That's the assumption the whole format quietly rested on.

What changed: capable assistants can now produce a fluent, well-organized artifact cheaply, fast, and largely undetectably (a 2023 study of seven GPT detectors found a 61% false-positive rate on non-native-English writing, and a single self-edit prompt cut detection of AI-generated essays from 100% to 13% — read at source, arXiv:2304.02819). The take-home's own operating conditions — no supervision, no clock pressure, internet access — are *exactly* the conditions under which that cheap production is least constrained. It's a resource-environment mismatch: the format's validity depended on an economic fact that LLMs falsified, not on students suddenly becoming more dishonest.

## Why the binary framing is false

Supervision, time-budget, and connectivity are three independent axes, not one switch — giving at least 8 combinations, several already in use (open-book timed exams, remotely-proctored take-homes, oral defenses with no artifact to outsource). There's a 4th axis the binary hides entirely: whether AI assistance is disallowed, tolerated, or the explicit thing being evaluated. Once you see that axis, "exam vs. take-home" collapses into a design problem, not a policy toggle.

## Where "it depends" actually cashes out

Every course's assessment is really certifying one of two different things:
- an **unaided-competence floor** (matters when a profession/regulator needs certified capability without tools — safety-critical or foundational-skill contexts), or
- **tool-inclusive competence** (the professional reality is you'll always have the assistant, so the skill worth certifying is using it well).

Content difficulty no longer tells you which bucket a course is in — GPT-4 clears the bar exam (≈297/400, above every jurisdiction's passing threshold) and scores 92–95% on the USMLE. Only the regulatory/professional purpose of the specific course decides the bucket, not how hard the material looks.

**Detection is a dead end either way** — unreliable (biased false-positive rate above) and trivially evadable, so it's at best a low-weight triage signal, never a primary control. 25+ universities have already restricted AI detectors in disciplinary decisions on this basis.

**Cost isn't the blocker people assume.** A rough Fermi estimate: full oral exams for everyone roughly doubles faculty grading load (~33–58 faculty-hours per 100 students combining written + oral), which is genuinely expensive at scale — but a spot-check oral audit on ~20% of submissions is an order of magnitude cheaper (~3-5 hours/100 students) and still works as a deterrent, because a real-time follow-up is much harder to fake than the original artifact was.

**There's a theoretical ceiling here worth naming explicitly:** no assessment not observed in real time can certify "produced without unauthorized assistance" with certainty — that's an observability limit, not a design flaw you can prompt-engineer around. Only two moves actually close the gap: real-time supervision, or redefining what's being certified (AI-permitted task design). Everything else is probabilistic mitigation below that ceiling.

**Doing nothing isn't neutral.** Unmitigated take-homes at scale plausibly compress grade distributions upward (even with no change in actual competence), which by signaling theory erodes the credential's value to employers regardless of individual student quality — and creates a new inequity (AI-access/fluency) replacing the old one (exam anxiety) rather than eliminating inequity. This is the weakest chain in the analysis (rated MEDIUM/LOW confidence) — plausible, not proven — and worth monitoring via employer feedback rather than treating as settled fact.

## Recommendation: a three-tier, per-course policy — not a campus-wide switch

1. **Tier 1 (unaided-floor courses — licensure-adjacent, regulatory, foundational skills):** keep or move to supervised/proctored/oral formats. Note: real-time oral audits have their own emerging weak spot — covert real-time AI assistance via earpieces/smart glasses — which device-free protocols need to address; this is flagged as unresolved, not solved.
2. **Tier 2 (the general-curriculum middle):** hybrid take-home — require process evidence (drafts, version history) plus a cheap spot-check oral audit (~20% of submissions). This reintroduces just enough real-time observation to make the expected cost of undisclosed misuse exceed the benefit for most students, without paying full-oral-for-everyone costs.
3. **Tier 3 (genuinely tool-inclusive professional skills):** keep the unsupervised take-home, but redesign the task to require and explicitly evaluate AI-assisted output, with disclosure.

**Bottom line:** a university shouldn't pick one format campus-wide. It should sort its own assessments by what they're actually certifying, and only the courses in the unaided-floor bucket need to move toward exam-like conditions — the rest are better served by redesign or a lightweight audit layer than by abandoning take-homes.

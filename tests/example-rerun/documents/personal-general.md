## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: applying the cost-of-living percentage directly to gross household income

**What was tried:** an early pass multiplied GT-5's 50.8% figure directly against the household's total income (rather than against locally-priced spending) to estimate the cost-of-living drag.

**Why abandoned:** cost-of-living indices are constructed against a market basket of local goods, services, and housing — not against income. A meaningful share of most households' income (savings, debt service, goods bought online at national prices) does not scale with local cost of living at all. Applying the percentage to gross income would have overstated the drag substantially and produced a bracket with a much more alarming, and wrong, lower bound.

**What it ruled out:** treating "SF is 51% more expensive" as "your income needs to be 51% higher to break even" — a common and materially wrong shortcut.

### Dead End: treating the state-tax difference as the dominant financial factor

**What was tried:** an initial framing (matching the common "moving to California is a tax disaster" intuition) treated state income tax as the main financial risk of the move, before GT-6 and GT-7 were read directly.

**Why abandoned:** once Oregon's own top marginal rate (9.9%, starting at $125,000) was checked against California's marginal rate at the relevant income band (roughly 9.3%–10.3%), the two turned out to be close — the marginal tax delta on the extra $70K is a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars, not a decisive factor. This dead end is worth recording specifically because it is the most common wrong intuition about this exact kind of move, and it is not this household's actual biggest financial risk.

**What it ruled out:** budgeting time and attention toward "minimizing California tax exposure" as if it were the central financial question — it isn't; cost-of-living exposure and the partner's income continuity are.

### Dead End: assigning a specific dollar figure to the partner's career-disruption risk

**What was tried:** an attempt to estimate a dollar cost for the partner needing to restart or relocate their career, to fold directly into the C1 estimate bracket.

**Why abandoned:** no ground truth exists about the partner's profession, its licensing or client-portability requirements, or the depth of that field's Bay Area job market. Any number produced would have been fabricated rather than derived, and would have falsely dressed a guess as a calculation.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Don't decide between accepting and declining yet. Have a genuine, two-way conversation with your partner first — not a formality, a real one — about whether their career could transfer to the Bay Area, at what cost, or whether a long-distance arrangement is something they'd actually consider. Then, on a short, explicit timeline (one to two weeks, stated to both employers up front), run two things in parallel: ask the new company whether the relocation timeline, in-office schedule, or offer structure has any flexibility, and take the offer to your current employer to see whether they'll counter. While that's happening, get the four real numbers: your household's actual annual budget (not a city-wide index), the exact base/equity split in the new offer, your current employer's own unvested-equity vesting date, and the new offer's vesting schedule. Only decide once those come back (chain C4). If neither negotiation produces anything better and the numbers land unfavorably, declining and staying is not a consolation prize — on today's information it ties for the strongest option, and it's the only one that's both fully reversible and leaves your partner's career untouched (chain C3).

**Key insight:** Two things about this offer are close to the opposite of what they look like at first glance. First, the tax difference between Oregon and California is nearly a rounding error at this income level — Oregon's own top income-tax rate is already 9.9%, so the marginal state-tax cost of the move is likely a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars a year, not the large hit most people assume (chain C1). Second, the real threat to the $70K raise isn't taxes, and it isn't even "the cost of living" in the abstract — it's your own household's specific spending, especially housing, which a generic city-comparison number cannot tell you, combined with the fact that a meaningful share of that $70K is almost certainly unvested equity subject to a standard one-year cliff, meaning the number you were quoted is not the number you'd actually have in hand twelve months from now (chains C1, C2).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Running both negotiations in parallel costs little financially, but it isn't free relationally — raising the offer with your current manager as leverage carries a real, usually survivable, cost to that working relationship even if they counter successfully, and it's worth walking in prepared for that rather than assuming it away (chain C4).

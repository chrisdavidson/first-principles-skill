# First-Principles Analysis: 5 MWh Molten-Salt Thermal Storage — Round-Trip Efficiency and Cost-Competitiveness Claim

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Does a 5 MWh molten-salt thermal energy storage system operating between 290°C and 565°C achieve an electricity-round-trip efficiency above 85%, and — under whatever reading of "round trip" and "nameplate capacity" the claim actually supports — is such a system cost-competitive, on an installed-$/kWh basis, with a lithium-ion battery system of equal nameplate capacity?

**Success criteria:**
1. States explicitly whether "round trip" is read as electricity→heat→electricity (full reconversion through a heat engine) or electricity→heat→heat-delivered (no reconversion), and shows that the Conclusion's verdict depends on which reading is used.
2. Computes the Carnot (2nd-law) ceiling on heat-to-electricity conversion at the claim's own stated temperatures and states whether 85% is inside or outside that ceiling.
3. Cites real-world achieved round-trip efficiencies for comparable high-temperature sensible-heat "Carnot battery" / electric thermal energy storage systems as an independent corroborating check on the theoretical ceiling.
4. States a $/kWh figure for (a) thermal-storage-only capital cost, (b) a full electricity-in/electricity-out system at the claim's stated 5 MWh scale, and (c) fully-installed utility-scale lithium-ion capital cost, and compares them on a like-for-like basis.
5. Flags explicitly whether "same nameplate capacity" is a well-defined comparison basis (electrical kWh vs. thermal kWh) and states the consequence for the cost-competitiveness claim if it is not.
6. Reaches a bounded verdict — true, false, or conditionally true — naming the exact conditions (if any) under which the compound claim as stated would hold.

### Conclusion C1: An electricity-to-electricity round-trip efficiency above 85% is thermodynamically impossible for this system at the stated temperatures

GT-1 (claim's stated 290-565°C range) + GT-2 (Carnot limit, 2nd law) + GT-9 (ambient/condenser temp 25-45°C)
→ converting Th=838.15K (565°C) and Tc=298.15-318.15K (25-45°C) through the Carnot formula gives a maximum theoretical heat-to-electricity conversion efficiency of 62.0-64.4%, the ideal ceiling for this temperature pair
→ this ideal ceiling bounds only the heat-engine discharge stage, and every other loss in the system can only push achievable round-trip efficiency below it, never above it *[Assumes: A11]*
→ an electricity-to-electricity round-trip efficiency above 85% is therefore thermodynamically impossible for a system whose only means of returning electricity is a heat engine operating between 565°C and typical ambient temperature

### Conclusion C4: The Carnot ceiling, not component engineering, is the irreducible bottleneck on electricity-to-electricity round-trip efficiency

GT-2 (Carnot limit) + GT-9 (ambient temp range) + C1 (62.0-64.4% ceiling)
→ decomposing "round-trip system efficiency" into its constituent stages — electricity-to-heat charging, thermal-storage retention, and heat-to-electricity discharge — shows that only the discharge stage is bound by the Carnot limit; the other two stages can independently approach 95-99% efficiency with good engineering *[Assumes: A12]*

→ no incremental engineering improvement to charging or storage equipment can close a 20-plus-percentage-point gap between this system's achievable ceiling and an 85% round-trip electricity-to-electricity target

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Treating "round trip" as equally ambiguous between the electricity and heat readings, with no controlling interpretation

**What was tried:** An initial framing gave the electricity-to-electricity reading and the heat-delivery-only reading equal weight, deferring any judgment about which one the claim actually asserts.

**Why abandoned:** The claim's own text — "round-trip electricity into heat and back" — is not neutral between the two readings in ordinary engineering usage; "back" most naturally refers back to the first noun in the chain of custody (electricity), not to "heat." Treating the two readings as equally likely would have produced an unfalsifiable, non-committal analysis rather than a bounded verdict, which fails the Problem Essence's success criteria.

**What it ruled out:** This saves a future reader from re-litigating the interpretive question from scratch; chain C1 uses the electricity-to-electricity reading as the claim's controlling interpretation, and chain C3 explicitly carries the heat-delivery reading forward as a named, addressed alternative rather than an unresolved ambiguity.

### Dead End: A non-Rankine heat-engine technology (e.g., supercritical CO2 Brayton, thermophotovoltaics) bypassing the Carnot ceiling

**What was tried:** Considered whether a different heat-to-electricity conversion technology, rather than a conventional steam Rankine cycle, could allow the system to exceed the 62-64% Carnot ceiling computed in chain C1.

**Why abandoned:** The Carnot limit is a Second-Law consequence of the temperature difference between hot and cold reservoirs alone; it is independent of the specific heat-engine technology used to extract work from that temperature difference (this is GT-2, a physical law). No heat-engine technology — supercritical CO2, organic Rankine, or otherwise — can exceed it. This path was explored in the Phase 5 adversarial pass (Inversion) as a candidate way the headline conclusion could be wrong, and was explicitly ruled out there.

**What it ruled out:** This closes off "a better power cycle would fix it" as a rescue for the 85% claim under the electricity-to-electricity reading — the shortfall in chain C1/C2/C4 is not an engineering-quality problem, it is a hard physical ceiling, and no future engineering improvement within known heat-engine physics can close a 20-plus-point gap to 85%.

### Dead End: A full weighted-criteria trade-off comparison between the molten-salt system and lithium-ion across multiple decision criteria

**What was tried:** Considered applying the trade-off analysis procedure (Phase 4) to compare the two technologies across criteria such as cost, footprint, cycle life, and safety.

**Why abandoned:** The trade-off procedure is designed for choosing among viable options when the ground truths support more than one candidate; this analysis is not a "which option should be chosen" decision but a "is this specific factual claim about one option true" verification. Applying a weighted-scoring procedure here would have manufactured a false sense of multi-criteria balance around a question that is actually a binary truth-check against physical law and cost benchmarks.

**What it ruled out:** This confirms the trade-off technique is not applicable to this analysis (see the Techniques-not-applied disclosure in the process output accompanying this document) and that the Estimate procedure (chain C6) is the correct tool for the cost side of the question instead.

## 6. Conclusion

**Key insight:** The claim silently conflates two structurally different systems under one phrase — "round-trip electricity into heat and back." As an electricity-to-electricity Carnot battery, 85% round-trip efficiency is not an engineering shortfall but a violation of the ~62-64% Carnot ceiling computed directly from the claim's own stated 565°C/290°C operating range (chain C1), a finding corroborated by real-world Carnot-battery and CSP power-cycle data (chain C2) and shown to be irreducible to any component-engineering improvement (chain C4). As a heat-delivery-only system (electricity in, heat/steam out, no reconversion), the same 85% figure is not merely plausible but conservative — commercially operating systems already exceed 97% (chain C3) — because bypassing the heat-engine stage entirely removes the Carnot penalty altogether. Reasoning by analogy to "storage is storage" would have missed this distinction entirely; first-principles decomposition of what "round trip" physically requires is what exposes it.

**Recommended verdict:** As literally worded — "round-trip electricity into heat and back," which denotes reconversion to electricity — the compound claim is false. An 85%+ electricity-to-electricity round-trip efficiency is thermodynamically unachievable at 565°C/290°C (chain C1), is roughly 20-35 percentage points above anything reported in the literature for comparable real systems (chain C2), and the accompanying cost-competitiveness assertion also fails at the claim's stated 5 MWh scale: a full round-trip system's installed cost is estimated at roughly $450-960/kWh (central ~$720/kWh) against lithium-ion's fully-installed $290-400/kWh (chain C6) — the thermal-storage tank alone is cheap (chain C5), but the power-conversion equipment required to return electricity is not, especially below utility scale.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Read charitably as a heat-delivery-only system, the efficiency figure is valid and conservative (chain C3), but the cost-competitiveness comparison then becomes invalid on its own terms — it compares $/kWh of delivered heat against $/kWh of delivered electricity, two non-substitutable products serving different end-uses (chain C7) — no chain — flagged assumption only, since which end-use the claim's audience actually needs is not stated anywhere in the claim and cannot be resolved from the text alone. This analysis could not independently open several primary technical and cost sources within this session (a Wiley thermodynamics paper and an industry-news article returned HTTP 403; two NREL pages/PDFs failed DNS resolution; one arXiv PDF exceeded the fetch size limit), so the real-world Carnot-battery efficiency range (chain C2) and the thermal-storage-only cost benchmark (chains C5, C6) remain `?`-marked and capped at MEDIUM rather than HIGH; the headline thermodynamic-impossibility finding (chain C1) does not depend on any of these unopened sources and is unaffected by this limitation. The small-scale power-block cost bracket used in chain C6 is an explicitly flagged, uncited engineering estimate — the single weakest link in the cost analysis and the most productive target for follow-up verification (chain C6).

**Under what conditions could the claim hold:** (1) If "round trip" means heat delivered as heat or steam for direct industrial use, not reconverted to electricity, the efficiency claim is valid and conservative (chain C3), though the cost-competitiveness comparison then requires the buyer's actual need to be process heat, not electricity (chain C7). (2) If deployed at utility scale — tens of MW and hundreds-to-thousands of MWh, not the claim's stated 5 MWh — power-block economies of scale would substantially lower the installed $/kWh estimated in chain C6, potentially approaching lithium-ion's cost; but this contradicts the claim's own stated 5 MWh scale and would need explicit re-verification against lithium-ion's own falling cost trajectory (chain C8), not be assumed true by extrapolation. (3) No adjustment of ambient/heat-rejection temperature or hot-side temperature within the claim's own stated 290-565°C range can satisfy the 85% electricity-to-electricity efficiency figure (chain C1); this condition is not satisfiable as the claim is stated.

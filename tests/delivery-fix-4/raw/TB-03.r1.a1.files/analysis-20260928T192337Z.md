## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End 1: A hard knockout eliminating full microservices outright

**What was tried:** An attempt to set a must-have knockout criterion in the trade-off procedure — "must not consume more than 30% of team capacity in ongoing ops tax" — which would have eliminated Option D (full microservices) before scoring.

**Why abandoned:** The C6 estimate bracket for ops-tax capacity consumption (4%-50%) is too wide and too unverified (resting on `[Assumes: A-15]`, an unpiloted assumption about decomposition granularity) to justify a hard elimination threshold; treating it as a knockout would overclaim precision the estimate does not support, violating the Estimate procedure's decision-resolution honesty requirement.

**What it ruled out:** Prevents a future re-analysis from treating full microservices as flatly infeasible on capacity-tax grounds alone — it remains a scored (weighted), not eliminated, option, so the trade-off stays auditable rather than resting on an unjustified hard cutoff.

### Dead End 2: "Decompose early, while it's cheap" (the microservices-first rival)

**What was tried:** Considered recommending full or partial microservices now on the theory that decomposing before the monolith calcifies is cheaper than decomposing later — the classic counter-argument to "monolith first."

**Why abandoned:** Directly contradicted by GT-2 (Fowler: "don't even consider microservices unless you have a system that's too complex to manage as a monolith") and GT-5 (Amazon's own retrospective: service count grew as team count grew, not ahead of it) — both read-at-source facts from organizations operating at far larger scale, and neither supports front-loading decomposition before organizational/scale need exists.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future analyst from re-litigating "decompose early to avoid technical debt" as a first-order justification at this team size/load — the sourced evidence points the other way. This is the rival ruling-out cited on chain C1's confidence line.

### Dead End 3: Treating Amazon's two-pizza-team practices as a direct playbook

**What was tried:** Initially considered anchoring the whole analysis to Amazon's operating practices as a general "do what Amazon does" prescription for this team.

**Why abandoned:** Amazon's practices describe an organization with many teams and 200+ services; direct analogy to a single 6-person team with one deployable would be reasoning by analogy without grounding, which this methodology disallows. GT-5 is instead used narrowly, only for the specific, transferable claim it actually supports (service-count growth follows team-count growth), not as a general prescription to imitate Amazon's architecture.

**What it ruled out:** Prevents overreaching claims like "Amazon does microservices, so this team should too," which ignore the multi-order-of-magnitude scale difference between the two organizations.

### Dead End 4: "Encapsulation behind clean APIs reduces communication overhead" (the rival to chain C2)

**What was tried:** Considered the argument that decomposing into services with well-defined APIs reduces effective team communication overhead by hiding complexity behind interfaces, rather than adding to it.

**Why abandoned:** This argument treats a service API as free, but per GT-4 a service API crossing a network boundary is itself a new negotiated contract subject to the eight fallacies (unreliable, non-zero latency, administratively distinct) — it trades internal, in-process coupling for network-mediated coupling rather than eliminating coordination cost. The "hiding complexity" benefit is real for a well-designed *internal* module boundary (which Option B, the recommended modular monolith, also provides) but does not depend on crossing a network boundary to obtain it.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future analyst from treating "encapsulation" as an argument unique to microservices — it is an argument for clean boundaries in general, satisfied by C7's recommended Option B without paying C3's/C6's network-call tax.


## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Refactor the existing single deployable into an enforced modular monolith now — explicit internal module boundaries, CI-enforced dependency direction, and codeowners per module — rather than decomposing into microservices; reserve selective service extraction for any one subsystem that can name a concrete, currently-existing resource or runtime mismatch with the rest of the monolith (chain C7).

**Key insight:** The usual "microservices vs. monolith" framing treats this as a technology choice, but Conway's Law and Amazon's own service-growth history reframe it as an organizational-design choice that architecture should follow, not lead — at 6 engineers and 1,200 DAU, neither the load precondition nor the organizational-boundary precondition for microservices' benefits currently holds, while the operational tax they impose is paid immediately regardless (chain C1, chain C4, chain C6).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:**
- This recommendation defers any team-boundary fault isolation until the team or load actually grows into needing it, accepting a wider (if currently low-probability) blast radius from a single-deployable outage in the meantime (chain C5; mitigated per the pre-mortem's Cluster 5 disposition).
- It defers whatever recruiting/retention value engineers place on hands-on microservices/cloud-native experience — no chain — flagged assumption only.
- It depends on the modular-monolith boundaries actually being enforced rather than merely drawn once and left to erode (chain C7; the pre-mortem's Cluster 2 disposition names the required enforcement mechanisms as part of the recommendation, not optional).
- It does not resolve cases where the real decision driver is organizational politics, an executive mandate, or investor optics rather than engineering need — this analysis can only speak to the technical merits — no chain — flagged assumption only.

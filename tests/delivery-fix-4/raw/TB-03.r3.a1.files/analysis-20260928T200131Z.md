### Conclusion C2: Splitting a monolith into services imposes an irreducible latency and failure-mode tax, independent of tooling or engineering skill

GT-5 (in-process ~0.5-100ns vs. same-DC RPC round trip ~500,000ns)
→ an in-process function call costs on the order of nanoseconds, roughly 0.5ns for an L1-cache-resident operation up to about 100ns for a call that touches main memory
→ crossing a network boundary for the same call imposes a same-datacenter round trip of about 500,000ns, a floor that is roughly 5,000 to 1,000,000 times the in-process cost depending which in-process baseline is used, before any serialization, retries, or auth-check overhead is added
→ this floor is irreducible rather than a tooling artifact -- better RPC frameworks can approach it but cannot cross it, because crossing a process or machine boundary can never be as cheap as not crossing one -- which rules out the rival reading that modern service-mesh or gRPC tooling makes the tax negligible in practice
→ every additional hop in a call chain compounds this floor at least linearly and adds independent failure modes (timeout, partial failure, retry-induced duplicate effects) that an in-process call structurally cannot have
→ conclusion: decomposing a monolith into services is not latency-free or failure-mode-neutral -- it imposes an irreducible tax that must be justified by a benefit large enough to be worth paying for, independent of engineering skill

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End 1: Full microservices from day one, treated as the default/best-practice choice

**What was tried:** Scored "full microservices" (option D) in the section-4 trade-off matrix (chain C5) alongside the status-quo monolith, a modular monolith, and selective extraction, using criteria weighted from C1-C4's findings.

**Why abandoned:** Option D totaled 40 of a possible 165, dominated on 6 of 7 weighted criteria by the modular monolith (option B, total 104); the flip-test showed no single-criterion reweight within the valid 1-5 weight range can make D competitive (it would require a weight of roughly 66 on D's one leading criterion, fault isolation, versus a maximum allowed weight of 5).

**What it ruled out:** The convention-driven assumption (A1, A2, A4, A6) that microservices are simply the modern best-practice default regardless of scale; it also grounds why C2's irreducible network tax and C3's team-size gating are treated as binding constraints rather than minor caveats in the final recommendation.

### Dead End 2: Selective extraction (option C) as the headline recommendation instead of a modular monolith

**What was tried:** Scored "selective extraction now" (extract one clearly-isolatable component, e.g. an async worker) as a competing option in the same trade-off matrix (chain C5).

**Why abandoned:** Option C totaled 87, close to but below the modular monolith's 104; the flip-test showed C cannot overtake B within the valid 1-5 weight range either (it would require a weight of roughly 19 on C's one leading criterion, fault isolation, versus a maximum allowed weight of 5).

**What it ruled out:** The belief that "any decomposition now is strictly better than none" -- boundary clarity without decomposition (option B) captures most of C's benefit at lower cost and risk, before a genuine hot path has been identified by production data. This dead end directly supports the flip-condition framing in chain C7: extraction becomes the right move only once a specific trigger is observed, not as a default first step.

### Dead End 3: Treating the platform-team model (many small teams, each owning services, à la Amazon's "two-pizza teams") as directly applicable to this team

**What was tried:** Considered whether a platform-team investment could let 6 engineers collectively own many more than 5-6 services, bypassing chain C3's team-size gating (the A15 rival named in C3's confidence line).

**Why abandoned:** GT-6 shows that even a company with substantially more total headcount than this team, attempting exactly this kind of decomposition, still had 3 full-time engineers consumed by platform/ops overhead at 140 services -- evidence that the platform-team model requires a funding and headcount investment this team does not currently have, rather than being a way around the team-size constraint at this team's scale.

**What it ruled out:** Direct analogy to large-company practice (Amazon, Google) without grounding in a ground truth about this team's own resourcing; per the no-analogy-as-direct-evidence discipline, this path is not cited as justification anywhere in section 4 except as a named, bounded flip condition in chain C7 ("funded platform/infrastructure capacity established ahead of decomposing").

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Adopt a modular monolith now: enforce clear internal module boundaries within the single deployable unit (dependency-direction rules checked in CI, not left as an unenforced convention), and defer both full microservices and ad hoc selective extraction until a specific, evidenced trigger makes extraction worth its cost (chain C5).

**Key insight:** The five canonical justifications for microservices -- independent scaling, independent deployability, fault isolation, team autonomy, polyglot flexibility -- are not simply true or false at this scale; each is gated by a specific, checkable threshold (load, headcount, funded ops capacity, demonstrated divergence) that this team currently sits below on every axis simultaneously, and the industry research most often cited in favor of microservices (DORA) attributes its own benefit to loose coupling matched to team size, not to service count or pre-emptive adoption (chains C1, C3, C4).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This recommendation accepts a real but currently low-stakes fault-isolation cost (a bug or resource leak in one module can still affect the whole process, mitigated by running the highest-risk module as a separate worker process) and a modest near-term module-boundary-enforcement cost, in exchange for avoiding the far larger, empirically-observed operational tax of premature decomposition (chain C6).

**Conditions that flip this recommendation:**
- Sustained load growth of roughly one to two orders of magnitude over the ~1,200 DAU baseline, toward ~10,000-100,000+ DAU, sustained rather than a single spike (chain C7).
- Engineering headcount growth to roughly 12-18, organized into two or more natural, domain-distinct sub-teams (chain C7).
- Funding a dedicated platform or infrastructure capability ahead of decomposing -- not merely deciding to decompose (chain C7).
- A specific component demonstrating divergent scaling, isolation, or release-cadence needs, backed by production data, which is sufficient on its own to justify selective extraction of that one component even if the other three conditions have not cleared (chain C7).
- Before applying this recommendation at all, verify whether the current monolith is already blocking deployment velocity via coordination bottlenecks (merge conflicts, CI-queue contention, release-train delays); this analysis could not check that against this specific team's actual data, and if it is already true, it would be the strongest evidence-based case for decomposition available -- no chain -- flagged assumption only.

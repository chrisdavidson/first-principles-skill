# First-Principles Analysis: Should We Buy Faster CI Runners?

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Leadership has proposed hardware acceleration (faster CI runners) as the fix for a 47-minute CI pipeline that is believed to be driving developers to batch changes into fewer, larger merges — but hardware acceleration only helps if the pipeline's time is dominated by raw compute throughput, and the *actual problem worth solving* may be the downstream behavioral and risk consequences of a slow feedback loop (merge-conflict risk, delayed feedback, larger blast radius), not the number "47" itself; these two framings can require different fixes, and neither the pipeline's time composition nor developers' true motivation for batching has been measured.

**Success criteria:**
1. The recommendation explicitly separates "is the pipeline compute-bound on its critical path" (a measurable technical question) from "will developers stop batching if CI gets faster" (a behavioral/threshold question) — the Conclusion section must name both and show they can have different answers.
2. The recommendation is actionable this week, at low cost, and does not require assuming facts about the pipeline's internal composition that nobody has yet measured — checkable by confirming the Conclusion's first recommended action requires no unverified `GT-N?` input about the org's own pipeline.
3. The recommendation states, with a derivation chain back to named ground truths, the specific conditions under which buying faster runners *would* be correct — checkable by confirming a chain in section 4 states this conditional explicitly rather than issuing a blanket "don't buy hardware."

### Inversion (Phase 2 — applied because leadership's conclusion, "buy faster runners fixes this," is stated as obvious and un-probed)

Claim inverted: *"Buying faster runners will fix the batching problem"* → *"Buying faster runners will NOT fix the batching problem."* Failure-guaranteeing conditions enumerated: (1) the pipeline's critical-path time is not dominated by compute/throughput; (2) even a real-time reduction doesn't cross whatever threshold actually governs developer re-engagement; (3) batching is driven by something other than CI wait time (code review SLA, deploy cadence, habit); (4) the pipeline's serial/DAG structure caps benefit regardless of per-node speed; (5) flaky-test retries or queueing dominate and are speed-insensitive. Necessary preconditions derived (each below, load-bearing, all currently unverified): **P1** — the bottleneck is measurably compute/throughput-bound on the critical path (→ A-5, load-bearing); **P2** — the resulting reduction crosses the behavioral re-engagement threshold (→ A-13, load-bearing); **P3** — CI time, not some other friction, is the dominant driver of batching (→ A-15, load-bearing).

## 3. Ground Truths

**GT-1** Amdahl's Law: overall speedup from accelerating one portion of a task is bounded by S(s) = 1/(1-p+p/s), where p is the fraction of execution time in the accelerated portion and s is that portion's speedup factor; as s→∞, speedup is capped at 1/(1-p) — "the overall performance improvement gained by optimizing a single part of a system is limited by the fraction of time that the improved part is actually used." — source: Amdahl's Law, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amdahl%27s_law; read-at-source: formula and diminishing-returns sections, quoted directly, fetched this session.

**GT-2** Little's Law: L = λW — the long-term average number of customers (or jobs) in a stationary queueing system equals the arrival rate multiplied by the average time each spends in the system; wait/queue time is driven by the relationship between arrival rate and service capacity (number of servers), and "in most queuing systems, service time is the bottleneck that creates the queue" — capacity (server *count*), not per-server *speed*, is what most directly relieves an arrival-rate-driven backlog. — source: Little's Law, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little%27s_law; read-at-source: formula and explanation sections, quoted directly, fetched this session.

**GT-3** Martin Fowler's canonical Continuous Integration guidance recommends "the XP guideline of a ten minute build," states most modern projects can hit this, and that "every minute chiseled off the build time is a minute saved for each developer every time they commit" — slow builds "suck the blood" from CI's core value because they remove the fast feedback that makes frequent integration manageable. — source: Martin Fowler, "Continuous Integration," martinfowler.com/articles/continuousIntegration.html; read-at-source: quoted directly, fetched this session.

**GT-4** DORA's guide to the Four Keys identifies "reducing the batch size of changes" as a common lever for improving delivery performance, because "smaller changes are easier to rationalize and to move through the delivery process" and "easier to recover from if there's a failure." — source: dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics-four-keys/; read-at-source: quoted directly, fetched this session.

**GT-5?** Google's own data found ~16% of tests exhibited flaky behavior, ~16% of total testing compute was consumed running/rerunning tests affected by flakiness, and 84% of pass-to-fail CI transitions involved a flaky test. — cited to: Google Testing Blog, "Where do our flaky tests come from?" (2017), via a WebSearch synthesis that also drew on a secondary aggregator (Visdom Maturity Matrix); reported-by-delegate: this analysis did not open testing.googleblog.com directly — cited source not opened by this analysis.

**GT-6?** Research by Gloria Mark (UC Irvine) found that, on average, it takes about 23 minutes 15 seconds for a person to fully return to a task at the same level of focus after an interruption requiring active thought. — cited to: Gloria Mark's research (UC Irvine, later summarized in *Attention Span*, 2023), via a WebSearch synthesis of secondary sources (Substack/blog aggregators); reported-by-delegate: this analysis did not open a primary Mark publication or a source directly quoting her original study — cited source not opened by this analysis.

**GT-7** Critical Path Method: in a dependency graph (DAG) of tasks/stages, total process duration equals the length of the longest sequence of dependent activities (the critical path); "activities that are off the critical path have float and are therefore not delaying completion" — shortening or speeding up non-critical-path work does not reduce total duration; only critical-path work does. — source: Critical Path Method, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_path_method; read-at-source: definition and core-principle sections, quoted directly, fetched this session.

**GT-8** GitHub Actions per-minute runner pricing scales super-linearly with core count: Linux 1-core (x64) is billed at $0.002/min, Linux 2-core (x64) at $0.006/min — doubling cores costs 3x the per-minute rate, confirming that faster/bigger hosted compute is a recurring cost that scales with both usage and speed tier, not a flat one-time fee. — source: GitHub Actions billing documentation, docs.github.com/en/billing/managing-billing-for-your-products/managing-billing-for-github-actions/about-billing-for-github-actions; read-at-source: pricing table quoted directly, fetched this session.

**GT-9?** [Situational] The organization's CI pipeline currently takes 47 minutes end-to-end. — unverified: stipulated by the requester as the scenario's premise; this analysis has no independent access to the org's system, and the pipeline's internal time composition is precisely the unmeasured fact this analysis identifies as decision-critical.

**GT-10?** [Situational] Developers have begun batching multiple changes into fewer, larger merges, reportedly to avoid triggering CI runs as often. — unverified: stipulated by the requester.

**GT-11?** [Situational] Leadership's proposed solution is to buy faster CI runners to bring the 47-minute figure down. — unverified: stipulated by the requester; this is the proposal under evaluation, not an established fact.

## 4. Derivation Chains

**Five-Whys (reduce-to-primitives mode) note on GT-1/GT-2/GT-7:** each of these three ground truths was checked for irreducibility before use — Amdahl's Law, Little's Law, and the Critical Path Method are each a named mathematical theorem with a closed-form proof; none decomposes into a more primitive fact within this domain, so each passes the irreducibility test directly (`Verified — mathematical theorem: <source>`) without further branching.

**Five-Whys (causal mode) note supporting C2, below:** *Why do developers batch merges?* → because triggering CI costs ~47 minutes of wait. *Why does that specific cost drive batching rather than mere annoyance?* → because the developer must mentally disengage during a wait that long, and batching amortizes the fixed cost of disengaging-and-re-engaging over more changes at once. *Why would amortizing be the rational response rather than tolerating N small waits?* → because if a single interruption costs ~23 minutes to recover from (GT-6?), N separate ~47-minute waits each carry that recovery tax once more than one combined wait does. *Why does this specifically bite now, at 47 minutes, and presumably didn't bite as hard when the pipeline was shorter?* → because pipeline duration crossed some disengagement-inducing threshold at some point (the exact crossing point and its relationship to today's 47 minutes is not established here — see A-13). This terminates in an actionable, in-control root cause: pipeline duration relative to a disengagement threshold, which is precisely what chain C2 formalizes below.

### Conclusion C1: Faster runners only help the fraction of the 47 minutes that is genuinely throughput-bound on the critical path — a fraction that is currently unmeasured

GT-1 (Amdahl's Law, S=1/(1-p+p/s)) + GT-7 (critical path: total duration = longest dependent-activity chain) + GT-2 (Little's Law: queueing wait is a function of arrival rate vs. server count)
→ hardware speed only reduces the portion of critical-path wall-clock time that is itself compute/throughput-bound; I/O wait, retry cycles, and queueing delay are governed by different variables (concurrency and count, not per-unit speed) and are structurally untouched by a faster processor
→ the return on "faster runners" spend is therefore capped by the fraction of the 47 minutes that is genuinely compute-bound on the critical path (A-5), a fraction nobody has yet measured for this org

### Conclusion C2: The behavior-change problem (batching) and the raw-number problem ("47 is too high") are not the same problem, and a fix that lowers the number without crossing the range associated with sustained engagement is unlikely to reverse batching

GT-3 (Fowler: ten-minute-build guideline; every minute saved matters) + GT-4 (DORA: smaller batch size improves delivery performance/stability) + GT-6? (Gloria Mark: ~23-minute refocus cost after an interruption requiring active thought)
→ industry guidance targets CI feedback in the single-digit-to-low-double-digit-minute range specifically because that is the range in which developers stay engaged and continue integrating frequently; once a wait exceeds the point at which a developer disengages to do something else, each additional slow cycle compounds into exactly the batching behavior this org reports [Assumes: A-13 — that a threshold model, not a purely linear/marginal-value model, governs this behavior]
→ a hardware-only fix that moves the pipeline from 47 minutes to roughly 30-40 minutes is unlikely to cross that engagement threshold, because both 47 and ~35 minutes sit well past the ~10-25 minute zone GT-3 and GT-6? point to
→ the behavior-change problem and the raw-number problem are therefore not interchangeable: a fix judged only by "did the number go down" can succeed by that measure while leaving the actual organizational risk (batching-driven merge conflicts, delayed feedback, larger blast radius) unresolved

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Recommend buying faster runners because it is the low-effort, leadership-endorsed path

**What was tried:** Accepting leadership's proposal at face value and simply recommending its execution, on the reasoning that "something is better than nothing" and hardware is the fastest thing to procure.

**Why abandoned:** Fails the trade-off matrix decisively (C6: weighted total 54, lowest of all four options, vs. 115 for diagnose-first) and rests on an unverified compute-bound assumption (A-5, feeding C1) that Amdahl's Law (C8) shows has a hard, possibly negligible ceiling regardless of spend.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future reader from re-litigating "just buy the runners" as a shortcut — the trade-off's flip test (C6) shows no plausible reweighting of the decision criteria rescues this option from last place.

### Dead End: Recommend re-architecting the pipeline (parallelize/cache/shard) immediately, without diagnosing

**What was tried:** Reasoning from the common industry lore that "CI slowness is usually a parallelization/caching problem," and recommending immediate re-architecture on that prior alone.

**Why abandoned:** Still a guess (C6: scores 3/5 on bottleneck-targeting probability vs. diagnose-first's 5/5) — it risks fixing the wrong thing if the true cause is queueing/concurrency (GT-2) or flaky tests (GT-5?), neither of which caching or parallelization addresses. Pre-mortem Cluster 2 further shows the "it's cheap, it's one-time" premise behind this option is itself fragile once legacy CI-config complexity is accounted for.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future reader from treating "just re-architect, everyone knows that's usually the problem" as a substitute for measurement — analogy-to-industry-lore is explicitly not used as direct evidence in this analysis (per the no-analogies rule); any such reference is grounded in a named ground truth (GT-5?) about a *specific, cited* other situation, not offered standalone.

### Dead End: Treat leadership's request literally as "make the number 47 smaller" and stop there

**What was tried:** Answering only the surface question — what single change most reliably reduces the 47-minute figure — without examining why leadership cares about that figure.

**Why abandoned:** This conflates the triggering event (a slow number) with the actual problem the situation itself flags: batching-driven merge-conflict risk, delayed feedback, and larger blast radius (C2). Per Phase 1 discipline, an Essence Statement built on the triggering event rather than the underlying question answers a proxy, and C2 shows the two questions can have different correct answers.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future reader from treating "the number went down" as sufficient success criteria — C2 and C8 both show a fix can lower the number while leaving the actual risk unaddressed.

### Dead End: Commit to a specific numeric CI target (e.g., "get to 10 minutes") before diagnosis

**What was tried:** Proposing a fixed target duration up front, to give leadership something concrete to plan against immediately.

**Why abandoned:** C8 shows the achievable floor depends entirely on the unmeasured compute-bound fraction p; committing to a specific number before diagnosis would repeat, in a different form, the same premature-commitment error C3 identifies in the original hardware proposal.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future reader from anchoring on a plausible-sounding target number before the diagnostic exists to justify it; pre-mortem Cluster 4's disposition (define a success metric jointly with leadership) is the corrected version of this idea, sequenced *after* the diagnostic scope is set rather than before it.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Diagnose the CI pipeline's actual time breakdown first — time the critical path by cause (compute, I/O, queueing, flaky-retry, serial-structure) using the fishbone's discriminating observations above — before committing to any spend; select the fix the data points to (hardware acceleration only if the bottleneck is genuinely compute-bound on the critical path; otherwise concurrency/runner-count increases, dependency/artifact caching, test sharding and parallelization, flaky-test remediation, or a fast-subset-plus-nightly test tier) (chains C4, C6).

**Key insight:** The "buy faster runners" proposal bundles two different bets that need not travel together — a technical bet (the pipeline is compute-bound) and a behavioral bet (a smaller number crosses whatever threshold actually governs re-engagement) — and Amdahl's Law puts a hard mathematical ceiling on the technical bet's payoff that no amount of budget can lift, a ceiling invisible to reasoning by analogy to "just buy better hardware" (chains C2, C8).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Diagnosing first delays any user-visible pipeline improvement by the diagnostic window, though that window is expected to be short (hours to a few days) if A-1 holds (chain C4); it also carries real organizational risk if not executed with explicit guardrails — a single low-load diagnostic sample can mislead (pre-mortem Cluster 1), the follow-on fix can cost more than expected once legacy CI-config complexity surfaces (pre-mortem Cluster 2), and the whole effort can be starved of staffing or killed by impatience if no success metric and no dedicated engineering time are locked in up front (pre-mortem Clusters 4 and 5) — no chain — flagged assumption only; and it may reveal that the correct fix is a multi-week re-architecture rather than the quick win leadership may be hoping for (chain C4).

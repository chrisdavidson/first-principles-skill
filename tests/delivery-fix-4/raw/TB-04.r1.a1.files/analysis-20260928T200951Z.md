## 1. Problem Essence

**Essence Statement:** Given a fixed target (cut commuter car journeys 20% within 3 years) and three candidate levers (fare-free transit, congestion pricing, protected cycle lanes) that operate through different behavioral mechanisms on different populations of trips, determine which lever, combination, or sequence of levers a specific mid-sized city should adopt — where the answer is a function of that city's density/sprawl, existing transit capacity, climate, budget structure, political/legal starting position, and equity commitments, not a universal ranking.

The prompt's own framing ("choosing between") treats the three as substitutes. That framing is itself a candidate assumption, not a given, and is challenged directly in Phase 2 and resolved in Phase 4 (Chain C6).

**Success criteria a correct answer must satisfy:**
1. States the specific causal chain (not "it should work") from each policy lever to an actual reduction in **car-commute trips** specifically — not proxy metrics like ridership counts or zone-entry vehicle volume, which the analysis must show are *not* the same thing as commuter mode-shift.
2. Grounds the magnitude of each lever's plausible effect in real observed cases, not intuition, and checks that magnitude against the stated 20%-in-3-years target.
3. Names the ground-truth constraints (cost per trip shifted, geography, elasticity, equity incidence, political feasibility, implementation lead time) as decision variables, not decoration.
4. Explicitly tests whether the three levers are substitutes, complements, or a sequence, rather than assuming the prompt's "choosing between" framing.
5. Produces a decision framework stated as conditional branches on verifiable city-level facts — not a single one-size-fits-all recommendation — while still naming which lever(s) the evidence favors as a general default when no city-specific fact yet displaces it.

### C6 — The three levers are complements, not substitutes (second-order extension)

*Second-order pass, both lenses walked: **actor lens** — commuters with no feasible alternative bear pricing's cost with no gain; transit agencies gain a self-funding constituency only under revenue-recycled pricing, not under a fare-free policy; parking-adjacent residents/businesses are the most common source of local opposition to cycle lanes; neighboring/boundary jurisdictions may see cordon-edge spillover traffic. **Time lens** — immediate (0-6mo) effects are dominated by novelty/announcement responses (largest at launch); medium-term (1-3yr, the policy window at issue) effects partially decay/rebound without a built-in escalation mechanism; long-term (>3yr) cycle-lane networks compound as they connect, while a static toll or fare level does not.*

```text
GT-6 (NYC: -11% CRZ volume, existing transit capacity absorbed diverted trips) + C2 (pricing mechanism) + C1 (transit weak)
→ NYC's diverted car trips in GT-6 were absorbed specifically because adequate transit capacity already existed to receive them, not because pricing alone converts trips into thin air
→ this shows congestion pricing's mode-shift depends on an already-adequate transit or cycling alternative existing to absorb diverted demand; without one, pricing mostly suppresses trips (fewer total trips, telework, hardship) rather than converting them — a second-order equity risk under the actor lens, since exactly the commuters with no alternative bear the toll's cost for no gain
→ symmetrically, C1 shows fare removal alone does not convert car commuters because price was rarely their binding constraint, but pairing a fare policy with a change that also improves the alternative's relative speed or reliability addresses exactly the mechanism fare removal alone misses
→ under the time lens, cycle-lane networks compound as they connect per GT-7's network effect, while a static pricing cordon or a static fare policy does not compound and is subject to decay/rebound per GT-4 and GT-5 absent a built-in escalation mechanism [Assumes: A11]
→ the three levers are complements operating on different trip populations, different time-horizons, and different funding structures; combining them addresses each lever's own second-order failure mode rather than requiring a single winner
```

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: transit ridership as a proxy for car-commute reduction

**What was tried:** Treating "increase transit ridership" as a usable stand-in for "reduce car commuting," which would have let the analysis rank levers on ridership figures alone.

**Why abandoned:** GT-2 and GT-3 show ridership and car-trip reduction are only weakly and inconsistently linked — ridership rose 28% in the Santiago RCT while car trips barely moved at all, so a ridership-based proxy would have misranked the levers on the analysis's actual target.

**What it ruled out:** Any use of a pure ridership-count metric (GT-2's 14-17% figures, GT-3's 28% figure) as direct evidence of car-commute reduction anywhere else in this analysis; chain C1 uses the car-trip figures specifically instead.

### Dead End: congestion pricing as the unconditional single winner

**What was tried:** Recommending congestion pricing outright as the dominant choice, on the strength of its largest observed launch-window effect sizes (GT-4, GT-5, GT-6).

**Why abandoned:** C5's trade-off shows no robust winner — the ranking flips under a 0.75-weight-point change on Equity or Political Feasibility — and GT-13?'s lead-time finding structurally conflicts with a 3-year deadline for a city with no existing legal/technical authority.

**What it ruled out:** A one-size-fits-all recommendation of congestion pricing; this dead end is what makes chain C7's city-conditioned sequencing framework necessary rather than optional.

### Dead End: Fermi cost-per-trip bracket as the sole ranking criterion

**What was tried:** Using the C4 Fermi cost-per-car-commute-trip-shifted bracket as the sole deciding criterion across all three levers.

**Why abandoned:** Comparably rigorous $/trip-shifted brackets could not be built for congestion pricing (capital/system cost not located in this session) or for a representative city's free-transit foregone-revenue figure, and a single-metric ranking ignores equity, political feasibility, and durability — exactly what the trade-off procedure's multi-criterion weighting exists to fold in instead.

### Dead End: treating cordon-traffic-reduction percentages as commuter-mode-share reduction

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** No single intervention should be chosen; the decision is a city-conditioned sequence and combination, not a pick among substitutes (chain C7). As a default sequence for a mid-sized city starting from typical conditions (no pre-existing tolling authority, moderate transit coverage, an average-density built form): in year 1, begin building a *connected* protected cycle-lane network rather than disconnected easy segments (chain C3, chain C7), pair it with *targeted* (income-qualified) transit fare relief rather than a blanket free-fare policy (chain C1, chain C7), and start the legal/political authorization process for congestion pricing immediately even though it will likely not be the load-bearing lever within the 3-year window (chain C7, GT-13?). If and when pricing authority is secured, launch it with revenue mandatorily ring-fenced for transit-capacity and cycle-network expansion, mirroring the design in GT-6, and write an automatic, formula-based escalation mechanism into the enabling rule at launch to counter the decay/rebound pattern documented in GT-4 and GT-5 (chain C7, `[Assumes: A14]`).

**Key insight:** Each lever's popular justification is not its actual car-commute-reduction mechanism. Free transit's real, evidenced effect is a mobility-equity gain for existing non-drivers, not a car-diversion tool (chain C1). Congestion pricing's real mechanism is charge-salience at the specific decision margin, not general price-sensitivity to driving — which is why it moves trips fast but only where an adequate alternative already exists to absorb them (chain C2, chain C6). Cycle lanes' real mechanism is removing a genuine safety barrier, which produces large *relative* gains off a low base but is capped by distance, climate, and cargo/childcare needs that no amount of infrastructure removes (chain C3). None of the three, alone, is sized to reach 20% in 3 years in the general case (chain C1, chain C3, chain C5); the trade-off among them is a near-three-way tie (chain C5), which is itself the reason the right unit of decision is a sequence conditioned on city facts (chain C7), not a ranking.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Cycle lanes are the fastest and most durable lever but have the lowest ceiling and no self-funding mechanism (chain C5). Congestion pricing has the highest ceiling and is revenue-positive but the slowest to authorize and the most politically contested, and its gains decay without a built-in escalation mechanism (chain C2, chain C5, GT-4, GT-5). Free transit is the most politically painless and most equity-positive on its own terms but the weakest car-commute-reduction lever per dollar of any of the three (chain C1, chain C5).

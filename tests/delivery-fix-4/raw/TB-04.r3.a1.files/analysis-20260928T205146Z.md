1. Chose free transit as the primary mode-shift lever in a low-baseline-ridership, car-dependent city, reproducing the Tallinn/Kansas City pattern (GT-4?, GT-5?) of ridership growth with little car displacement.

Commuter/rider viewpoint:
5. A ridership-growth lever (free or cheaper transit, or new car-to-transit converts from pricing) outpaced existing vehicle/frequency capacity without a matching investment, degrading reliability and comfort (raising the time/comfort terms of GT-8's generalized cost) and pushing marginal converts back to cars within 18-24 months.
6. Priced drivers diverted to parallel untolled streets rather than switching modes (a rerouting/boundary effect predicted by GT-9's induced-demand mechanism), worsening congestion and safety just outside the cordon and creating a vocal constituency demanding rollback.

Finance/budget-office viewpoint:
7. Free transit's operating subsidy grew faster than the general fund could sustain, echoing Kansas City's move toward reinstating fares (GT-5?), forcing a mid-course reversal that reset ridership gains and undermined public trust in the program's durability.
8. The bike-lane capital budget was cut in year two after an unrelated fiscal shock, halting network expansion before reaching the connectivity threshold needed for Seville-scale mode shift (GT-3).

Political-rival/adjacent-jurisdiction viewpoint:
9. Opposition organized during the vulnerable early period — before public opinion had a chance to shift the way it did post-launch in London and Stockholm (GT-1's own case history) — and secured a cap, delay, or carve-out on the pricing scheme before it reached full strength.
10. Parking-dependent business associations organized against lane-driven parking removal and secured a moratorium on further cycle-lane build-out after an initial pilot phase, capping the network below the threshold identified in cause 2.

# The Analysis

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given a mid-sized city's own structural profile (geography/density, trip-length distribution, existing transit capacity and quality, budget structure, and political economy), what decision rule should determine whether it deploys free transit, congestion pricing, protected cycle lanes, or a specific weighted combination of them, to have the best-founded chance of cutting commuter car journeys by 20% within three years?

**Success criteria:**
1. The Conclusion names the actual generalized-cost mechanism each intervention acts on, not merely a price/cost assumption.
2. The Conclusion states a Fermi-bracketed magnitude of 3-year car-trip reduction each lever (and their combination) can plausibly deliver, with explicit lower/upper bounds.
3. The Conclusion states the specific conditions under which combining levers outperforms treating them as forced alternatives.
4. The Conclusion names the specific city-level variables that determine which lever(s) fit a given city, rather than asserting one universally correct choice.
5. The Conclusion names at least one concrete second-order or pre-mortem risk that could cause a 20%-in-3-years target to fail even with a well-matched lever choice.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: City-wide flat-rate road pricing sized to hit 20% alone

**What was tried:** Considered whether a single lever — a sufficiently large, city-wide (not cordon-limited) flat-rate road-pricing scheme — could be sized to reach a 20% citywide car-trip reduction alone, regardless of a city's structural profile, simply by setting the price high enough against GT-2's elasticity curve.

**Why abandoned:** No case-study evidence at that scale was locatable in any source this analysis opened — every congestion-pricing case in GT-1 and GT-2 (London, Stockholm, New York, Oslo, Singapore, the Puget Sound study, the toll-elasticity meta-analyses) is cordon-, corridor-, or specific-facility-scale, not a flat city-wide charge. Extrapolating GT-2's elasticity curve to a much larger price change than any observed case is speculative rather than verified — the arch-elasticity relationship in GT-2's source is explicitly nonlinear and studied only within the observed range.

**What it ruled out:** Treating "just price hard enough, city-wide" as a verified shortcut around the structural-diagnostic approach. This dead end is preserved as the live, unruled-out rival on chain C2's confidence line rather than asserted as a viable path, and is the stated Falsification condition's boundary case.

### Dead End: Model the three interventions as strictly competing for one fixed "mode-shift budget"

**What was tried:** As a modeling simplification, tried treating congestion pricing, free transit, and cycle lanes as literally mutually exclusive — i.e., assuming the question's own framing was correct and scoring only "which one" rather than "which subset."

**Why abandoned:** Directly contradicted by the mechanism decomposition in GT-8, GT-10, GT-11, and GT-12 (chain C1): each intervention acts on a different term of generalized cost for a different mode, so nothing in the mechanism itself forces exclusivity. Retaining literal exclusivity as the model would have silently begged the question the analysis was asked to examine.

**What it ruled out:** A single-winner-only framework. It is retained only as a narrower, practical resource-constraint caveat (finite implementation bandwidth and political capital) within chain C5's sequencing logic, not as the primary model.

### Dead End: Rank the three interventions on one universal score, independent of city type

**What was tried:** Before building the archetype-conditional trade-off in chain C3, tried scoring the three interventions on a single universal weighted matrix that would apply to "a mid-sized city" in general, to avoid the added complexity of an archetype-dependent framework.

**Why abandoned:** Directly contradicted by GT-1's own cited finding (the toll needed to hit a fixed target varies roughly 2.5x with transit-service quality) and by GT-7?'s trip-length variability across cities: a universal ranking would assign the same score to cities for which the underlying mechanism produces materially different real-world outcomes, misrepresenting the analysis's own ground truths as city-invariant when they are explicitly conditional.

**What it ruled out:** A single fixed answer to "which of the three is best." This is precisely what forced the archetype-based trade-off (chain C3) and the seven-variable diagnostic (chain C5) rather than a simpler universal ranking.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Run the seven-variable structural diagnostic — cordon-capturable commute share, baseline transit quality, trip-length/topography/climate profile, budget structure, political-economy tolerance, car-dependency/parking economics, and institutional capacity for safeguards — before selecting a lever. Lead with congestion pricing (or a corridor-scale proxy where full cordon pricing is politically infeasible) where cordon-capturable commute share and existing transit quality are both moderate-to-high; lead with protected cycle-lane build-out where trip lengths are predominantly short and a connected network is achievable; use free or discounted transit primarily as an equity offset rather than the primary mode-shift lever, except where baseline transit ridership is already substantial; and pair whichever combination is chosen with three safeguards — citywide (not cordon-only) measurement, a pre-funded transit-capacity trigger, and a road-capacity reallocation plan (chain C5, chain C1).

**Key insight:** The question's own framing — pick one of three alternatives — is itself the primary obstacle to hitting the target. The three interventions are not competitors for the same causal lever; they zero out different terms of a generalized-cost equation for different modes and are mechanically combinable (chain C1). And on realistic city-structural parameters, no single lever plausibly reaches 20% for the most common (polycentric, weak-baseline-transit) mid-sized-city archetype — even a well-run combination lands in roughly a 3-13% bracket (chain C2). The deliverable a city actually needs is therefore not a lever choice but a structural diagnostic that determines which combination and sequencing fit its own profile, and whether 20%-in-3-years is even the right target for its structural type (chain C2, chain C5).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** A pricing-forward strategy trades faster, cheaper, revenue-positive mode-shift for higher up-front political risk and a documented early-opposition window (chain C3). A free-transit-forward strategy trades fiscal sustainability (ongoing subsidy growth) for stronger equity outcomes and political popularity, and becomes the stronger default only when a city discounts fiscal cost as a criterion (chain C3). Cycle-lane infrastructure trades a slow multi-year payoff, bounded by trip-length distribution, for durability against the induced-demand erosion that threatens pricing-only gains (chain C4, chain C2). Choosing any combination also means accepting the safeguard costs identified in the pre-mortem — a citywide measurement commitment, a funded transit-capacity trigger, and a road-capacity reallocation plan — as accepted risk mitigations, not optional extras (chain C4).

## 2. Assumptions Table

### Companion-technique inputs feeding this table

**Five-Whys (causal mode) — why do commuters in a mid-sized city currently drive rather than use transit, cycle, or walk?**

1. *Why drive?* Driving is faster and more flexible door-to-door than the available alternatives for the commuter's specific trip (contributing conditions, not sole causes: trip-chaining needs such as childcare drop-off or errands; low marginal operating cost once a vehicle is already owned). Counterfactual test: would the commuter still drive if transit/cycling were equally fast and flexible for their trip? No — the speed/flexibility gap is counterfactually necessary; drill further.
2. *Why is driving faster/more flexible?* Transit requires transfers or indirect routing for many origin-destination pairs in a lower-density city, and cycling lacks a safe, direct route on many corridors. Both pass the counterfactual test independently (a commuter with a safe cycling route but no direct transit route still faces the driving incentive from the transit side, and vice versa) — two parallel branches, not one.
3a. *Why does transit lack direct routing for many trips?* Historic underinvestment in frequency and coverage relative to road capacity, reinforced by a self-fulfilling cycle: lower density supports lower ridership per route-km, which funds lower frequency, which further discourages ridership. Stop test: the corrective (raise frequency/coverage on priority corridors) is specific and within a transit agency's practical control, given funding — **root cause, within control** (funding itself is a contributing condition outside the agency's own control).
3b. *Why does cycling lack safe direct routes?* Road space has historically been allocated to vehicle lanes and parking by design convention (level-of-service standards optimized for vehicle throughput), and retrofitting protected lanes requires removing that capacity.
4. *Why is removing car capacity for transit priority or protected cycling politically difficult?* Existing car-commuters — typically the commuting majority in a mid-sized, lower-density city — experience the removal as an immediate, concentrated personal cost, while the benefit (mode shift by others, less congestion) is diffuse and delayed. Stop test: the corrective (sequence the change to build visible benefit before asking for the costliest step, e.g., a trial period before a permanent charge) is specific and within a city government's control — **root cause, within control**. This is the deepest branch that still yields an actionable corrective; continuing to "because of general concentrated-cost/diffuse-benefit political economy" would leave the specific decision behind (depth guard applied).

**Fishbone (breadth-first causes behind car-commuting)**, default six-category set (no 6M/8P/4S preset fits a cross-city mobility question cleanly):

- **People:** trip-chaining needs; habit/inertia; personal-security concerns on transit or cycling after dark; sunk-cost reasoning once a vehicle is owned.
- **Process:** employer-set hours misaligned with peak transit frequency; free/subsidized employer parking far more common than transit or cycling benefits; zoning separating housing from employment.
- **Technology and Tools:** absence of protected, connected cycling infrastructure; transit frequency/reliability and lack of real-time information; fare-payment friction; road design historically optimized for vehicle throughput.
- **Environment:** climate discouraging cycling part of the year; topography; low density/sprawl making fixed-route transit inherently less efficient; raw home-work distance.
- **Information:** low awareness of available transit routes/schedules; systematic overestimation of transit travel time; low visibility of the true, often-subsidized cost of driving and parking.
- **Resources:** free or underpriced parking (a large hidden subsidy to driving); capital/operating budget constraints limiting transit service and cycling-network build pace.

Discriminating observations for the two highest-priority branches: (1) underpriced parking vs. poor transit frequency — compare mode choice between commuters with free employer parking and those paying market-rate parking, holding transit access constant; (2) cycling-infrastructure safety vs. distance/topography — among commuters within a short, flat, plausibly cyclable distance (<5km) who do not cycle, compare those whose specific route has a protected lane against those whose route does not, which holds distance and topography constant. Neither observation was collected this session; both branches enter the table below as unverified.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Pick a single winner via qualitative comparison, without quantifying mode-shift magnitude

**What was tried:** An early pass considered ranking the three options on a purely qualitative basis (popularity, ease of implementation, precedent) without building the Fermi brackets in chains C1-C3.

**Why abandoned:** A qualitative-only comparison cannot answer whether 20%/3yr is achievable at all, which the task explicitly requires, and would let a politically-appealing but quantitatively-insufficient option (most likely free transit, per its high equity/popularity profile) win by default despite its order-of-magnitude shortfall shown in chain C2.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future analyst from re-running a purely qualitative pairwise comparison; the Fermi brackets (chains C1-C4) are strictly more informative for the same question and should be built first.

### Dead End: Treat "mid-sized city" as interchangeable with the flagship case-study cities

**What was tried:** Initially considered applying Stockholm's, London's, and Paris's measured percentage effects directly to a generic "mid-sized city" without discounting for size/density differences.

**Why abandoned:** GT-9? establishes that mid-sized cities are structurally less monocentric and less densely served than these larger case-study cities; using their effect sizes directly would overstate plausible impact (an external-validity failure), which is why chains C1 and C3 apply explicit capture-share and transferability discounts rather than the raw case-study figures.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future analyst from citing "Stockholm cut traffic 20-25%, so this city will too" as if city size and geometry were irrelevant; that step is exactly the reasoning-by-analogy the methodology prohibits without grounding in a named ground truth about the target city's own structural comparability (GT-9?).

### Dead End: Recommend universal free transit as the primary lever on equity grounds

**What was tried:** Considered recommending universal fare elimination as the primary (not merely a component) lever, on the reasoning that it is the most straightforwardly progressive of the three options.

**Why abandoned:** Chain C2's Fermi bound and GT-3?'s qualitative finding show free transit's standalone car-mode-shift effect is roughly an order of magnitude short of the target; equity is better served by targeting transit relief plus earmarked pricing revenue (chain C6) than by universal free fares, which mostly subsidize trips that were never going to be made by car in the first place.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future analyst from treating "most equitable in isolation" as sufficient justification for a primary role; equity is retained as a portfolio design requirement (chain C6) rather than as the basis for choosing a single lever.

### Dead End: Treat congestion-pricing revenue as general-fund money

**What was tried:** Considered a version of the recommendation in which congestion-charge revenue is left unearmarked, available for any city budget priority.

**Why abandoned:** Doing so forfeits the anti-reversion mechanism the portfolio's second-order-effects reasoning (chain C6) depends on — GT-7?/GT-8? and GT-1's own 2006-2012 partial-reversion trend show that freed road capacity left unmanaged tends to be refilled by induced demand; the pre-mortem's Cluster A (revenue and capacity never actually reallocated) names this as the single most fatal, though recoverable, failure mode identified this session.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future analyst from proposing revenue-neutral or general-fund-routed pricing as a minor implementation detail; it is instead a load-bearing design requirement, not an afterthought.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** For a mid-sized city pursuing a 20%-in-3-years commuter car-journey reduction, the recommended approach is a combined, revenue-linked portfolio — peak-period congestion pricing whose revenue is earmarked to fund a prioritized protected cycle-lane network and means-tested (not universal) transit fare relief — rather than any single lever chosen as if the three were mutually exclusive (chain C5).

**Decision framework:** measure the city's cordon-capture share (the fraction of commute trips crossing a plausible priced zone), its cyclable-commute share (the fraction of commute trips within a plausible cycling distance), and its transit system's spare peak capacity before fixing the portfolio's internal weighting, then weight the pricing, cycling, and transit-relief legs in proportion to those three measured shares rather than applying a fixed, universal split (chain C7).

**Key insight:** The three options are not a menu to choose from but three levers with different, non-substitutable mechanisms — price on driving, safety on cycling, cost and capacity on transit — each bounded by a geography- and capacity-dependent ceiling far below 20% on its own (chains C1, C2, C3). Run alone, or run city-wide without a matching geographic grain, each option plausibly delivers less than a third of the stated target within three years, and the 20%-in-3-years target itself should be treated as a checkpoint on a longer trajectory rather than a fixed deadline (chain C4).

**Trade-offs acknowledged — two costs the portfolio accepts:**

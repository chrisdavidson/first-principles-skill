# First-Principles Analysis: "Buy Faster CI Runners" as a Fix for Merge-Batching

**Subject:** Should leadership buy faster/more-parallel CI hardware to cut a 47-minute pipeline, in order to stop developers batching changes into fewer merges?
**Scope note:** No company-specific CI telemetry (stage-level timings, queue-wait, flake/retry rate, commit-batching metrics) was supplied. This analysis reasons from the stated facts (47-minute end-to-end duration; observed batching behavior) plus verified general facts about CI systems, queueing, and developer-behavior research, and states explicitly wherever the absence of company data caps confidence.

---

## 1. Problem Essence

**Essence Statement:** Given a 47-minute CI pipeline and developers who have started batching changes to avoid re-triggering it, is procuring faster/more-parallel CI hardware the correct lever to pull — or does it spend money against a fraction of the problem that hardware cannot fix, while leaving the actual mechanism that drives batching untouched?

**Success criteria a correct answer must meet:**
1. Names what actually composes the 47 minutes (compute-bound/parallelizable vs. architectural/serial/I-O-bound), to the extent evidence allows, and states what is unknown.
2. Establishes the real causal mechanism linking pipeline characteristics to batching behavior — duration itself, or queue-wait, flakiness/retries, or fear of blocking others — rather than assuming "duration → batching" by default.
3. Compares hardware spend against software/architecture fixes on cost, root-cause fit, and bounded by Amdahl's Law.
4. Addresses second-order and threshold effects: does a partial time cut actually change behavior, or is there a discontinuity leadership's mental model ignores?
5. States a falsifiable test of the "buy faster runners" hypothesis and the theoretical floor on pipeline time even with infinite hardware.
6. Produces an actionable verdict: right call, partial fix, or wrong lever — and what to do instead/in addition.

## 3. Ground Truths

`?`-marked: GT-2, GT-5, GT-6 (3 of 7).

- **GT-1** (read-at-source): In a documented CI pipeline optimization case study starting at a nearly identical duration to this company's (41m10s vs. 47 min), the root causes were architectural, not hardware capacity: redundant per-test-file schema teardown/rebuild cycles cost 11 of an 18m40s integration-test stage; Docker layer-cache invalidation (source copied before dependency manifest) cost ~7m25s of a 9m55s Docker build; and stage-scheduling contention produced a 4-minute queue wait despite only 2m50s of actual lint-stage runtime. All fixes were software/configuration only (transaction-rollback test isolation, Dockerfile layer reordering, stage rescheduling) — zero hardware spend — cutting total time from 41m10s to 9m05s, a 78% reduction. Read-location: body of the article, the root-causes list and the before/after timing table. Source: dev.to/yureki_lab, "How I Cut a 41-Minute CI Pipeline to 9 Minutes" — opened and quoted directly via WebFetch.

- **GT-2?** (reported-by-delegate — underlying pages returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch attempt, so this remains a search-summary, not a verified read): Multiple independently published CI-optimization case studies (45→8 min; 22→7 min; 35→5 min) attribute the dominant share of the time reduction to caching (dependency cache, Docker layer cache) rather than to compute capacity; one account attributes roughly 80% of its total speedup to caching changes alone. Phase 3 failure record: sources at medium.com and aws.plainenglish.io returned HTTP 403 Forbidden when a direct read was attempted; only the WebSearch tool's synthesized summary was obtained. Treated as directionally corroborating GT-1, not as independently verified.

- **GT-3** (read-at-source): Research cited via Carnegie Mellon SEI and Dr. Gloria Mark (UC Irvine) finds that a single unplanned context switch can consume up to 20% of a developer's cognitive capacity for the day, and that it takes an average of 23 minutes 15 seconds to fully regain focus after an interruption. Read-location: jellyfish.co, "Mitigating Context Switching in Software Development" — fetched and quoted directly.

- **GT-4** (physical/mathematical law — no external source-read required, per the assumption taxonomy's treatment of physical-law-type ground truths, analogous to citing Carnot efficiency from thermodynamics): Amdahl's Law. For a task with serial (non-parallelizable / non-accelerable) fraction `s` of total duration, the maximum possible speedup obtainable by accelerating the remaining `(1−s)` fraction to an arbitrarily large degree is bounded by `1/s`. Equivalently: no amount of additional or faster hardware applied only to the accelerable portion can push total wall-clock time below `s × (original duration)`. Source: Amdahl, G. (1967), "Validity of the Single Processor Approach to Achieving Large-Scale Computing Capabilities" — standard, uncontested identity.

- **GT-5?** (reported-by-delegate — synthesized from WebSearch across multiple secondary sources, not independently opened at source): Engineering-productivity practitioners commonly cite a target of CI/CD pipeline duration at or under ~10 minutes specifically to limit developer context-switching and blocking; feedback loops around ~20 minutes are commonly reported as the point developers switch to an unrelated task. Not read at source; treated as an industry heuristic, not a peer-reviewed finding.

- **GT-6?** (reported-by-delegate / general industry knowledge, not fetched from a specific pricing page in this session): Cloud/CI runner costs are typically usage-based (per-minute or per-machine-tier billing), so faster or larger runners are a recurring operating cost that scales with the number of jobs run and the fleet size, not a one-time capital cost. Directionally uncontroversial (this is how GitHub Actions, CircleCI, GitLab, and self-hosted-autoscaled runner billing models generally work) but not verified against a specific vendor quote for this company.

- **GT-7** (definitional — a fact about this analysis's evidentiary base, not an external claim, so no citation applies): No company-specific CI stage-level timing breakdown, queue-wait data, flaky-test/retry data, or developer-behavior/commit-batching instrumentation was supplied. The only fact given is the aggregate end-to-end duration (47 minutes) and the qualitative observation that batching has increased. **This is the single fact that caps every confidence rating in this analysis below HIGH.**

```text

→ absent this company's own stage-level data (GT-7), the prior probability that its 47 minutes is similarly architecture-dominated is higher than the prior probability that it is compute-dominated [Assumes: A12]
→ buying faster runners is likely to buy only a small fraction of the theoretically available time savings, because most of the 47 minutes is probably not the kind of time faster hardware fixes [Assumes: A6]
```

### Second-order effects (both lenses walked)

**Actor lens.** *Leadership:* a completed hardware purchase reads as "solved," reducing political urgency to fund the harder instrumentation/architecture work later (effort substitution). *Developers:* if wall-clock only partially drops (e.g., 47→30 min) but stays above the ~10-min band in GT-5?, the threshold research in GT-3/GT-5? predicts batching persists largely unchanged; developers who had informally built local workarounds (local caching scripts, running a subset of tests) may abandon them once "IT bought faster machines," removing bottom-up pressure that might otherwise have surfaced the real bottleneck. *Infra/platform team:* absorbs procurement/integration effort that is opportunity-cost against actually instrumenting the pipeline. *Finance/vendor:* a sunk, recurring cost line becomes an anchor — "we already spent $X on faster runners" — biasing later decisions against admitting the fix should have been architectural (escalation of commitment).

**Time lens.** *Immediate (weeks):* some visible improvement is likely, because some sub-portion of the pipeline probably is genuinely compute-bound (large compiles, big test matrices), so the purchase will not look like a total failure even if it is the wrong primary lever. *Medium (1–2 quarters):* as the fleet/team grows, the same architectural inefficiencies (cache invalidation, serial stage scheduling, queueing) reassert themselves and eat back the gains — this is exactly the pattern GT-1 documents (queue-wait as a *separate*, recurring bottleneck from raw compute). Recurring hardware cost compounds with growth (GT-6?). *Long run (a year+):* if batching behavior did not actually change (per C3), the org has spent a scaling, recurring budget line and still has the original problem, with reduced institutional appetite to fund the real fix ("we already tried that").

Neither lens produced an effect that contradicts a Ground Truth, so no return to Phase 2 was triggered structurally. However, two effects work **against the decision's own success criterion** (durably reducing batching): the leadership moral-hazard effect and the long-run "tried that, didn't work" effect. Both are carried into the pre-mortem (§5) as structural risks, not dropped.

### C5 — Theoretical floor on pipeline time, even with infinite hardware

Direction: lower is better (a duration), so the bound sought is an **ideal floor**, not a ceiling. Governing hard constraints: (1) the pipeline's task-dependency graph has a genuine critical path — some steps must complete before others start (checkout before install, install before build, build before test, test before publish) — a graph-theoretic constraint, not a convention; (2) fixed-latency external calls (a single registry auth handshake, a minimum artifact-transfer time bounded by payload size and network bandwidth) cannot be parallelized away within one run; (3) GT-4 (Amdahl's Law) is the umbrella limit on both.

```text
GT-4 + GT-1 (comparable pipeline floored near 9m05s after removing all convention/redundancy)
→ the ideal floor for a comparably-scoped pipeline is the sum of the critical path's unavoidable serial minimums (checkout, minimum dependency resolution, minimum required build, minimum required test gate, publish), each pushed to its physical or network minimum, which is not zero and not 47 minutes
→ the best-demonstrated tier sits well below this company's current 47-minute conventional figure and well above that hypothetical zero floor
→ the gap from conventional down to best-demonstrated is headroom already proven reachable without new hardware [Assumes: A9]
→ the further gap from best-demonstrated down to the ideal floor is unproven headroom that would require attacking the genuine critical path, and it is the only gap where faster or more-parallel hardware is the correct lever, because only there is the remaining time actually compute or parallelization-bound rather than architectural waste
```

**Pre-check:** head = GT-4 (HIGH) · GT-1 (HIGH) → `?-marked:` none · `lowest cited:` none · `Inputs ceiling:` HIGH.
**Confidence:** MEDIUM, not HIGH — the *identities* (Amdahl's Law, critical-path reasoning) are HIGH-confidence, but sizing the two gaps *for this company* is analogical (borrows GT-1's numbers) rather than measured, which is exactly what caps this at MEDIUM. Rival: it remains possible (not ruled out) that this company's genuine critical path is unusually short and most of its 47 minutes really is compute-bound test execution — see C1's rival, which is the same rival restated here.

**Falsification condition** (recorded here per its Derivation Chain step; the standing falsification-condition sentence itself is recorded once more, verbatim, in the adversarial pass record in §5's process output, never in §6): *the "buy faster runners" hypothesis is false if, after a stage-level timing/queue-wait/flake-rate instrumentation pass, (a) the compute-bound-and-parallelizable fraction of the 47 minutes is small relative to the architectural/serial/queue fraction, or (b) after a hardware upgrade wall-clock duration drops but commit-batching behavior does not measurably change, or (c) queue-wait rather than execution time turns out to be the dominant component, so faster machines simply wait in the same queue.* This is testable **before** spending money: the 1–2 week instrumentation pass in Option C directly measures (a); Option C's phased structure defers hardware spend until (a) is known, which is exactly what makes the hypothesis falsifiable rather than an act of faith.

## 6. Conclusion

**Verdict:** buying faster runners is, on current evidence, the wrong primary lever (C1, C2, C4) — not because hardware is worthless, but because it is very likely aimed at the smaller fraction of the 47 minutes, while leaving the larger fraction and the actual behavioral mechanism untouched.

**Recommended approach:** (C4) Do not commit to a runner upgrade as the first move. Spend one to two weeks instrumenting the existing pipeline — stage-level timing, queue-wait time separate from execution time, flaky-test/retry rate, and (in parallel) a short developer check-in on *why* they batch (duration, fear of blocking, distrust of flaky reruns, or something else). Ship whatever trivially-available caching win exists immediately as a fast, visible first result (this addresses the realistic political risk, named below, that leadership wants to see action inside week one, not week six). Only then buy hardware, and only for whatever fraction of the 47 minutes the data shows is genuinely compute-bound and parallelizable — per C1 and C2, treat that as likely a minority of the total, not the majority, until proven otherwise for this specific pipeline.

**Key insight:** (C1, C2) The leadership framing — "time is the disease, hardware is the cure" — implicitly assumes the 47 minutes is mostly compute-bound. In every comparable, independently documented case examined (GT-1, read at source; GT-2, corroborating but unread at source), the dominant recoverable time in pipelines of this scale was architectural — cache invalidation, redundant per-test setup work, and stage-scheduling/queue contention — not raw compute capacity, and every one of those fixes was software/configuration, at zero recurring cost. Amdahl's Law (GT-4) then does the rest of the work: whatever fraction of the 47 minutes is genuinely serial or I/O-bound is a hard floor no amount of hardware, however fast or parallel, can cut below. If that serial fraction is large — which the comparable cases suggest is the more likely situation, not the less likely one — hardware spend buys a small, bounded improvement for a recurring cost that scales with fleet size.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** (C4, C3) The diagnose-first path is slower to produce a visible executive win and carries real organizational risk: leadership may lose patience and buy the hardware anyway before the two-week instrumentation window closes (this is the dominant failure mode identified in the pre-mortem below). It is also not guaranteed to fix the actual problem even if executed perfectly — C3 shows that "duration" may not be the true behavioral driver of batching at all; if the real driver is fear of blocking teammates or distrust of flaky reruns, then even a well-executed architectural fix that gets the pipeline to 9 minutes may not move batching much, and the instrumentation plan must capture behavioral data, not just timing data, or it will solve the wrong half of the problem.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM overall — the Conclusion's own rating is stated at MEDIUM rather than at C3's LOW ceiling because the headline recommendation (diagnose first) does not depend on C3's specific causal-mechanism claim to be correct; C3 is cited as a trade-off/caveat (informing *what the instrumentation must measure*, not *whether to instrument*), not as a load-bearing input to the recommendation itself — see the Trade-offs-acknowledged line above, which names C3 explicitly for exactly this reason. This is capped below HIGH by three honest facts, not by hedging: (1) GT-7 confirms no company-specific telemetry exists — every quantitative claim about *this* pipeline's composition is a base-rate inference from other companies' published pipelines, not a measurement; (2) two of the seven ground truths (GT-2, GT-5) and one supporting fact (GT-6) are unread-at-source, marked `?`, because the underlying pages 403'd on direct fetch; (3) the evidentiary base itself (blog-post case studies) is plausibly selection-biased toward architecture-dominant stories, since a pipeline that really is compute-bound makes for a less shareable blog post — a genuine weak link named in §5's sensitivity analysis, not papered over.

## The core finding

Your assumption breaks down because **"faster shipping" and "more retained value" are independent axes** — cadence measures *how often you deploy change*, not *how much value you add*. Speed is a proxy, not a cause. The claim only holds when **all seven hidden preconditions hold at once**; fail any one and faster shipping becomes neutral or actively harmful.

## When it breaks (concrete tripwires for your two-week cadence)

1. **Value-per-release is falling** while frequency holds — track feature-adoption per release, not release count.
2. **"Where did X go?" / disruption tickets rise** after releases — change cost is exceeding change value.
3. **Bug or incident rate climbs with cadence** — the most common failure for two-week teams under deadline pressure.
4. **Exit surveys cite price, integrations, or competitors — not "stale product"** — retention isn't product-limited, so shipping speed optimizes the wrong constraint entirely.
5. **Telemetry shows users don't touch what you ship** — changes aren't perceived.
6. **A stability-preferring segment (enterprise/infrequent users) churns faster than power users** — they experience constant change as erosion.
7. **"Stop notifying me" / changelog opt-out rates rise** — notification fatigue.

## The single highest-leverage action

Verify **P4 first**: pull your churn-exit reason data. The load-bearing unknown is whether your retention is even product-change-limited. If churn is actually about price or missing integrations, the faster-ships assumption is **already false today** — no matter how clean your releases are — and the whole lever isn't worth pulling.

For a two-week team specifically, the two preconditions most likely to break first are **P3 (quality buckling under cadence pressure)** and **P4 (retention not actually product-limited)**.

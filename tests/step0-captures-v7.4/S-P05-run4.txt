# Database Trade-Off Analysis: Transactional E-Commerce Backend

**Recommendation: PostgreSQL** (managed via RDS or Aurora Serverless v2) — wins decisively. *Confidence: HIGH*

## Weighted Decision Matrix

| Criterion (weight) | PostgreSQL | MongoDB | DynamoDB |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transactional fit for e-commerce (5) | 5 | 4 | 2 |
| Write throughput @ 500/s peak (4) | 4 | 4 | 5 |
| Cost within $3k/mo (2) | 4 | 3 | 4 |
| Operational simplicity / ecosystem (3) | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| Schema flexibility for evolving catalog (2) | 3 | 5 | 4 |
| Data-model fit (relational orders/inventory) (4) | 5 | 3 | 2 |
| **Weighted total** | **85** | **74** | **66** |

## Why PostgreSQL Wins
The word **"transactional"** is load-bearing. E-commerce orders require atomic multi-entity writes (inventory decrement + order row + payment record). PostgreSQL leads on the two heaviest-weighted criteria — transaction fit and relational data-model fit — which are exactly what the workload demands.

## Cost Is a Near-Tie, Not a Differentiator
A Fermi estimate on DynamoDB write cost showed:
- **At realistic diurnal load** (~185M writes/mo): ~$231–463/mo
- **At sustained 24/7 peak** (1.3B writes/mo): ~$3,250/mo → *breaks budget*

All three fit $3k at realistic load, so cost reduces to a low-weight near-tie. The decision turns on the **transaction model**, not price.

## Why DynamoDB Loses Despite Winning Throughput
Verified ground truths penalize exactly the workload's needs:
- Transactional writes consume **2x WCU**
- Transactions cap at **100 items / 4 MB** per transaction
- Forces single-table design + application-side invariant enforcement

## Key Caveats (unverified inputs worth confirming)
1. **Peak vs. sustained load:** If 500/s is sustained 24/7, switch DynamoDB to *provisioned capacity* for a fair cost comparison (doesn't change the ranking).
2. **Budget scope:** If $3k must also cover app/network/backup, PostgreSQL with HA + replicas is most likely to approach the ceiling.
3. **Choose MongoDB only if** the catalog/order data is genuinely document-shaped with high schema churn — but that contradicts the relational nature of the stated workload.

The result is robust to sensitivity analysis: PostgreSQL's lead (85 vs 74) exceeds 10%, and the only re-weighting that flips it would contradict the established relational nature of the workload.

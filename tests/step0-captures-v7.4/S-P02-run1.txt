## The core finding

"Faster ships → better retention" is **not a causal law — it's a conditional**. Shipping frequency only multiplies the *sign* of per-ship net value (perceived value delivered − cost imposed on the user). If that net value is zero or negative, faster shipping holds retention flat or actively hurts it.

## The three ways the assumption breaks

| Break type | Cause | Detectable symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **Ceiling** | Retention bottlenecked by price/fit/competitor, or you're shipping the wrong things | Retention flat *regardless* of cadence |
| **Inversion** | Each release imposes switching cost, defects, or goes unnoticed | Churn/support **spikes after releases** |
| **Misallocation** | Velocity diverts effort from existing-user pain to new-user features | Existing-base churn rises *while* output rises |

## Tripwires to monitor (declare the assumption broken when...)

1. Post-release churn/support tickets spike in the 1–2 weeks after ships
2. Cohort retention is flat across periods of differing cadence
3. Churn-interview reasons don't map to anything on your roadmap
4. <~50% of releases are ever noticed/surfaced to users
5. Existing-user-pain backlog grows while feature throughput rises

## The single highest-leverage check

**Confirm your churn-interview reasons actually map to your roadmap.** If they don't, your two-week cadence is optimizing a rate that never touches the metric you care about — and the assumption is *already* broken whether you've noticed or not.

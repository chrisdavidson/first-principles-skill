## The headline correction

The intuitive answer — Carnot at the hot-tank temperature, ~64% — is **wrong**, and not by a little. Carnot assumes an infinite reservoir at fixed T. Sensible-heat molten salt is a *finite* reservoir that cools as it discharges, so you must integrate Carnot over the discharge window.

## The actual bound

Using the log-mean temperature of the cooling reservoir:

- Solar salt window: 838 K → 563 K, giving T_lm = **691 K**
- η_max = 1 − 300/691 = **56.6%** (wet-cooled)
- η_max = 1 − 313/691 = **54.7%** (dry-cooled)

Naive Carnot overstates by 7.6 percentage points.

## The gap

| | |
|---|---|
| Law-permitted ceiling | 54.7–56.6% |
| Current practice (supercritical steam Rankine) | ~41–43% gross |
| **Engineering headroom** | **~13–15 points** |
| **Irreducible Second-Law loss** | **~43 points** |

That headroom is exactly the territory sCO₂ Brayton cycles (~50% targets) are chasing — which makes those targets physically legitimate rather than promotional.

## What actually moves the ceiling

Not turbomachinery — higher-temperature media. The 838 K/563 K bounds are materials constraints (nitrate decomposition above ~600 °C; freezing near 238 °C), not laws. Chloride salts at 993 K/773 K lift the ceiling to **65.8%**.

A counterintuitive second-order result: lowering the cold tank *reduces* efficiency (T_c = 400 K → η_max 49.3%). You extract more energy per kg but at lower mean exergy — so efficiency and work-per-kg-of-salt pull in opposite directions, and a plant optimizing storage cost will rationally sit below the ceiling.

**Scope note:** this bounds storage-to-work conversion. Latent/phase-change storage discharges isothermally and *would* justify straight Carnot. Collector-side bounds (Petela, ~93%) and round-trip losses were excluded as outside the question. Confidence: high; weakest link is the sink temperature, which is site-dependent and bracketed rather than resolved.

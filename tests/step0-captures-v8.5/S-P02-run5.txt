## The claim breaks down in three structurally different ways

**1. Speed becomes irrelevant** — when retention is capped by something cadence doesn't touch: acquisition fit, pricing, or an onboarding cliff. Also fires if churners leave *before* your next release lands. This is the expensive failure mode because the team feels productive while the metric flatlines.

**2. Speed becomes unmeasurable** — and this one is likely *already true for you*. Measuring a 30-day retention effect requires a ≥30-day observation window. At biweekly cadence, your releases overlap their own measurement windows. You can't attribute retention movement to any specific ship; you're shipping blind and calling it velocity. (High confidence, conditional on your retention interval being ≥14 days.)

**3. Speed becomes harmful** — when per-ship regression rate or change fatigue outruns per-ship value. More ships is linearly more regression exposure; if value is sublinear, there's a crossover past which speed is net-negative.

## The load-bearing assumption

Every argument for shipping faster assumes **product quality is the binding constraint on retention**. The agent flagged this as the weakest link in the whole chain — if it's false, the claim isn't wrong so much as *irrelevant*.

- **Median time-to-churn vs. 14 days.** If churners leave inside your release interval, cadence is the wrong lever entirely.
- **Churn reasons segmented by cause.** If they cluster on fit or price rather than missing capability or bugs, stop optimizing cadence.

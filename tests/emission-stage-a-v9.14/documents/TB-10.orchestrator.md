Here's the full analysis. The core finding is that the question as posed ("should we move?") is the wrong frame — the real question is what's actually driving the need, and most of the bundled assumptions in it don't survive scrutiny.

## The reframe that matters most

"Caregiving capacity" isn't one thing. It splits into two components with very different properties:
- **Functional/ADL support** (help with tasks, medical coordination) — purchasable. National median for professional in-home care is $35/hr, ~$80k/yr full-time (Genworth/CareScout 2025).
- **Relational presence** — proximity itself is what reduces emotional/relational strain (NAC/AARP national survey data, PMC11692659); money and technology don't substitute for it.

Once you separate these, three of the four assumptions baked into "should we move" collapse:

| Assumption in the framing | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| Moving closer is the only way to help | False — it's the only way to close the *relational* gap specifically; the functional gap is buyable or delegable without relocating |
| Both adults must change jobs at once | A convention, not a requirement — staging job changes (one adult keeps/secures a portable role first) avoids concentrating financial risk. Relocation-driven job change carries real, sometimes-permanent earnings loss for the mover without a soft landing (Venator/Equitable Growth research) |
| Kids' schooling cost is comparable to career disruption | It's execution-dependent, not fixed — ~3 months of learning loss per move on average, but heavily moderated by timing (summer/grade-boundary vs. mid-year) and move count (Education Week/Rumberger) |
| This is a binary, now-or-never decision | False in both directions — advanced-age decline trajectories (frailty, organ failure) are typically *unpredictable in onset* (JAMA, Lunney et al. 2003), so you can't safely wait for a clear signal, but you also can't force a "correct" moment now |

## What's actually load-bearing vs. not

- **Load-bearing:** the relational (not functional) component of proximity; reversibility of each sub-decision (housing is reversible, job seniority and a child's developmental-window position are not); time-sensitivity of the parents' trajectory (drives needing a trigger plan, not a deadline).
- **Load-bearing for *how*, not *whether*:** financial risk of dual job changes, career capital — these argue for staging, not for blocking the move.
- **Real but not decisive:** schooling continuity — a timing/execution constraint, not a veto.
- **Not load-bearing:** social-network disruption — lowest weight in the trade-off scoring, no chain treats it as decisive.

## The actual decision

Running a weighted trade-off across five options (immediate full relocation eliminated outright for failing the continuous-household-income must-have), the ranked result was:

1. **Scale up support in place** (paid care + telehealth + visit cadence) — wins on reversibility, speed, and cost, though it doesn't close the relational gap
2. Parents relocate toward the family — close second, avoids all the family's own disruption, contingent on the parents' willingness
3. Wait-and-monitor with a pre-committed trigger plan
4. Staged relocation (last, but not eliminated)

This ranking is fairly robust — it would take an extreme devaluation of "how fast can we respond when something changes" to flip it.

**So: what should actually drive the decision is not "move or don't move," but a standing process** —
1. Diagnose which need is actually gapped (functional vs. relational).
2. Default to scaling up support in place, since it's cheap, fast, and reversible.
3. Keep "parents move to you" live in parallel if they're open to it.
4. Pre-commit *now* to specific, observable triggers (not a date) that convert the plan to staged relocation — with school changes anchored to a natural grade boundary wherever the trigger allows.
5. If a job change becomes necessary, stage it rather than doing both at once.

**Honest limits:** this lands at medium confidence — it has no path to higher confidence without your specific facts (local caregiver availability/cost, whether the parents would actually accept help, whether either of your jobs is portable). It deliberately doesn't paper over that, because those are exactly the facts that should tip a specific family's answer.

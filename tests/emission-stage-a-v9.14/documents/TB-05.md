# 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Would a national requirement that AI hiring systems publish their training-data sources actually change what covered entities do, and would that change reduce discriminatory hiring outcomes or meaningfully increase accountability/trust — as opposed to producing compliance paperwork that is causally disconnected from either aim?

**Success criteria:**
- The analysis names, for the rule as specified, the most probable *operational/behavioral* response of covered entities, distinguishing genuine from cosmetic compliance, and grounds that prediction in a real precedent rather than intuition.
- The analysis states explicitly whether "publishing training-data sources" is the same causal object as "detecting discriminatory bias," and shows the mechanism (or its absence) connecting the two.
- The analysis tests technical/legal feasibility (what "sources" concretely denotes for a modern AEDT; whether disclosure survives trade-secret challenge) against real evidence, not assertion.
- The analysis reaches a bounded, falsifiable verdict on whether the rule achieves its likely stated aim, with an explicit confidence band and a named condition under which the verdict would be wrong.

# 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** NYC Local Law 144 requires covered employers/agencies to (i) commission an independent bias audit within one year of using an automated employment decision tool, (ii) make a summary of the bias-audit results publicly available, and (iii) give candidates/employees advance notice — source: NYC Dept. of Consumer and Worker Protection, "Automated Employment Decision Tools"; read-at-source: nyc.gov/site/dca/about/automated-employment-decision-tools.page, quoted directly ("information about the bias audit is publicly available," "post a summary of the results of the bias audit," 10-business-day notice).
- **GT-2** EU AI Act Article 10 imposes internal data-governance/documentation duties (design choices, data collection/preparation, bias-mitigation steps, statistical properties) on providers of high-risk AI systems — recruitment/CV-sorting tools are named as high-risk — reviewed by notified bodies/market-surveillance authorities; it does **not** require public publication of training-data sources — source: euaiact.com/article/10; read-at-source: Article 10 text ("Training, validation and testing data sets shall be subject to data governance and management practices appropriate for the intended purpose") plus the page's explicit statement that no public-disclosure duty exists.
- **GT-3** Independent researchers, examining NYC LL144 compliance, could find only 19 employers (of an estimated "thousands" covered) who had publicly posted the required bias-audit summary as of their 2024 study, and note no authoritative denominator exists to compute an exact rate — source: "Auditing Work: Exploring the New York City Algorithmic Bias Audit Regime," arXiv:2402.08101; read-at-source: arxiv.org/html/2402.08101v1, quoted directly ("Of the thousands of employers in NYC, we could find only 19 who have publicly posted audits").
- **GT-4** A California federal court (2026) rejected xAI's trade-secret defense against a state AI-training-data-transparency disclosure law, because xAI's showing was too generalized to establish its own datasets merited protection — not a categorical ruling that training data can never be a trade secret — source: Fisher Phillips LLP case summary; read-at-source: quoted ruling language attributed to Judge Bernal ("X.AI's 'resort to generalizations and hypotheticals about the AI model development industry make it difficult for the Court to find that Plaintiff has carried the heavy burden of showing a [trade secret]'").
- **GT-5?** Under the EEOC's Uniform Guidelines, a selection-rate ratio below four-fifths (80%) for a protected group relative to the highest-scoring group is a rule-of-thumb, non-dispositive trigger for a disparate-impact inquiry — the test operates on selection-rate *outcomes*, not data provenance — cited to: EEOC Uniform Guidelines, 29 CFR 1607.4(D); reported-by-delegate: WebSearch aggregate of secondary legal commentary; primary CFR text not opened this session.

# 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Equate source-disclosure with outcome-based bias auditing as functionally interchangeable transparency tools

**What was tried:** initially treated "publish training-data sources" as a variant of the NYC/EU bias-audit-and-documentation model, assuming both serve the same detection function.

**Why abandoned:** GT-2 (the EU pairs internal documentation with a *separate* conformity assessment, not with public source-publication-as-detector) and GT-6? (proxy/redundant encoding) show the two disclosure objects are causally different; conflating them is a category error the theoretical-limit derivation (chain C3) exposes directly.

**What it ruled out:** any conclusion that source disclosure "does roughly the same job" as an outcome-based bias audit — this path would have produced false confidence that the proposed rule and existing outcome-audit regimes are substitutes.

### Dead End: Low NYC LL144 compliance proves all algorithmic-disclosure mandates are inherently useless

**What was tried:** attempted to generalize directly from GT-3/GT-8?'s near-zero verified compliance to a blanket claim that disclosure regulation as a category cannot work.

**Why abandoned:** over-generalization — LL144 bundles a specific enforcement design (self-report, city agency, public posting, no proactive audit) with a specific disclosure object (completed-audit results); GT-2's EU model, pairing documentation with regulator-held conformity assessment, is architecturally different and not covered by the same evidence. Chain C5 treats enforcement architecture and disclosure object as separable variables rather than collapsing them.

**What it ruled out:** a conclusion that "no version of a disclosure mandate could work," unsupported by the evidence available — what the evidence supports is narrower: *this* object (training-data sources) paired with *this* enforcement architecture (self-report + public posting, unspecified verification) is unlikely to work.

### Dead End: Trade-secret law will categorically block the proposed rule, making the rest of the analysis moot

**What was tried:** considered treating GT-4's litigation risk as dispositive — i.e., the rule would simply be enjoined before it could take effect, so its behavioral/operational effects need not be analyzed.

**Why abandoned:** GT-4 shows the opposite of a categorical block — the court upheld the disclosure law against a *generalized* trade-secret claim, and xAI's defense failed for lack of particularity, not because the claim-type is barred. A generalized "this will get struck down" belief is exactly the kind of untested belief discarded elsewhere in this analysis (A4), and it is not supported by the one ruling located.

**What it ruled out:** skipping the operational/behavioral analysis (chains C1, C4) on the assumption that litigation makes it unnecessary — GT-4 shows litigation is more likely to *narrow and shape* compliance (boilerplate, narrowed source categories) than to void the rule outright, which is exactly what chain C4 models.

# 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Pair — or replace — any training-data-source disclosure requirement with a mandatory outcome-based selection-rate audit, verified through regulator-held access rather than self-report-plus-public-posting alone (chain C5).

**Key insight:** The proposal's implicit theory of change treats "where the training data came from" as if it were the same object as "how discriminatory the system's outcomes are." It is not: the object that carries discriminatory signal is the joint feature–outcome correlation structure a model is fitted on, which a source citation does not expose — which is precisely why the one regime that already requires deep data-provenance documentation (EU AI Act Art. 10) keeps it as regulator-reviewed internal documentation paired with a separate outcome-oriented conformity assessment, not as public source-publication in its own right (chains C2, C3).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** A regulator-verified outcome-audit-plus-documentation regime costs more to build and staff than a source-disclosure mandate and gives the public audited results rather than the underlying provenance documentation itself — trading maximal public visibility for actual verifiability. This trade-off is not knife-edge for the recommendation's main claim (the proposed rule is Pareto-dominated regardless of weighting) but is knife-edge for the specific choice between the two stronger alternatives, where a 2-point cut to the enforceability weight flips the preference (chain C5).

- Operationally and behaviorally, the rule produces a bimodal compliance response — a small genuinely compliant minority and a boilerplate-or-noncompliant majority — plus incremental market consolidation toward large incumbents, not a change in hiring outcomes (chain C1, chain C4).
- On the stated aim, the rule is very unlikely to reduce discriminatory hiring outcomes, because it discloses an object causally disconnected from the outcome regulators care about, and its accountability/trust value is marginal and largely symbolic rather than substantive (chain C2, chain C3).

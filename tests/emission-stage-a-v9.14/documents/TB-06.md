# First-Principles Analysis: Timed Exams vs. Take-Home Assessment Under Widely Available AI Writing Assistants

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given that capable AI writing assistants now let a student produce fluent, well-organized, often high-scoring written work without demonstrating personal understanding, what assessment design — not merely "keep timed exams" or "switch to take-home" — actually serves the university's several, partly conflicting purposes for assessment (certifying individual mastery to third parties, giving formative feedback, motivating learning, ranking/sorting students, protecting accreditation/reputation), and does an unmodified swap from timed written exams to take-home assessment satisfy that test?

**Success criteria:**
1. The Conclusion names, for its recommended design, which of the five stated purposes it serves fully, partially, or not at all.
2. The Conclusion states a concrete integrity/enforcement mechanism for its recommended design and cites that mechanism's *measured or reported* reliability, not an assumed one.
3. The Conclusion directly answers the literal question asked — whether unmodified take-home replacement of timed exams is recommended (yes / no / conditional) — rather than dissolving it into pure hybridism without an answer.
4. The Conclusion states explicit, checkable conditions under which the recommendation would flip.
5. The Conclusion names at least one second-order effect (workload, equity, or institutional-incentive) as an acknowledged trade-off, not a costless win.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** Structured oral assessment formats achieve substantially higher reliability than unstructured oral formats: "structured oral formats achieve high validity and reliability, with Cronbach's α of 0.75–0.80 versus 0.50 for unstructured formats." — source: arxiv.org/html/2603.18221v1; read-at-source: quoted verbatim from the fetched page.
- **GT-2** A single administration of a 25-minute, two-grader human-scored oral exam for a 36-student class requires ≈30 faculty-hours (≈$750); one AI-assisted deployment of the same format was measured at ≈$15 total (≈$0.42/student). — source: arxiv.org/html/2603.18221v1; read-at-source: quoted verbatim ("A 25-minute oral examination for a class of 36 students, evaluated by two graders, requires 30 hours of faculty or teaching assistant time... approximately $750... whereas their AI system cost only $15 total").
- **GT-3** In a controlled field study (Scarfe & Roesch, University of Reading), 33 unedited, non-prompt-engineered ChatGPT-4 answers submitted into live 24-hour take-home psychology exams across five modules went undetected by markers in 94% of cases (96% under a stricter "explicit AI mention" criterion), and scored on average about half a grade classification higher than genuine student submissions. — source: arcticshores.com report of the study; read-at-source: quoted verbatim. *Scope note (not a provenance flag): n=33, single institution/discipline, not independently replicated in this analysis — priced explicitly in C1's confidence line below.*
- **GT-4** Turnitin publishes a claimed AI-detection accuracy of 98%+ with under 1% false positives on documents with >20% AI-generated text; independent reporting found false-positive rates climbing to 5–12% on edge cases (non-native English, heavily-edited drafts, technical prose), a Washington Post test found a 50% false-positive rate on a smaller sample, Turnitin's own checker was reported to miss ≈15% of AI-generated text, and one security researcher reported detectors evadable 80–90% of the time. — source: lawlibguides.sandiego.edu, tryleap.ai; read-at-source: quoted verbatim from both fetched pages.
- **GT-5** Vanderbilt University disabled Turnitin's AI-detection feature in August 2023, citing false-positive risk to students. — source: tryleap.ai; read-at-source: quoted verbatim.
- **GT-6?** AI-detection tools show demonstrated demographic/linguistic bias: one study reportedly found non-native-English (TOEFL) essays flagged at a 61.3% false-positive rate by seven detectors versus near-zero for native-English writing; a 2024 Common Sense Media report reportedly found ≈20% false-positive rate for Black students vs. 10% Latino vs. 7% White. — cited to: primary studies referenced in web-search synthesis; reported-by-delegate: search-engine summary, not opened directly this session.
- **GT-7?** GPT-4 reportedly scores above the passing threshold on all UBE (bar exam) jurisdictions (~297 points on the relevant scale) and achieved 72.5% (passing) accuracy on Japan's National Pharmacist Licensing Examination. — cited to: PMC articles referenced in web-search synthesis; reported-by-delegate — contextual only, feeds no derivation chain.
- **GT-8?** Estimated test-anxiety prevalence varies widely by population/measure: roughly 15–25% of general university students by some estimates, 25–40% by others, and ≈49.8% pooled prevalence specifically among medical students in one systematic review/meta-analysis. — cited to: studies referenced in web-search synthesis; reported-by-delegate — feeds no derivation chain directly; context for GT-9.
- **GT-9?** More methodologically careful, ability-controlled research finds a much smaller test-anxiety effect than the folk narrative assumes: one 2022 high-stakes-examination study reportedly found effect sizes below 0.1 SD once prior ability is controlled for, and a separate 2022 study is reported to conclude "strong evidence against the interference hypothesis of test anxiety." — cited to: tandfonline.com (doi:10.1080/03054985.2022.2079616), journals.sagepub.com (doi:10.1177/09567976221119391); **Phase 3 failure record:** both sources returned HTTP 403 (paywall) on direct fetch attempt this session — source unreachable; feeds only the MEDIUM chain C2 below, consistent with the EXCEPT clause for unreachable sources.
- **GT-10?** Large language models (Claude, ChatGPT, etc.) generate text by statistical next-token pattern completion conditioned on prompt and training data; they are comparatively strong at fluent, well-organized prose and at reproducing well-represented reasoning patterns, and comparatively weak/unreliable without external verification at tasks requiring information outside training data/context, live original observation, or execution-checked computation the model does not actually run. — cited to: Vaswani et al. 2017, "Attention Is All You Need," and the subsequent LLM-reliability/"hallucination" literature; not independently re-opened this session — marked unverified for provenance discipline even though this is well-established technical consensus; feeds no derivation chain (explanatory/mechanism context only, addressing the user's explicit request to name the causal mechanism rather than just "cheating becomes easier").

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: An unsupervised, single-artifact take-home design can no longer distinguish AI-authored from student-authored high-quality submissions at population scale, for prose-heavy tasks

→ a pure take-home design with no additional verification step cannot currently distinguish AI-authored from student-authored high-quality submissions at population scale, for prose-heavy tasks of the kind these studies tested

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Rely on AI-detection software as the primary integrity mechanism

**What was tried:** Treating Turnitin/GPTZero-style AI detectors as an adequate enforcement layer for either take-home or hybrid designs, avoiding the cost and disruption of redesigning assessment format.

**Why abandoned:** Assumption A-4 was tested and discarded — false-positive rates as high as 50% in independent testing, ≈15% false-negative rates, 80–90% evasion success against current detectors, and demonstrated demographic bias directly contradict the belief that these tools are reliable enough to justify institutional action.

**What it ruled out:** Saves future analysts from re-proposing detector-based enforcement as a standalone fix; ruled out directly by GT-4, GT-5, GT-6?.

### Dead End: Treat "replace timed exams with take-home" as a literal binary and simply pick one

**Why tried:** The question as posed frames the decision as a swap between two fixed formats.

**Why abandoned:** Assumption A-11 (false binary) was explicitly discarded — a third design (take-home + structured oral defense) dominates both pure extremes on several weighted criteria in the trade-off collapsed into C5, and neither pure extreme survives the must-have knockout test at high stakes.

**What it ruled out:** Saves future analysts from re-litigating "exam or take-home" as though no middle design existed; ruled out by C3 and C5.

### Dead End: Treat test anxiety as, by itself, a strong independent case for abandoning timed exams

**What was tried:** Using the commonly-cited large test-anxiety effect (0.5–0.8 SD, per informally-synthesized sources, GT-8?) as a stand-alone argument against timed exams, independent of the AI question.

**Why abandoned:** More rigorous, ability-controlled research (GT-9?) reports effect sizes below 0.1 SD once prior ability is controlled, and a separate study is reported to find "strong evidence against the interference hypothesis." This doesn't eliminate the anxiety concern (a real, smaller, meta-analytic correlation of r ≈ −0.23 to −0.33 persists) but rules out treating it as a dominant, freestanding argument.

**What it ruled out:** Saves future analysts from overweighting test anxiety as *the* reason to abandon timed exams; the more defensible case rests on construct validity (A-6b) and the AI-authorship problem (C1), not primarily on anxiety.

### Dead End: Assume the AI-detection-vs-generation arms race will self-resolve in detection's favor

**What was tried:** Assuming (A-8) that improving detection technology would close the gap with generative capability on a short horizon, making the whole authorship question temporary.

**Why abandoned:** Discarded against trend evidence — GT-4's reported 80–90% evasion rate and the documented regression in detectability from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4 both point toward generation capability outpacing detection, not converging with it, on current evidence.

**What it ruled out:** Saves future analysts from treating "wait for better detectors" as a viable holding pattern; the pressure to redesign assessment is not temporary on present trend.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not perform an unmodified swap in either direction. For most disciplines and course sizes, replace the pure take-home essay/problem-set with a take-home artifact paired with a structured, rubric-based oral defense (or equivalent process-tracked verification), funded and trained as a precondition of adoption rather than an afterthought; retain unmodified timed, closed-book exams only for the narrow set of licensure-relevant competencies where instant unaided recall under pressure is itself the real-world target skill; retain unmodified, unsupervised take-home nowhere at high stakes (chain C5).

The hybrid serves certification-validity and formative feedback well (it directly repairs the specific mechanism AI broke — authorship/understanding — rather than reverting to a narrower, less ecologically valid construct), serves motivation adequately (deadlines and live defense both create real stakes), and serves ranking/sorting adequately once reliability-validated rubrics are used; it serves accreditation/reputation protection only conditionally, contingent on funded, consistent implementation across departments — a condition this analysis cannot verify will hold in practice (see the second- and third-order effects in chain C5, and Cluster A of the pre-mortem below).

**Key insight:** The change AI assistants cause is not "cheating becomes easier" in the abstract — it is that AI collapses the correlation between fluent, well-organized written output and actual understanding, which is exactly the correlation take-home assessment was built to exploit as its verification mechanism (chain C1). Timed exams do not "fix" this by being harder to cheat on; they fix it by changing what is measured entirely, back to unaided-recall-under-pressure — a construct most third parties relying on the credential do not actually want measured for most disciplines (chain C2, assumption A-6b).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The hybrid is not a costless fix. It has a real scale/cost boundary (chain C4): human-graded oral defense costs scale roughly linearly with enrollment (≈$750 per 36 students recomputed to ≈$300/hour-equivalent burden at 360), and an AI-assisted grading substitute trades that labor cost for an unvalidated-at-scale reliability question of its own. It also trades one equity problem (unequal AI access under pure take-home) for others that are not smaller by default: language/anxiety disadvantage in live oral formats, and a plausible future market in commercial "viva-prep" coaching that could partially reintroduce socioeconomic advantage (chain C5's second- and third-order extension; pre-mortem Cluster B below).

**Pre-check:** head C5 (MEDIUM) · ?-marked: none · lowest cited: MEDIUM · Inputs ceiling: MEDIUM
**Confidence:** MEDIUM — matches ceiling. The Conclusion rests entirely on C5, itself capped at MEDIUM by its four cited constituent chains (C1–C4), each independently MEDIUM for reasons already named on their own confidence lines and not re-derived here.

This conclusion is false, and should flip, under any of the following, stated as observable conditions rather than vague hedges: if independent, multi-institution replication finds that oral-defense-augmented take-home does *not* meaningfully reduce undetected AI-ghostwriting relative to pure take-home (A-12 empirically refuted, undermining C3 directly); if properly measured equity harms from the oral format (documented disadvantage to non-native speakers and anxious students, plus any emergent coaching-market advantage) are shown to *exceed*, not merely parallel, the AI-access equity harm of pure take-home (A-16 resolved against the hybrid); or, at the institutional-policy level, if a department or course genuinely cannot fund the training and grading-time A-13/A-14 require and has no accreditor-level lever to prevent quiet under-implementation — in which case the honest recommendation for that specific context reverts toward unmodified timed exams despite their own reinstated construct-validity cost, because a poorly funded hybrid that degrades to a rubber-stamp viva (pre-mortem Cluster A) is worse than either pure alternative, not better.

## Summary for the reader

The literal question — replace timed exams with take-home now that AI is available — does not have a clean yes/no answer, and manufacturing one would misreport what the evidence supports. The defensible answer is: **no, not as an unmodified swap in either direction.** Pure take-home, unmodified, is no longer viable at high stakes (a real study found AI answers undetected 94–96% of the time and outscoring genuine work, while detection tools carry false-positive rates as high as 50% and are evadable 80–90% of the time). Pure timed exams "solve" that specific problem by removing tool access, but reinstate a narrower, less realistic construct that most third parties don't actually want measured, at a cost (test anxiety) that turns out to be smaller than commonly claimed but not zero. The evidence-supported design is a funded, trained take-home-plus-structured-oral-defense hybrid, with unmodified timed exams retained only for licensure-relevant unaided-recall skills — rated MEDIUM confidence throughout, with the specific unresolved unknowns (does adaptive oral questioning actually resist AI-assisted rehearsal? does it scale without a new AI-reliability dependency? does its equity cost really beat pure take-home's?) named rather than papered over, and explicit conditions given for when this recommendation should flip.

## Short answer: No, not as framed

"Buy faster runners" treats 47 minutes as one problem when it's structurally at least three different ones — queueing, flakiness/overhead, and genuine serial compute — and only the last one is actually fixed by faster hardware.

**Why the assumption breaks down:**
- Amdahl's Law caps what parallel/faster compute can achieve: wall-clock time can't drop below (1−p)×47min, where p is the parallelizable fraction. Nobody in this scenario has measured p — so the ceiling on "faster runners" is currently unknown, possibly small.
- Real-world precedent cuts the other way: Slack's engineering team cut CI failure rates from 57% to 4% via flaky-test detection — zero new hardware. That's the kind of thing that commonly dominates CI pain, not raw compute.
- Runner pricing (checked against GitHub's actual table) scales almost linearly with size — there's no cliff that makes "buy bigger" cheap relative to what it delivers.

**The deeper problem hardware doesn't touch:** developers are batching to spread the fixed pain of the wait over more work per run. That doesn't reverse with a faster pipeline unless the reduction crosses whatever threshold actually makes running CI per-commit feel "free" — and per DORA's own data, the batching behavior itself is what's increasing blast radius and revert cost. Faster runners might dampen this; there's no strong reason to expect it reverses it.

**Trade-off scoring (6 weighted criteria, wall-clock/cost/effort/root-cause/risk/reversibility) ranked seven options** — faster/bigger individual runners scored *lowest* of the six live alternatives, because it's the only one that scores near-bottom on both root-cause and risk-reduction. The fast-feedback/full-suite pipeline split scored highest, and the ranking is robust (would take cutting the wall-clock weight by more than half to flip it).

**Recommended sequence:**
1. Instrument first — split the 47 minutes into queue-wait, per-job overhead, compute time, and flaky-retry time. This is the missing prerequisite and costs almost nothing.
2. If queueing dominates → add parallel *capacity* (more concurrent runners — a "buy more" move, different from "buy faster").
3. If overhead/flakiness dominates → caching + flaky-test elimination (cheapest, highest-scoring, has direct precedent).
4. In parallel, build a fast pre-merge tier (<10 min) + full suite async — this directly attacks the incentive to batch.
5. Reserve an actual runner-speed upgrade for the one case this can't rule out without data: genuinely serial, compute-bound work.

**Caveat flagged explicitly by the analysis itself:** the weakest link is whether shrinking CI time actually reverses batching at all — it's possible the real driver is review latency, deploy cadence, or per-minute cost chargebacks rather than CI wait time itself. That's worth checking directly (e.g., ask developers why they batch) before assuming any pipeline fix solves it.

**Falsification condition:** if profiling shows queue+serial+flaky time is under ~15% of the 47 minutes, then compute actually is the bottleneck and buying runners would have been correct — but that's a measurement, not an assumption to lead with.

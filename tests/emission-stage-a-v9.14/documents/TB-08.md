# First-Principles Analysis: "New catalyst raises yield from 62% to 71%, measured across three runs"

## Step 1 — Ground truths (what is actually asserted, stripped of interpretation)

Only four facts are given:
1. A yield figure of 62% under some prior condition ("baseline").
2. A yield figure of 71% under some new condition (catalyst added/changed).
3. The comparison rests on **three runs**.
4. The source of the claim is **the lab itself** (self-reported, not independently verified).

Everything else — that the catalyst *caused* the difference, that 62% and 71% are each reliable central estimates, that "yield" means the same thing in both conditions, that nothing else changed between the two sets of runs — is inference, not given fact. The first-principles move is to separate the four hard data points above from the causal story wrapped around them.

## Step 2 — Challenge the embedded assumptions

The sentence smuggles in assumptions that have to be surfaced individually:

- **Definitional stability**: "yield" could mean isolated yield, NMR/GC yield, theoretical vs. actual, crude vs. purified. If the two conditions used different measurement conventions, the numbers aren't comparable regardless of the catalyst.
- **Ceteris paribus**: the claim implies the catalyst was the *only* variable changed. In practice, reagent batch, solvent lot, temperature control, reaction time, operator, instrument calibration, and workup procedure are all plausible co-varying factors ("batch effects," "operator effects").
- **Baseline comparability**: was 62% also averaged over multiple runs, or is it a single historical number, a literature value, or a best-case figure? Comparing a 3-run average to a 1-run (or cherry-picked) baseline is not an apples-to-apples comparison.
- **Independence and randomization**: were the six total runs (three old, three new, or however the baseline was generated) interleaved in time and blinded to the person measuring yield, or run in a block, in sequence, with the "new" condition always run later (confounding catalyst with drift/learning/improving technique)?
- **No selective reporting**: were these the only three runs attempted, or the best three out of some larger, unreported set? A lab motivated to show improvement has an incentive (even unconsciously) to report favorable runs.
- **Statistical adequacy of n=3**: three runs give 2 degrees of freedom. No variance/spread is reported at all — just two point estimates. Without a standard deviation or range, there is no way to know whether a 9-percentage-point gap is a real effect or ordinary run-to-run noise.

## Step 3 — Decompose the claim into its separable sub-claims

The one sentence actually bundles three distinct claims of increasing strength:

1. **Descriptive claim**: "Across three runs, we measured higher yield numbers when using the new catalyst." — an observation.
2. **Causal claim**: "The catalyst caused the increase." — requires isolating the catalyst as the sole changed variable and ruling out confounds.
3. **Generalization claim** (implied, not stated, but usually what such a report is *used* for): "This catalyst will reliably improve yield in future runs / other batches / at scale / in other labs." — requires reproducibility and external validity, neither established by three in-house runs.

Each layer needs different evidence, and the report only supports the first.

## Step 4 — What "three runs" statistically establishes vs. doesn't

**Establishes:**
- A rough point estimate of central tendency for each condition (weak — n=3 is barely above the minimum to compute any spread at all).
- A qualitative signal that the new-catalyst runs did not scatter wildly around 62% or below — some consistency, if the three values were in fact all elevated (not, e.g., one 90% and two 61%'s averaging to "71%").

**Does not establish:**
- Statistical significance — no confidence interval, no p-value, no reported variance are given, and n=3 makes any test severely underpowered.
- That the effect size (9 points) exceeds the reaction's natural run-to-run variability — many real reactions swing more than that from ordinary noise (humidity, aging reagent, minor technique differences).
- Reproducibility beyond this lab, this operator, this specific reagent batch, or this specific day.
- Causality — correlation between "catalyst present" and "higher number" is consistent with a causal effect but equally consistent with any of the confounds in Step 2.

## Step 5 — What would have to be true for the causal claim to actually hold

For "the catalyst caused the yield increase" to be a validated conclusion, at minimum:
- Baseline and new-catalyst runs must be otherwise identical (same reagent lots, same operator or randomized operators, same equipment, same yield-measurement protocol) — i.e., a true controlled comparison, not a before/after historical comparison.
- Run order should be randomized/interleaved (or, ideally, blinded) to rule out drift, learning effects, or seasonal/instrument confounds.
- Sample size sufficient to show the mean difference exceeds measurement and process noise (a reported SD/SEM and a significance test, not just two averages) — three runs per arm is close to the minimum for even a rough estimate, not a demonstration.
- No undisclosed run selection (all attempted runs reported, not a favorable subset).
- Ideally, independent replication — a second operator, a second batch of catalyst, or a second lab — since self-reported, uncontrolled, internal comparisons are exactly the situation most prone to unconscious bias.
- A plausible mechanism for why the catalyst would raise yield, which raises prior credibility but doesn't substitute for the controls above.

None of this is stated to be present or absent — it's simply unknown from the sentence given.

## Step 6 — Reasoning upward to a validated conclusion

Combining the above: the sentence is evidence of an *observed association* between "catalyst used" and "higher measured yield" in a small, internally generated dataset. It is a legitimate first data point worth taking seriously (three consistent-direction measurements is more than zero evidence), but it sits well below the bar needed to certify a causal, generalizable, or statistically robust effect. The honest epistemic status is "promising preliminary signal," not "established improvement."

---

## Final validated conclusion

The lab observed higher average measured yield (71% vs. 62%) when using the new catalyst across three internally run trials. This is a legitimate, worth-following-up preliminary signal that the catalyst *may* improve yield under the specific conditions tested by this lab — but it is not, on this evidence alone, a demonstrated, statistically significant, or causally attributed effect. Treat it as a hypothesis warranting a properly controlled, adequately powered, and ideally independently replicated follow-up experiment (with reported variance, randomized/blinded run order, and matched conditions apart from the catalyst) before acting on it as a settled improvement.

## What should NOT be concluded from this report

- That the catalyst *causes* the yield increase (confounds — reagent batch, operator, instrument drift, workup differences, run order — have not been ruled out).
- That the 9-percentage-point gap is statistically significant (no variance, confidence interval, or significance test is reported; n=3 is underpowered to distinguish a real effect from ordinary run-to-run noise).
- That 71% and 62% are reliable, well-characterized central estimates of what each condition "truly" yields (each is only a 3-run — or possibly fewer-run — sample).
- That the two percentages measure the same thing ("yield" may differ in definition or measurement method between the two conditions).
- That the result will reproduce in other labs, with other reagent lots, at other scales, or by other operators (no external replication).
- That this is the best-performing or most cost-effective catalyst available (no comparison to alternatives, no cost/practicality analysis).
- That there was no selective reporting (the three runs shown may not be the only runs attempted).
- That the effect will hold at production scale or under different reaction conditions (temperature, time, substrate) than those tested.
- That the mechanism by which the catalyst supposedly improves yield is understood or validated.
- That this constitutes independent, peer-reviewed confirmation — it is a self-reported internal result only.

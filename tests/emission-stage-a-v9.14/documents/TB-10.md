## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given that "moving closer" is only one of several levers for increasing an aging parent's care, and that the sub-decisions bundled into a relocation (job change, school change, housing change) differ sharply in reversibility and in how predictably urgent they are, what set of factors should actually determine *whether, when, and how* this family increases caregiving capacity — as opposed to what the framing of "should we move" presupposes must decide it?

**Success criteria** (checkable against §6 without further clarification):
- **SC1:** The Conclusion names, for each of the seven factors listed in the prompt (proximity/caregiving capacity, career capital, financial risk of dual job changes, children's schooling continuity, relationship/social-network disruption, reversibility, time-sensitivity of the aging trajectory), whether it is load-bearing or not, each with a chain citation.
- **SC2:** The Conclusion states explicitly whether the decision must be resolved as one binary commitment now, or can be staged/triggered — and if staged, names at least one concrete, observable trigger condition.
- **SC3:** The Conclusion names at least one caregiving-capacity lever other than relocation that was evaluated before any relocation option was recommended.
- **SC4:** The Conclusion's recommended-approach is a decision *rule/process*, not a single "move" / "don't move" verdict — because this analysis has no access to any specific family's private financial or health facts.

## 3. Ground Truths

**Phase 3 failure record:** Attempted `https://www.terry.uga.edu/wp-content/uploads/venator_jmp_uifortrailingspouses_11192020.pdf` (primary source for the trailing-spouse/UI research) — unreachable, HTTP 403 Forbidden. GT-2 cites the substitute, not the blocked PDF.

- **GT-1** Family caregivers living close to a parent report significantly lower emotional strain than more distant caregivers, while long-distance caregivers show significantly more physical and financial strain (regression coefficients: physical strain b=-0.236, p<.05; financial strain b=-0.228, p<.05; proximity/closeness associated with lower emotional strain, b=-0.171, p<.05) — source: "Proximity, Care Values, and Caregiving Strain: Evidence from the 2020 NAC/AARP National Survey," PMC11692659; read-at-source: Results section, the three quoted regression coefficients.
- **GT-2** Without portable support (e.g., unemployment-insurance eligibility for a relocating partner), a partner who relocates for the other's opportunity "experience[s] earnings losses following a move and never recoup those losses in the jobs they eventually find"; with such support, the gap closes to roughly $4,500–$12,000 higher earnings three years post-move — source: Equitable Growth, "Moving with two careers is hard..." (summarizing Venator's dual-earner migration/UI research); read-at-source: quoted directly from the fetched page.
- **GT-3** Students lose "about three months of reading and math learning each time they switch schools" on average; severity is heavily moderated by whether the move is voluntary/summer-timed (less disruptive, sometimes net-positive) versus involuntary/within-year, and by cumulative move count ("even one move increases the risk of not graduating or getting delayed") — source: Education Week, "Student Mobility: How It Affects Learning" (2016), citing meta-analytic literature and researcher Russell Rumberger; read-at-source: quoted directly.
- **GT-4** Of the four documented end-of-life functional-decline trajectories (sudden death, cancer, organ failure, frailty), only the cancer trajectory has a relatively predictable terminal phase; the frailty and organ-failure trajectories — dominant in advanced age — involve "steadily diminishing reserve capacity to cope with inevitable but unpredictable acute health challenges" and may occur "without a discrete terminal illness phase" — source: Lunney, Lynn, Foley, Lipson, Guralnik, "Patterns of Functional Decline at the End of Life," *JAMA* 2003;289(18):2387–2392; read-at-source: fulltext at jamanetwork.com, quoted directly.
- **GT-5** The 2025 national median hourly rate for non-medical in-home caregiving is $35/hour, with a full-time-equivalent (44 hrs/week) annual cost of $80,080 — source: CareScout/Genworth 2025 Cost of Care Survey press release; read-at-source: quoted directly.
- **GT-6** A household relocation is reversible (the family can move back at a bounded, largely financial cost); job-specific seniority/vested relationship capital and a child's developmental-window-specific social/academic position are not restored by reversing the move — verified via the Five-Whys reduce-to-primitives irreducibility test: housing reversibility bottoms out at the *definition* of "reversible" (prior state restorable by undoing the action); job-capital irreversibility bottoms out at the already-verified GT-2; developmental-window irreversibility bottoms out at the definitional fact that a specific school year and its peer context cannot be re-lived. Not an external citation — a definitional/logical ground truth.
- **GT-7?** At least five channels other than relocation can increase caregiving capacity: professional in-home care (priced per GT-5), care-coordination/case-management services, telehealth/remote monitoring, redistributing load among other family members, and the parents relocating toward the adult children instead — unverified: this is an analytic enumeration, not tied to a single external source; GT-1 and GT-5 independently substantiate two of its items, but the list's completeness and each item's local availability for any specific family are not confirmed here.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: "Moving closer" is the right lever only for the relational-presence gap, not the functional-support gap

GT-1 (proximity reduces relational/emotional strain) + GT-5 (priced professional in-home care) + GT-7? (enumerated non-relocation levers)
→ caregiving capacity for an aging parent is not one undifferentiated need but at least two distinct ones: functional/ADL task support and relational/in-person presence
→ GT-5 and GT-7? show the functional component is purchasable or delegable without relocation, at a known, bounded market price *[Assumes: A-9]*
→ GT-1 shows only physical proximity reliably reduces the relational/emotional-strain component, and this reading presumes the family's post-move contact pattern resembles the surveyed population's *[Assumes: A-13]*
→ relocation is the correct lever only for closing a relational-presence gap, not a functional-support gap, so the framing that "moving closer is the only way to increase caregiving capacity" (A-1) conflates the two and should be discarded

### Conclusion C2: Simultaneous dual job change is a convention, not a requirement, and concentrates avoidable financial risk

GT-2 (relocation-driven job change often produces un-recouped earnings loss)
→ if both adults change jobs at the same time, neither has a proven, stable income in the new location to fall back on if one new role fails to materialize *[Assumes: A-10]*
→ staging the two job changes — one adult securing or keeping a portable/remote-compatible role before the move — preserves a fallback income without reducing the caregiving-capacity gain the move is meant to achieve
→ the assumption that both adults must change jobs simultaneously (A-2) is a convention, not a structural requirement, and staging materially reduces financial risk at little cost to the goal

Rival — "staggering causes damaging household separation" — ruled out by a magnitude comparison against GT-2's multi-year earnings-loss risk; see Abandoned Reasoning §5.

### Conclusion C3: Children's schooling cost is execution-dependent, not a fixed quantity comparable to career disruption

GT-3 (school-move cost ≈3 months/move, severity moderated by timing/count) + GT-6 (reversibility asymmetry)
→ the schooling cost named in GT-3 is not fixed — it is a function of controllable execution choices: moving once, timing it in summer or at a natural grade boundary, and avoiding mid-year or mid-high-school moves
→ GT-6 shows that a poorly-timed move's bad schooling outcome is harder to reverse than the relocation decision itself, which raises the stakes of getting timing right rather than the stakes of whether to move at all
→ treating children's schooling disruption as automatically "comparable" to adults' career disruption (A-3) is false in an actionable way — the real lever is designing when and how often a move happens

### Conclusion C4: The decision is neither forced-now nor safely-deferrable — it is a standing, trigger-based capability question

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Caregiving capacity is a single undifferentiated resource, substitutable by any lever equally
**What was tried:** Treating "increase caregiving capacity" as one fungible quantity, so that paid help, telehealth, or relocation would all count as interchangeable increments toward the same goal.
**Why abandoned:** GT-1's finding is specific — proximity, not support-in-general, is what tracks lower emotional strain. A model that treats all levers as fungible cannot explain why a well-staffed but distant arrangement would still leave a relational gap.
**What it ruled out:** Presenting "just buy more care" as a complete answer to caregiving-capacity questions; it is a complete answer only to the functional half.

### Dead End: Staggering job changes is riskier than simultaneity because it prolongs household separation
**What was tried:** Scoring simultaneous relocation as lower-risk on the theory that a single clean break avoids months of a split household.
**Why abandoned:** The separation cost is real but bounded and temporary (months); GT-2's earnings-loss risk is frequently multi-year and sometimes permanent. The two costs are not the same order of magnitude, so ranking by "avoid any temporary separation" inverted the actual risk comparison.
**What it ruled out:** Treating a clean, simultaneous break as the financially conservative choice; per C2, it is the financially aggressive one.

### Dead End: A single now/never decision date
**What was tried:** Assuming the family must fix a single calendar date by which the relocation question is permanently resolved, either committing or declining.
**Why abandoned:** GT-4 shows both halves of this framing are false — the "decide now" half over-pays for a benefit whose timing is usually not yet needed (most trajectories don't have a knowable near-term terminal phase), and the "we'll wait for a clear sign" half under-prepares (frailty/organ-failure trajectories can arrive without a discrete warning).
**What it ruled out:** Any conclusion of the form "the family should move by [date]" or "the family can safely table this indefinitely" — see chain C4.

### Dead End: GT-5's national median paid-care price transfers directly to the parents' specific location
**What was tried:** Using the $35/hr national median directly as Option C's expected cost without a local check.
**Why abandoned:** The pre-mortem (below, Cluster B) surfaced that local caregiver-market availability and pricing can diverge substantially from national medians, especially in under-served or rural areas — a risk this analysis cannot resolve generically.
**What it ruled out:** Presenting Option C's financial-risk score as unconditionally reliable rather than conditional on a local market check.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Adopt a standing decision *process*, not a single verdict. (1) First diagnose which of the two components of "caregiving capacity" is actually missing — functional/ADL support or relational/in-person presence — because only the second is proven not to be substitutable by money or technology (chain C1). (2) For a family without an already-severe care crisis, default to Option C: scale up paid/professional care, telehealth, and visit cadence, because it wins the weighted trade-off (91 vs. 84 for the runner-up) and is reversible, fast, and inexpensive to start (chain C5). (3) Pursue Option D — parents relocating toward the family — in parallel as a standing, near-equally-scored alternative if the parents are willing, since it avoids the family's own career and school disruption entirely (chain C5). (4) Do not treat "move or don't move" as binary: pre-commit now to named, observable trigger conditions (grounded in GT-4's finding that decline is often abrupt) that convert the plan to staged relocation (Option B), with any resulting school change deliberately anchored to a natural grade-transition boundary wherever the trigger's urgency allows (chains C4, C5). (5) If either adult's job changes, stage rather than synchronize them, to avoid concentrating financial risk (chain C2).

**Load-bearing status of the seven named factors** (chain C5 unless noted): proximity/caregiving capacity — **load-bearing, but only its relational component** (chain C1); reversibility — **load-bearing**, one of the two axes that actually decides the winning option; time-sensitivity of the aging trajectory — **load-bearing**, drives rejection of the binary framing and the requirement for named triggers (chain C4); financial risk of dual job changes — **load-bearing for *how* the move happens, not *whether* it happens** — staging matters, raw cost comparison is closer than assumed (chains C2, C5); career capital — **load-bearing secondary factor**, the main argument against forced simultaneity (chain C2); children's schooling continuity — **not decisive in itself, but a real execution constraint** on timing and frequency (chain C3); relationship/social-network disruption — **not load-bearing** — it carries the lowest criterion weight in the matrix (2 of 21 weight points) and no chain treats it as decisive (chain C5).

**Key insight:** The load-bearing question was never "proximity or not." It is which of the two components of caregiving capacity — functional support (substitutable by money and coordination) or relational presence (not, per GT-1) — is actually the family's real gap. Once that split is made, three of the four framing assumptions named in the prompt (forced simultaneity, comparable schooling cost, a single now-or-never date) turn out to be conventions that collapse under inspection rather than fixed constraints (chains C2, C3, C4), and the fourth ("moving closer is the only way") turns out to conflate the two components of the need (chain C1).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Choosing Option C now accepts an ongoing, likely-rising financial cost (GT-5's 3%/yr trend) and a real, often under-budgeted coordination burden on the couple (adversarial-pass Cluster D, below) as the price of preserving career capital, school stability, and reversibility; it also knowingly *defers rather than solves* the relational-strain gap GT-1 identifies, pending either the parents' own relocation or a future triggered move (chain C5) — no chain — flagged assumption only insofar as the size of that deferred cost depends on A-9/A-13, which are unverified for any specific family.

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** A mid-sized city has fixed a numeric target (20% fewer commuter car journeys in 3 years) and must choose among three interventions that change three *different* inputs to a commuter's mode-choice calculus (the price of driving, the price of transit, and the safety/comfort cost of cycling) — so the real question is not "which lever is best" in the abstract, but **which checkable, city-specific facts determine which lever's causal mechanism can actually operate, and what conditions flip the winner from one option to another.**

**Success criteria:**
1. The framework names, for each of the three levers, its causal mechanism and the precondition that mechanism requires — checkable against Derivation Chains C1–C3.
2. The framework identifies at least five checkable city-level facts that shift which lever wins the trade-off — checkable against the Conclusion's Recommended-approach list and chains C6–C9.
3. The framework states, for each option, at least one condition under which it wins and one under which it is dominated — checkable against chains C7, C8, C9 and the C6 scoring.
4. The framework states whether a single lever can plausibly reach the 20%-in-3-years magnitude, and what follows if not — checkable against chain C4 and the Conclusion's bundling instruction.
5. The framework names at least one hidden/second-order cost for each option rather than treating any as costless — checkable against C1's and C2's second-order extensions and C3's ceiling limitation.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Trade-off procedure applied (Phase 4 — multiple viable options pull in different directions)

*(Procedure opened and read from `references/trade-off.md` before application.)*

**Options:** A — Free public transit (fare removal); B — Congestion pricing (cordon charge); C — Protected cycle-lane network; D — Status quo (always included per the procedure).

**Must-haves:** No blanket must-have universally eliminates A, B, or C for an unnamed generic city — feasibility gates (legal authority for B, spare transit capacity for A/B, trip-length/density fit for C) are city-specific preconditions, treated as scored criteria and moderators below rather than knockouts. **One must-have does apply to D**: the city has already adopted the 20% target as a fixed constraint of this decision, not a free variable — an option embedding no mechanism for approaching that target is not a live choice regardless of its score elsewhere. D is scored below for transparency (it is the clearest demonstration in this analysis of why "Impact" cannot be treated as merely one weighted criterion among several) and then knocked out.

**Criteria & Weights:**

| Criterion | Weight | 1 means… | 5 means… |
|---|---|---|---|
| Impact (3-yr mode-shift magnitude) | 5 | negligible measured effect on commuter car journeys | double-digit-percent cut in the targeted trip population, demonstrated elsewhere |
| Cost (net 3-yr fiscal cost) | 4 | large uncapped recurring operating subsidy required indefinitely | net revenue-positive, or one-time capital fully amortized |
| Speed (time to effective launch) | 3 | multi-year legal/construction lead time likely consumes the window | substantially live within one budget/construction cycle |
| Equity (low-income commuter impact) | 3 | regressive burden, no offsetting mobility gain | clear net mobility/cost gain concentrated among lower-income commuters |
| Feasibility (political/public adoption) | 4 | referendum/litigation-prone new charge, high organized-opposition risk | broadly popular, no new charge on voters |
| Durability (resistance to rebound) | 3 | documented erosion/reversal within a decade | effect self-reinforcing or self-funding, holding or growing over time |
| Capacity dependency (needs another system's spare capacity) | 2 | mechanism fails without a concurrent capacity build-out elsewhere | delivers value on its own, no added capacity required elsewhere |

Total weight budget: 24.

**Scoring:**

| Option | Impact ×5 | Cost ×4 | Speed ×3 | Equity ×3 | Feasibility ×4 | Durability ×3 | Capacity dep. ×2 | **Total** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| D (status quo) | 1→5 | 5→20 | 5→15 | 3→9 | 5→20 | 3→9 | 5→10 | **88 (knocked out)** |
| A (free transit) | 2→10 (GT-4) | 1→4 (GT-14) | 4→12 | 3→9 (GT-3/GT-4, mixed) | 5→20 | 1→3 (GT-4) | 2→4 (C2) | **62** |
| B (pricing) | 5→25 (GT-1/GT-2) | 5→20 (GT-15?) | 2→6 (GT-2 context) | 2→6 | 2→8 (GT-1/GT-2 context) | 5→15 (GT-1) | 2→4 (C1) | **84** |
| C (cycle lanes) | 3→15 (GT-6/GT-10) | 4→16 | 3→9 | 3→9 | 3→12 | 4→12 | 4→8 (GT-13) | **81** |

**Recommendation:** D is knocked out per its must-have. Among live options: **B (84) > C (81) > A (62)**. B's lead over C is a narrow 3 points; A trails both by a wide margin.

**Recompute check:** B's advantage = Impact(+10)+Cost(+4)+Durability(+3) = +17; C's advantage = Speed(+3)+Equity(+3)+Feasibility(+4)+Capacity(+4) = +14; net +3, matching 84−81. ✓

**Flip test:** the smallest single-weight change that flips B↔C is **Δ=2** on either Capacity-dependency (2→4) or Impact (5→3) — both produce a 1-point win for C. No Δ=1 change closes the 3-point gap. This is real but narrow, not robust. A vs. B/C: no single-criterion weight change within the 1–5 bound flips A over either rival (gap to C is 19 points) — **A is robustly dominated under this generic weighting.**

### Conclusion C1: Congestion pricing's mode-shift mechanism is conditional on pre-existing alternative-mode capacity

GT-11 (definitional: price-only instrument) + GT-1 (Stockholm 20%→~50%) + GT-2 (NYC 11% year one)
→ raising the priced cost of driving into the zone shifts marginal drivers toward whichever available mode then carries the lowest generalized cost for their trip
→ both cities that produced large, sustained reductions already had high-capacity rail transit serving the priced corridors before the charge began, giving diverted trips somewhere with spare capacity to land
→ pricing's demonstrated mode-shift magnitude is conditional on pre-existing high-capacity alternative-mode service in the priced corridors, not a property of the price mechanism alone *[Assumes: A-10]*
→[2nd] dedicated toll revenue gives transit agencies and elected officials an ongoing institutional stake in defending the charge once live, which helps explain why both charges proved durable despite pre-adoption opposition
→[2nd] land uses near a durably priced zone shift toward less car-oriented development over multi-year horizons, reinforcing rather than eroding the initial mode shift

### Conclusion C2: Free transit's mode-shift ceiling is capacity-bounded, and its cost structure is a permanent fiscal exposure with documented crowding/cannibalization risk

GT-12 (definitional) + GT-4 (Tallinn car-share rose) + GT-14 (farebox recovery ~15–25%) + GT-5? (Tallinn year-one detail)
→ removing fares lowers only transit's monetary cost, leaving frequency, coverage, and crowding unchanged in the short run, so its mode-shift ceiling is bounded by existing spare seat-capacity on the routes and times a car-commuter would actually use
→ in Tallinn the riders best positioned to respond to a zero fare were pedestrians and already transit-dependent residents rather than car owners, whose generalized cost of driving includes large non-fare components a fare cut does not touch *[Assumes: A-9]*
→ across the following decade Tallinn's car-commute share rose rather than fell, showing a fare-only lever does not durably suppress driving even where transit is nominally free
→ the recurring fare revenue lost must be replaced every year the policy runs, a permanent annual fiscal exposure the other two levers do not carry in the same form
→[2nd] transit management facing that permanent hole either cuts service elsewhere or competes annually for general-fund dollars against police, schools, and roads
→[2nd] new fare-free ridership crowds already-loaded routes and times, raising the comfort/reliability cost for exactly the car-commuters the policy hoped to attract
→[3rd] some car-commuters, meeting a more crowded "free" bus rather than a faster or more frequent one, stay with or revert to driving — consistent with GT-4's decade-long car-share rise
→[2nd] once free transit has been the baseline long enough to be assumed, re-introducing fares later to close a budget gap triggers a larger political and ridership shock than never removing them

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Locking one weighting for "the" mid-sized city
**What was tried:** Presenting the trade-off (C6) as if its 84/81/62 ordering were *the* answer for any mid-sized city.
**Why abandoned:** The brief explicitly required a framework, not a single prescription; separately, the D=88 result showed a naive weighting without a stated objective produces a degenerate, uninformative answer ("do nothing" wins on points).
**What it ruled out:** Any chain presenting C6 as a standalone recommendation rather than as an input to the conditional chains C7–C9.

### Dead End: Treating the 20% figures in GT-1, GT-4, and GT-6 as directly comparable
**What was tried:** Ranking the three levers by simply comparing their headline percentages (11–20% vs. ~5% vs. a 1.8×/4.3× multiplier).
**Why abandoned:** These are different units measuring different populations (zone vehicle-entries, citywide mode share, block-level relative multiplier) — see chain C5.
**What it ruled out:** Any direct numeric ranking of the three interventions on a single scale without first fixing what the city's own target metric actually is.

### Dead End: Applying theoretical-limit reasoning to derive a physical ceiling on achievable mode shift
**What was tried:** Considering whether a Carnot-style "governing hard constraint" could bound achievable car-trip reduction, the way thermodynamics bounds engine efficiency.
**Why abandoned:** No such hard constraint exists here; the closest candidate (GT-10's short-trip-share distribution) is itself a soft, city-specific empirical fact already used directly as a ground truth, not a fundamental limit requiring constraint-relaxation derivation.
**What it ruled out:** Manufacturing false rigor around a number (a "theoretical ceiling on mode shift") that the framework already handles honestly as an empirical moderator.

### Dead End: Scoring congestion pricing's Feasibility high because it is economically "correct"
**What was tried:** Rating pricing's political feasibility on the strength of its economic elegance (it prices an externality directly, so "it should be popular").
**Why abandoned:** This is reasoning by analogy to economic theory, not evidence; the empirical record (Stockholm's referendum, NYC's multi-year litigation) shows pricing is the *hardest* of the three to adopt regardless of its theoretical merit.
**What it ruled out:** Using theoretical/normative economic argument as a substitute for the cited adoption-history evidence in C6's Feasibility score.

## 6. Conclusion

No single city was specified, so this conclusion is a decision framework rather than a single prescription:

**Recommended approach:** Do not pick one lever from intuition or popularity; gate and weight the choice using five checkable city facts, then plan to bundle the remaining levers into the same 3-year window (chain C10):
- Trip-length and density profile: short-trip-dominant, dense cities raise cycle lanes' ceiling and lower congestion pricing's precondition risk (chain C3, chain C7).
- Pre-existing alternative-mode capacity: cities with spare high-capacity transit in the would-be-priced corridors make pricing's demonstrated mode-shift achievable; cities without it should expect trip suppression rather than mode shift (chain C1, chain C7).
- Legal/political authorization status: cities that already hold cordon-charge authority without a mandatory referendum can compress pricing's multi-year adoption lag (chain C8).
- Capital-versus-operating budget posture: capital-frozen, operating-flexible budgets favor free transit's cash-flow profile despite its weak car-shift record; capital-available, operating-constrained budgets favor pricing's self-funding structure or cycle lanes' low recurring cost (chain C2, chain C6).
- True underlying objective: a car-trip-reduction goal dominates toward pricing or cycle lanes; a mobility-access goal for car-less and low-income residents makes free transit the right tool despite its worst car-mode-shift score (chain C9).

**Key insight:** The same "20%" figure is not one target — it is three different quantities depending on which lever is used to pursue it (chain C5), and a naive multi-criteria comparison that treats "Impact" as merely one weighted criterion among several — rather than as a gate tied to the city's own already-fixed goal — lets "do nothing" win on points (chain C6). Reasoning by convention (just pick the cheapest or the most popular lever) would not surface this.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Free transit trades a large, permanent, recurring fiscal exposure and documented crowding/cannibalization risk for political ease of adoption and genuine access gains for the already-transit-dependent (chain C2). Congestion pricing trades the hardest pre-adoption political fight and a dependency on pre-existing transit capacity for the strongest, most durable, self-funding effect (chain C1, chain C7, chain C8). Protected cycle lanes trade a hard geographic ceiling (they cannot help a sprawling, long-commute city) for low recurring cost and no dependency on another system's spare capacity (chain C3). No lever is free of a structural cost; the framework makes these trade-offs explicit and conditions them on checkable city facts rather than eliminating them.

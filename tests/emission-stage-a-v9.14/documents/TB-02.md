## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Is "reduce CI wall-clock time by purchasing faster/bigger compute" the correct fix for the actual cost the organization is incurring — developers batching changes to avoid a 47-minute, single-tier CI pipeline — or does the proposal misdiagnose which of several independent levers (raw compute speed, parallel capacity, pipeline structure, flakiness, merge/branch-protection policy) is actually binding?

**Theoretical-limit reframe (Phase 1):** 47 minutes is a **convention**, not a physical constant — it is the emergent product of this org's specific pipeline architecture (sharding, caching, test ordering, branch-protection gating), not a hard floor imposed by the work itself. That reframing matters: the question is not "how do we shrink a number" but "which architectural choices produced this number, and which of them are removable without spending on hardware."

**Success criteria** (checkable against the Conclusion):
1. States whether CI wall-clock time is shown, or only assumed, to be the variable actually driving the batching behavior.
2. Derives a ceiling on how much of the 47 minutes faster/bigger hardware can plausibly remove, and names what that ceiling depends on.
3. Compares the cost and expected effect of "faster runners" against at least one concrete architectural/process alternative, using an explicit weighted method.
4. States a mechanism-level answer to whether faster runners reverses, or merely dampens, the batching behavior.
5. Reaches an explicit yes/no/conditional verdict, and names the actual root-cause fix.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** Amdahl's Law: the maximum speedup obtainable by parallelizing a fraction *p* of a workload across unlimited processors is bounded by 1/(1−*p*); equivalently, wall-clock time cannot fall below (1−*p*)×current-time no matter how much parallel compute is added — source: Amdahl (1967), standard CS identity; provenance: **derived directly** (mathematical identity, not a source-read case).
- **GT-2** GitHub Actions Linux x64 hosted-runner pricing: 2-core $0.006/min, 4-core $0.012/min, 8-core $0.022/min, 16-core $0.042/min, 32-core $0.082/min, 64-core $0.162/min, 96-core $0.252/min — implying a roughly constant $0.0025–0.003 per vCPU-minute across the entire published range (at most a ~15% volume discount at the top end) — source: [GitHub Actions runner pricing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/reference/actions-runner-pricing); provenance: **read-at-source** (full table fetched directly).
- **GT-3** DORA's own guidance: "Smaller changes are easier to rationalize and to move through the delivery process. Smaller changes are also easier to recover from if there's a failure," and reduced batch size "contributes to both change throughput and change stability" — source: [DORA, "The Four Keys"](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics-four-keys/); provenance: **read-at-source** (quoted passage confirmed).
- **GT-4** Slack's engineering team measured CI test-job failure attributable to flaky tests drop from 56.76% (Jul 27, 2020) to 3.85% (Feb 22, 2021) after building automated flaky-test detection/suppression — no new hardware — saving 553 engineer-hours of triage and raising main-branch stability from 19.82% to 96% — source: [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/handling-flaky-tests-at-scale-auto-detection-suppression/); provenance: **read-at-source** (figures quoted directly).
- **GT-5?** Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008, CHI), "The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress" — measured finding: brief (2-minute-cadence) office-task interruptions did **not** increase objective completion time but significantly raised self-reported stress, frustration, time pressure and effort (p<.05–p<.001) — source: [ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf); provenance: **read-at-source**, but flagged `?` because its lab paradigm (2-minute interruptions) does not test a multi-hour, once-per-push CI delay — used only as weak supporting texture, never load-bearing.
- **GT-6** Definitional decomposition (five-whys reduce-to-primitives): Total CI wall-clock = Queue_wait + Σ over sequential pipeline stages of [max(parallel-shard compute time in that stage) + fixed per-job overhead + retry overhead]. True by construction; provenance: **derived directly**.
- **GT-7?** For this scenario, the actual magnitudes of Queue_wait, per-stage compute time, fixed overhead, and retry overhead — in particular the parallelizable/compute-bound fraction *p* of GT-1/GT-6 — are **not given or measured**. Unverified: no pipeline-timing data exists for this generic scenario; this is precisely the missing measurement the Conclusion calls for.

**Provenance summary:** `?`-marked: GT-5, GT-7 (2 of 7). Read-at-source: GT-2 — full per-vCPU-tier table, docs.github.com/en/billing/reference/actions-runner-pricing; GT-3 — quoted passage, dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics-four-keys/; GT-4 — quoted figures, slack.engineering/handling-flaky-tests-at-scale-auto-detection-suppression/; GT-1, GT-6 — derived directly (no source-read applicable).

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: The achievable wall-clock reduction from "faster runners" has an unmeasured, possibly small, ceiling

GT-1 (Amdahl's Law) + GT-7? (this pipeline's parallel fraction is unmeasured)
→ "faster runners" bundles two distinct levers — more parallel capacity for shardable work, and higher per-operation speed (clock/IPC/disk/network) for both parallel and serial work
→ Amdahl's Law caps the horizontal lever alone: wall-clock cannot fall below (1−p) times the current total regardless of how much parallel compute is added, so an unmeasured p leaves that lever's ceiling unmeasured too
→ the vertical lever is not capped by p in the same way, but a routine hardware-generation speed bump is typically incremental rather than multiplicative [Assumes: A14 — incremental, not multiplicative, generational speedups; if false, the case for measuring first only strengthens, so this hop's failure does not overturn the endpoint]
→ because GT-7 is unmeasured, nobody can currently say which lever dominates the 47 minutes or how close either lever's ceiling sits to today's figure

**Confidence:** MEDIUM — GT-7? is unverified; verified by instrumenting the pipeline to measure queue time, per-stage compute time, and retry time (this is the analysis's own headline recommendation). A14 is unverified but its failure does not change this chain's endpoint (see hop 3), so it does not further downgrade this chain.

### Conclusion C2: Spending on faster/bigger runners before profiling is a bet with an unknown, possibly poor, payoff — and this holds across a wide bracket of the unknown

GT-2 (near-linear $/vCPU-min pricing) + C1 (MEDIUM — ceiling unmeasured)
→ bracketing the unmeasured parallel fraction from a conservative 30% to an optimistic 85%, Amdahl's floor (1−p)×47min ranges from about 33 minutes down to about 7 minutes — i.e., a plausible saving anywhere from roughly 14 to 40 minutes
→ GT-2's real pricing shows cost scales at a roughly constant $0.0025–0.003 per vCPU-minute across the whole published range, so there is no volume discount that makes buying more/bigger compute cheap relative to what it delivers

→ the two ends of the bracket do not disagree on what to do next: at the low end the payoff is small, and at the high end the payoff is capped by a serial/overhead remainder hardware speed cannot touch by definition — both ends recommend measuring before buying

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: "Amdahl's Law doesn't bind because cloud parallelism is elastic"

**What was tried:** Considered whether unlimited on-demand shard count removes the sequential-fraction ceiling entirely, making "just buy/spin up more" a way to make the ceiling disappear rather than merely approach it.

**Why abandoned:** GT-1's sequential fraction is structural — fixed per-job overhead, cross-stage dependencies, single-threaded steps — not resource-constrained; no amount of elastic capacity changes a term that is defined as *not parallelizable*. Contradicts GT-1's own definition.

**What it ruled out:** Treating elastic/cloud parallelism as an escape hatch from C1's ceiling argument.

### Dead End: the popular "23 minutes to resume a task" interruption-cost statistic

**What was tried:** Intended to cite the widely-repeated claim that a single work interruption costs ~23 minutes of resumption time, to quantify the cost of delayed CI feedback as a load-bearing ground truth.

**Why abandoned:** Fetched and read the primary source most commonly cited for this number (Mark, Gudith & Klocke, 2008, CHI, "The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress") directly. The paper's own measured result is different from — and in the completion-time dimension, opposite to — the popular claim: interrupted work was completed *faster*, not in 23 more minutes, though at a significant cost in self-reported stress/frustration/effort. No "23 minutes 15 seconds" resumption-time figure appears anywhere in the paper. This is a Phase 3 failure record: **citation does not support the claim**.

**What it ruled out:** Using a precise, popularly-cited numeric magnitude for "cost of delayed feedback." The analysis instead carries only the narrower, source-verified GT-5? (stress/effort cost, explicitly flagged as extrapolated beyond the paper's own 2-minute-interruption paradigm) and builds no load-bearing chain on it.

### Dead End: manufacturing a specific "typical" queue/compute/flaky percentage breakdown of the 47 minutes

**What was tried:** Looked for an authoritative, generalizable published split (e.g., "X% queue, Y% compute, Z% flaky retries") to use as a point estimate instead of a bracket.

**Why abandoned:** No such universal breakdown exists — GT-6 shows the composition is pipeline-specific by construction, not a population-level constant. Fabricating a specific split for this org without measurement would present an assumption as a ground truth, violating D-07.

**What it ruled out:** A false-precision single number in place of C2's honest bracket.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not buy faster/bigger individual CI runners as the first move. First, instrument the pipeline to split the 47 minutes into queue-wait, per-job fixed overhead, compute time, and flaky-retry time (chain C5) — this is cheaper and faster than any other option considered and is the prerequisite every other decision depends on. Then:
- If queueing dominates, add parallel runner *capacity* (more concurrent slots), which is orthogonal to C1's per-job-speed ceiling and not what this analysis challenges (chain C5).
- If fixed overhead or flakiness dominates, apply caching and flaky-test elimination — the highest-cost-efficiency, fastest-to-ship options in the matrix, with a real-world precedent (chain C4, GT-4).
- In parallel, build a fast-feedback/full-suite split so small changes get a sub-10-minute pre-merge signal, which is the top-scored option overall and the one most directly aimed at the incentive that makes batching individually rational (chain C4).
- Reserve a faster/bigger individual runner purchase for the specific, currently unverified case where profiling shows the bottleneck is genuinely serial, single-job compute (chain C1).

**Key insight:** "Faster runners" is not one lever but two — more parallel capacity (bounded by Amdahl's Law at an unmeasured ceiling, chain C1) and faster per-operation hardware (uncapped by Amdahl but typically only incrementally faster) — and leadership's proposal, together with the trade-off matrix, treats a queueing problem, a flakiness problem, and a genuine compute-bound problem as if they were the same problem with the same fix (chain C5; chain C4).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Instrumenting first costs a small amount of calendar time before any relief is visible, and the fast/full split requires touching branch-protection policy, which the pre-mortem identifies as the change most likely to meet organizational resistance (chain C4). This recommendation also does not guarantee the batching behavior itself reverses — chain C3 is explicitly the weakest link in this analysis: even a well-executed fix may only dampen, not reverse, batching if the real driver turns out to be review latency, deploy cadence, or chargeback incentives rather than CI wait time (assumptions A11–A13) — no chain — flagged assumption only for the reversal-specific risk beyond what C3 already states.

**Pre-check:** head C5 (MEDIUM), C3 (LOW), GT-7? · ?-marked: GT-7? · lowest cited: LOW · Inputs ceiling: LOW
**Confidence:** LOW — the architecture/cost-sequencing half of this recommendation (C5) is solidly MEDIUM, but the overall banner is capped by C3 (LOW): whether the fix also reverses the batching behavior specifically depends on the unverified threshold assumption A9, and the whole recommendation ultimately rests on GT-7?, the pipeline-timing data this analysis does not have. This closes to MEDIUM once GT-7 is measured (pipeline profiling) and A9 is tested (a before/after read on merge frequency and batch size against a deliberate, measured pipeline-speed change).

8. Sharding surfaces latent shared-state test dependencies as new flakiness, and the org blames the architecture change and reverts it.
9. Queue-wait doesn't improve because runner capacity is shared/fixed; sharding into more, smaller jobs just means more jobs queueing, until concurrency (not speed) is also increased.
10. No before/after measurement of merge-batch size, CI wall time, or revert rate is set up, so six months later leadership cannot tell whether anything worked and concludes "we should have just bought the runners" — repeating the original mistake with worse information.

**Falsification.** This conclusion is false if a direct pipeline-timing breakdown of this org's actual CI shows queue-wait + sequential-stage + flaky-retry time is under roughly 15% of the 47 minutes (i.e., *p* ≥ 0.85 and the remaining serial floor is already near its practical minimum) — in that case additional parallel or faster compute directly attacks the dominant term, and buying runners first would likely have been correct.

## Bottom line

**No — not as leadership has framed it.** "Buy faster runners" answers a symptom ("47 minutes feels slow") without first measuring which of at least three structurally different problems — queueing, flakiness/overhead, or genuine serial compute — actually produces that number, and Amdahl's Law means hardware can only ever address the third. Real-world precedent (Slack, GT-4) shows the dominant CI pain in comparable pipelines is frequently non-compute and fixable at near-zero marginal cost. The trade-off analysis scores the hardware option lowest of six live alternatives specifically because it doesn't touch the incentive that makes batching individually rational. **The actual root-cause fix is: instrument first, then split into a fast pre-merge tier plus caching/de-flaking (and, if queueing is confirmed, more parallel capacity — a "buy more," not "buy faster," move) — with an individual-runner-speed upgrade reserved for the one case this analysis cannot currently rule in or out.**

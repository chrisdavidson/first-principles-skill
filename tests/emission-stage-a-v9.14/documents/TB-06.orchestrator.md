Here's the analysis, reasoned from the ground up:

**Short answer: no, not as an unmodified swap in either direction. The defensible position is a hybrid — take-home work plus a structured oral defense — not a wholesale replacement of exams.**

**Why "replace" is the wrong frame**

Exams and take-home assessments serve several purposes that don't all point the same way: certifying individual mastery to third parties (employers, licensing bodies), giving students formative feedback, motivating learning, sorting/ranking, and protecting the institution's accreditation. A policy that's right for "feedback and learning" can be wrong for "certifying that *this specific person* can do *this specific thing* unaided," which is exactly what licensure-track fields (nursing boards, bar exams) still need.

**What actually changed**

The key insight: capable AI assistants didn't just make cheating easier — they broke the correlation between *fluent, well-argued prose* and *verified individual understanding*, which take-home assessment was quietly relying on as a proxy. A real study found AI-generated answers went undetected 94–96% of the time and outscored genuine student work, while AI-detection tools have false-positive rates up to ~50% and are evadable 80–90% of the time. That means the enforcement mechanisms typically proposed to save take-home assessment (detectors, honor codes) don't actually hold up at high stakes.

**Why pure timed exams aren't the fix either**

Reverting to unmodified timed exams "solves" the AI-authorship problem by removing tool access, but reinstates a narrower construct — performance-under-pressure, unaided recall — that most downstream stakeholders don't actually want measured, and it doesn't eliminate the older, independent failure modes of timed exams (test anxiety effects are real but smaller than commonly claimed, not zero).

**The recommended design**

- Take-home work, submitted normally.
- A funded, trained, structured oral defense on that work — adaptive questioning is much harder to game with AI-generated rehearsal than a static submission.
- Unmodified timed exams retained *only* where the actual goal is certifying unaided-recall skill for licensure purposes.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** This isn't hedging for its own sake — the analysis explicitly flags what would flip it: whether adaptive oral questioning actually resists AI-assisted rehearsal at scale, whether it scales without creating a new dependency on AI-based grading/evaluation tools (trading one reliability problem for another), and whether its equity costs (oral exams can disadvantage some students) actually beat pure take-home's.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** the hybrid is not costless — it adds faculty time for oral defenses and introduces new equity considerations. It's the best-supported option given what's currently knowable, not a clean win.

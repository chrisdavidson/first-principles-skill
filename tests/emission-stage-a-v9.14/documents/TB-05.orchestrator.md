Here's the analysis. Bottom line first, then the reasoning behind it.

## Verdict: the rule would produce paperwork, not fairness — MEDIUM confidence

**What would actually follow operationally:** Based on the closest real precedent (NYC Local Law 144, which already requires bias-audit disclosure with real per-day penalties), you should expect **bimodal, mostly-cosmetic compliance**. A study of LL144 found only 19 of an estimated several thousand covered employers had actually posted the required disclosure publicly. A national training-data-source mandate built on the same self-report-plus-posting architecture would likely reproduce that pattern: a small genuinely compliant minority, a majority filing generic boilerplate ("web text," "licensed HR data," "standard embeddings") that's true but uninformative, and — over a few compliance cycles — that boilerplate hardening into an industry-standard template. A secondary effect: compliance and litigation overhead (data-provenance disclosure has already drawn trade-secret litigation, per a 2026 California ruling against xAI) would land harder on smaller vendors than incumbents, nudging the market toward consolidation with no fairness payoff attached.

**Would it achieve the stated aim? No — it audits the wrong object.** This is the core finding. Discriminatory hiring outcomes come from the *joint distribution of features and outcome labels a model is fitted on* — things like proxy variables (zip code, name, employment gaps) that statistically encode protected-class membership even with sensitive attributes removed. A list of training-data *sources* tells you nothing about that. Knowing a model was trained partly on an employer's ATS records and partly on a third-party language-model component doesn't tell you whether it produces a discriminatory selection-rate ratio. Tellingly, the one regime that already mandates deep data-provenance documentation (EU AI Act Article 10) keeps it as **regulator-reviewed internal documentation paired with a separate outcome-based conformity assessment** — not as public source-publication treated as a bias detector in its own right. That design choice is itself evidence the two objects aren't substitutes.

**What would actually work better, and why this one is dominated:** A mandatory outcome-based selection-rate audit (testing actual hiring results against something like the EEOC's four-fifths rule) beats source-disclosure-only on every relevant criterion — this ranking holds regardless of how you weight the criteria. Combining outcome-auditing with regulator-held verification access does better still, though that finer choice is closer and can flip with modest reweighting.

**Where this could be wrong:** The whole analysis assumes the national rule doesn't specify unusually strong enforcement teeth beyond self-report-plus-posting — no actual statutory text exists yet to check that against. It would also weaken (not reverse) if disclosed sources turn out to meaningfully feed outside investigators, journalists, or plaintiffs' litigation discovery — a real but historically narrow channel, bounded by how few independent auditors have actually done that work under the existing NYC regime.

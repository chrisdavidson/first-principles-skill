# First-Principles Analysis: Should a 6-Engineer Team at ~1,200 DAU Adopt Microservices?

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Should this team decompose its single deployable into multiple independently-deployed services now, given that the coordination problem microservices exist to solve is a *multi-team* organizational problem, and this team currently constitutes exactly one team?

**Success criteria:**
1. The recommendation names what specific problem microservices solve (not what they are).
2. The recommendation names what problem this team actually has at 6 engineers / 1,200 DAU, and whether the two match.
3. The recommendation states explicit, checkable trigger conditions under which the answer would flip — not a permanent verdict.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Stay on the monolith. Invest the engineering effort that would have gone into service decomposition into internal modularity instead — enforced module/package boundaries, a clean dependency direction, mechanical CI-level enforcement of that boundary (not just a stated intention), a strong automated test suite, and trunk-based CI/CD. Scale the single deployable horizontally (stateless replicas behind a load balancer) if and when load actually requires it (chain C1, chain C2). Separately, pre-identify the 1–2 components most likely to ever need isolation (e.g., auth, payments, PII-handling) and keep them behind clean internal interfaces now, so that a future extraction is a deliberate strangler-fig operation rather than an emergency rewrite (chain C3).

**Key insight:** The coordination overhead microservices exist to relieve is a property of the *organization* — specifically, the number of independently-operating teams — not a property of the *user base*. A team of 6 people has exactly 15 pairwise coordination paths whether its code lives in one deployable or twenty; splitting the deployable does not touch that number, it only adds a new, avoidable operational cost on top of it. The pattern that made microservices famous (Amazon, Google, Netflix) was solving a many-team problem these companies had, not a many-user problem this team has (chain C1).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This recommendation accepts a larger single-failure blast radius than a fully decomposed system would have, and explicitly trades it for a much lower total operational surface — a trade addressed with named, low-cost mitigations (process supervision, circuit breakers, horizontal replicas) rather than eliminated outright (chain C1; see also the pre-mortem's Cluster 2 disposition, above). It also depends on module-boundary discipline actually being enforced going forward, not merely declared — a real and ongoing cost, not a free lunch (chain C1's second-order extension; Cluster 1 disposition). Finally, it defers (not forecloses) any component-specific extraction: if a specific piece of the system develops a genuinely different scaling, reliability, or compliance profile, extracting just that piece is the correct move regardless of the team's overall size (chain C3) — this recommendation is a default given the stated team size and load, not a permanent architectural stance.

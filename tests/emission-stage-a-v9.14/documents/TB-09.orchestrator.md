## The core question
It's not "is this a good startup" — it's a threshold test: does the equity's expected value clear the bar set by four years of foregone salary, and does your cushion actually protect you through the sequence of risks (startup failure → job search) rather than just covering 11 months in isolation?

## The math that matters

**Equity value (rough, wide bracket):** Weighting typical VC outcome buckets (~30% total loss, ~40% return-only-capital, ~29% modest exit, ~1% breakout) against a typical 4th/5th-hire grant (~0.3–0.5%, subject to dilution and liquidation preferences that can zero out common stock), the expected value over a 4-year vest lands around **$112K**, bracketed roughly $20K–$460K depending on your actual grant %. Setting that equal to 4 years of a 30% cut implies a **breakeven salary around $93.5K** — below that, the equity plausibly outweighs the cut; above it, the cut costs more than the equity is likely worth. This is the single most decision-relevant number, and it hinges on your real salary and your real equity terms — neither of which I have.

**Runway isn't 11 months of protection — it's sequential.** The real risk isn't "will I survive 11 months," it's: how much of that cushion gets burned while employed at reduced pay (depends entirely on your expense-to-income ratio, which I don't know), and how much is left over when/if the startup fails, to fund a job search that can run several months to nearly a year in the current market. The number that actually protects you is the *residual* cushion at failure, not the headline 11 months.

**The true pay cut is probably worse than 30%** if the startup doesn't offer health coverage — individual marketplace insurance can add several more points to the effective cut.

## The assumptions worth challenging
- **"Stable job" isn't a guarantee** — it's a probability label, same as the startup's survival odds are.
- **"Hard to reverse" is misdirected.** What's actually hard to reverse is your *cash position* if you burn it down — not your career standing. At 34 with an existing track record, a startup stint that doesn't pan out generally reads as ordinary experience, not a black mark.
- **It's not strictly binary.** Resigning sometimes triggers a counteroffer from your current employer — a third option the framing skips.
- **Team size (4 people) alone tells you nothing about risk** — funding status and runway are what matter, and you haven't stated either.

## Bottom line
On today's information, a weighted comparison across financial safety, expected value, career growth, and benefits favors **staying**, and that lean is fairly robust to how you weight those factors. But it's *not* robust to the actual numbers — three of the biggest inputs (your real salary/expenses, the startup's real funding runway, and the actual signed equity terms) are unknowns that could flip it. Treat "stay" as the default only until you've plugged in real numbers, not as a verdict.

**Before deciding, get these four things:**
1. Your exact salary and monthly expenses (tests the breakeven math)
2. The startup's actual funding status and runway
3. The exact equity %, vesting, and liquidation preference terms — not industry medians
4. Whether health coverage is included

One more thing worth naming: the framework here leans on "financial safety" and "expected value" as the heaviest-weighted factors, but the one input nobody but you can score — how much you actually want to do this, mission/autonomy fit — was left neutral by necessity. That's a legitimate rival case for joining that this analysis can't resolve for you.

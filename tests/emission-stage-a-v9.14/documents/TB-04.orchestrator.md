Here's the analysis. The core finding: **these three levers work through different mechanisms with different preconditions, so the right choice depends on checkable facts about the specific city — not on which policy is generically "best."**

## What each lever actually does (mechanism, not intuition)

- **Congestion pricing** raises the price of driving. It only converts that into car-trip reduction if there's spare capacity on other modes for diverted trips to land on. Evidence (Stockholm: ~20%→50% cut over time; NYC: 11% first year) comes exclusively from cities that already had high-capacity rail transit in the priced corridors *before* the charge began.
- **Free transit** lowers transit's price to zero but adds no capacity. In Tallinn (fare-free since 2013), the people who responded were pedestrians and already-transit-dependent riders — not drivers. A decade later, car-commute share had *risen*, not fallen. It also creates a permanent recurring subsidy (transit agencies typically recover only 15–25% of operating costs from fares), plus crowding on already-loaded routes.
- **Protected cycle lanes** reduce cycling's safety/comfort cost, not its range. Effects are real (1.8–4.3x more cycling on protected vs. unprotected blocks) but structurally capped by trip length — only useful for the roughly half of trips under 3 miles, and a citywide effect requires a *connected network*, not a showcase corridor.

**None of the three has a documented case of hitting 20% citywide in 3 years on its own** — the closest analog (pricing) only demonstrates that magnitude for the narrower population of trips crossing a priced cordon, not all commuter car journeys citywide.

## Five facts that should actually decide it

1. **Trip-length/density profile** — short, dense trips favor cycle lanes and de-risk pricing's precondition; sprawling long commutes favor neither.
2. **Pre-existing spare transit capacity** — without it, pricing suppresses trips rather than shifting them, and cycle lanes' capacity-independence becomes decisive.
3. **Legal/political authorization already in hand** — pricing's biggest cost is a multi-year adoption fight (Stockholm needed a referendum; NYC needed years of litigation even after state authorization). A city that's already cleared that hurdle can move fast.
4. **Capital vs. operating budget posture** — free transit is a permanent operating drain; pricing is usually net-revenue-positive; cycle lanes are a one-time capital cost.
5. **What the target is actually for** — if the real goal is *car-trip reduction*, pricing or cycle lanes win. If it's *mobility access for low-income, car-less residents*, free transit's poor car-shift record is irrelevant — it's scoring the wrong objective.

## A subtlety worth flagging

The "20%" target isn't one comparable number across options — pricing studies measure zone vehicle-entries, free-transit studies measure citywide mode share, cycle-lane studies measure a relative multiplier on a small base. A city has to fix which of these its own target means before comparing track records.

Under a generic weighting (no city specified), pricing edges out cycle lanes (84 vs. 81 — a narrow, reversible lead), with free transit trailing both by a wide, robust margin on car-trip reduction specifically. But that ordering flips toward cycle lanes in a low-transit-capacity, short-trip city, and flips toward free transit if the real objective is access rather than car reduction.

**Bottom line:** since no single lever has demonstrated the required magnitude in the cited record, the practical answer is to use the five facts above to pick a *starting* lever, then plan to bundle in the other two within the same window — every real-world success case (Stockholm, NYC) actually combined pricing with concurrent transit investment rather than relying on one lever alone.

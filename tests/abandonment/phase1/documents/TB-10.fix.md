## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given that "move closer to ageing parents" is being evaluated as if geographic proximity itself were the thing that must be decided, what is the variable that should actually govern the decision — and is proximity that variable, a proxy for it, or a mechanism competing with other mechanisms that could serve it better?

**Success criteria:**
1. The Conclusion section names a specific, non-proximity variable (or small set of variables) that a family can verify about its own situation, such that a reader can check whether the Conclusion's recommended approach actually turns on that variable rather than on distance itself.
2. The Conclusion section presents at least two structurally distinct options besides "move the whole family" or "don't," each scoreable on the same criteria, such that a reader can check the option set is not artificially binary.
3. The Conclusion section states, for at least one criterion, a concrete verification step (a question to ask, a fact to check) that would change which option a specific family should choose — checkable by seeing whether such a step appears attached to a criterion in the Conclusion.
4. The Conclusion's confidence rating is derived from, and matches, its weakest contributing Derivation Chain — checkable by comparing the stated band against the chains cited.

## 2. Assumptions Table

### Supporting brainstorm — Fishbone (category scan, default six-category set)

To surface the full space of untested beliefs before classifying them, categories scanned: **People** (parents' actual needs/preferences, spouse's career, each child's developmental stage, siblings), **Process** (how caregiving tasks are currently divided), **Technology and Tools** (telehealth, remote monitoring, video contact), **Environment** (destination labor market, housing cost, school quality), **Information** (uncertainty about parents' health trajectory), **Resources** (financial runway, employer flexibility, local paid-care market). Discriminating observations: for "is the unmet need hands-on or emotional?" — a discriminating observation is *whether parents report specific tasks going undone versus reporting loneliness/lack of contact*; these are distinguishable by directly asking, and no analysis substitutes for that. For "will siblings' contribution change?" no discriminating observation exists without an explicit conversation — flagged as untested rather than guessed.

### Supporting brainstorm — Five-Whys (causal mode)

Symptom: "We feel we need to move." Why? → Parents are ageing and something must be provided. Why (that something isn't already provided)? → Some tasks require physical presence and no one currently supplies them. Why does no one supply them? → Because siblings, paid help, and the parents' own capacity aren't currently organized to cover them. Counterfactual test: had an explicit division-of-labor conversation occurred, would the "we must move" pressure still exist in its current form? **No** — much of the pressure is counterfactually dependent on the absence of that conversation, so it is not a fixed root cause, it is an addressable one. Root cause verdict: **Root cause — hold an explicit caregiving-division conversation with siblings and parents, within control.**

## 3. Ground Truths

### Supporting decomposition — Five-Whys (reduce-to-primitives mode)

Claim: "Moving closer to ageing parents meets their needs." Immediate constituents: (a) a taxonomy of what "needs" means for an ageing person, (b) a mechanism by which proximity changes task-delivery, (c) a mechanism by which proximity changes response latency to acute events, (d) a claim that these mechanisms are what actually improves outcomes for parents/family. Testing each: (a) reduces to the Katz/Lawton ADL-IADL clinical taxonomy — verified by professional convention, flagged unverified-at-source here (GT-1?). (b) and (c) reduce to a single empirical study's reported findings — (b) verified read-at-source (GT-2), (c) reduces further to transportation-speed and flight-logistics parameters — an estimate, not a citation (GT-6?). (d) does **not** reduce to a verified primitive — GT-2's own adjusted analysis found no significant association between proximity and caregiver mental-health outcomes, so (d) is **not verified**; it is the load-bearing gap this whole analysis turns on.

### Estimate — emergency-response latency by baseline distance (feeds GT-6, C1)

Target quantity: door-to-door hours for a family member to physically reach parents during an acute event.
- **Local/co-located** unit factors: distance (~0–15 mi) ÷ avg urban/suburban speed (~25–35 mph) + minor delay → central ≈ 0.3 hr; bracket **[0.1, 0.75] hr**.
- **Long-distance** (requiring a flight) unit factors: drive-to-airport (~0.5–1 hr) + arrive-early buffer (~1.5–2 hr) + flight (~2–5 hr) + deplane/rental (~0.5–1 hr) = best-case central ≈ 5–9 hr; realistic bracket accounting for flight availability/connections: **[4, 30] hr**, central ≈ 10–14 hr.
- **Decision-resolution stop criterion:** even the local bracket's *pessimistic* end (0.75 hr) is exceeded more than 5× by the long-distance bracket's *optimistic* end (4 hr) — the gap is decision-resolving without tighter figures.

### Ground Truths list

- **GT-1?** Ageing-related care needs decompose into basic ADLs (bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding — Katz Index, 1963) and instrumental IADLs (phone use, shopping, food prep, housekeeping, laundry, transportation, medication management, finances — Lawton & Brody scale, 1969), a framework explicitly designed "to identify improvement or deterioration over time." — cited to: HIGN "Try This" series / sralab.org summaries; reported-by-delegate: WebSearch snippet, cited source not opened by this analysis.
- **GT-2** Among U.S. caregivers of adults 50+, 26.3% co-reside, 47.4% live 1–20 min away, 13.1% live 21–59 min away, 13.2% live ≥1 hr away; co-residing/closer caregivers do significantly more hands-on ADL/IADL tasks (transportation, chores, shopping, medication tracking, diet, skin/foot care); there is **no significant difference by proximity** for financial support, patient advocacy, or health-system-logistics tasks; and after adjusting for caregiver education, marital status, and employment, **residential proximity itself shows no significant association with caregiver mental-health/depression outcomes**. — source: "Caregiving across distances: residential proximity, care activities, and mental health," *The Gerontologist*; read-at-source: PMC13418846, quoted passages on distance distribution and the adjusted mental-health finding.
- **GT-3?** The National Institute on Aging defines "long-distance caregivers" as those living ≥1 hour away by any means of travel, and estimates ~7 million Americans are long-distance caregivers. — cited to: nia.nih.gov/health/long-distance-caregiving; reported-by-delegate (WebSearch snippet). **Phase 3 failure record:** direct WebFetch of this URL returned HTTP 405 (Method Not Allowed) — unreachable.
- **GT-4** Each additional residential move is associated with declines in reading/math scores and less positive social skills/higher emotional-behavioral problems; moves during early/middle childhood produce longer-lasting social-emotional effects, while moves during middle-childhood/early-adolescence (after school has started) produce shorter-term academic effects that diminish over time; school moves specifically carry a distinct, slightly stronger association with cognitive-skill effects than residential moves alone; effects accumulate across multiple moves. — source: Society for Research in Child Development summary of a *Child Development* study; read-at-source: srcd.org/news/childrens-social-and-academic-functioning-impeded-when-their-families-move-more-often, quoted passages on timing and cumulative effects.
- **GT-5?** Irreversible decisions made under uncertainty that will resolve further over time carry a positive "value of waiting," distinct from and additional to the direct payoff of the choice (real-options / investment-under-uncertainty theory). — cited to: Dixit & Pindyck, *Investment Under Uncertainty* (1994); unverified — not opened at source this session.
- **GT-6?** Local emergency-response travel time brackets to roughly [0.1, 0.75] hr; long-distance (flight-requiring) travel time brackets to roughly [4, 30] hr — a gap of one to two orders of magnitude. — source: first-principles estimate above using representative transportation parameters; unverified — not measured for a specific family/route.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Treat proximity itself as the maximand

**What was tried:** Scoring options purely on distance-to-parents, treating any proximity gain as a free-standing benefit above some threshold.
**Why abandoned:** Contradicts GT-2 — proximity's demonstrated effect is task-specific (more hands-on ADL/IADL delivery) and, after adjustment, shows no significant association with overall caregiver mental-health outcomes. Treating proximity as the maximand double-counts a benefit not shown to exist at the outcome level that supposedly justifies the disruption.
**What it ruled out:** Any downstream framework that ranks options by distance rather than by verified task/support delivery against a diagnosed need (this is exactly what C1 and C3 replace it with).

### Dead End: Use "grandparents nearby is good for kids" as a stand-alone justification

**What was tried:** Adding a scored criterion for "grandparent bonding" as an independently scored benefit, backed only by the general cultural expectation that grandparent proximity helps children.

**Why abandoned:** This is exactly the analogy-as-direct-evidence move D-07 prohibits — no ground truth in this analysis establishes the magnitude or reliability of this benefit for this family's specific children, while GT-4 already shows a real, sourced *cost* on the children's side that would need to be outweighed by evidence, not assumed by analogy ("of course it's good for kids, families do this").

**What it ruled out:** Including "grandparent bonding benefit" as a scored criterion in C5's trade-off matrix without a ground truth backing its magnitude. It remains a plausible consideration a specific family could add to the framework with its own self-supplied estimate — this general analysis cannot size it.

### Dead End: Model the decision as a single irreversible binary choice (move / don't move)

**What was tried:** Presenting one recommendation — relocate or stay — as if these were the only two live options.

**Why abandoned:** Once GT-2 established that different task categories can be delivered from different distances (C3), and GT-1?'s longitudinal design plus geriatric-decline logic established that needs change over time (C6), a binary permanent framing was shown to be strictly narrower than the facts support — not wrong in every case, but dominated once C5 and C6 were derived (C5's four-option matrix ranked the binary framing's implicit "full relocation" option last under the stated illustrative weights).

**What it ruled out:** Presenting a single yes/no verdict as if the decision has only two live options; replaced by the four-option structure (A–D) in C5.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Before treating relocation as the answer, run a four-step process: (1) diagnose the specific unmet need using the ADL/IADL and emotional/social categories, verified directly with parents, and quantify each child's specific relocation cost individually rather than as a lump sum; (2) score the full option set — full relocation, augmented status quo, parents relocating instead, and a partial-proximity compromise — against weighted criteria where verified delivered support and reversibility carry real weight, not felt proximity; (3) treat parents' preference and destination job-market/remote-work flexibility as load-bearing facts to verify before committing, and treat sibling division-of-labor as a precondition, not an afterthought; (4) build explicit revision triggers into whatever is chosen, since the diagnosed need is expected to change (chains C1, C3, C4, C5, C6, C7).

**Key insight:** The decision is conventionally framed as a location decision ("move or don't"), but the ground truths show it is actually a task-diagnosis-and-allocation decision: proximity's only demonstrated effect is on which hands-on tasks get delivered faster (GT-2, chain C3), not on caregiver or parent wellbeing directly after adjustment for other factors — and the costs usually cited (career, school) are smaller and more uniform than the actual dominant costs, which are age/timing-dependent and asymmetric to reverse (chains C2, C4). Reasoning by analogy ("of course you move closer, that's what family does") would never surface this distinction (chain C1).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Running the diagnosis, preference-verification, and division-of-labor conversation before committing costs time and emotional bandwidth, and some family members may feel this delays acting on a felt urgency — this is priced in chain C6's handling of the option-value tradeoff, not free (chain C6).

**Confidence:** LOW — the Conclusion's rating matches its weakest contributing chains. C1 and C2 (MEDIUM) support the *reframing* (diagnose first; true costs are asymmetry/reversibility) fairly solidly, each capped only by one unverified `GT-N?` input each (GT-1?/GT-6? for C1, GT-5? for C2 — verification paths stated on those chains' own lines). But C5, C6, and C7 (all LOW) carry the *specific structural recommendations* — which option wins, whether triggers add value, whether preconditions are truly load-bearing — and each is short on two axes (Inference and/or Rivals), chiefly because the option-scoring is illustrative rather than a specific family's verified data, and because the rival "proximity confers real holistic benefit this analysis's single study didn't detect" is not ruled out anywhere in this document.

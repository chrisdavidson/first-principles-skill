# 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End 1: Pure trust-based, all-take-home redesign with no synchronous channel

**What was tried:** Considered recommending complete elimination of proctored/synchronous assessment in favor of redesigning every take-home task to be "AI-proof by design" (personal reflection, process documentation, staged drafts).

**Why abandoned:** This satisfies the pedagogical and fidelity functions but cannot satisfy the certification function's requirement to verify, to a third party, that a specific individual independently possesses the competence (GT-2?, part b), given that no channel in this design is resistant to undisclosed AI authorship end-to-end (GT-3/GT-4/chain C2). It also leaves the retrieval-practice loss (GT-5?/chain C3) unaddressed.

**What it ruled out:** Saves future analysts from re-proposing a no-synchronous-channel design as sufficient on its own; chain C2 is the specific reason it fails, not a vague "it might not work."

### Dead End 2: Keep timed exams unchanged and rely on AI-detection software as the integrity backstop

**What was tried:** Evaluated whether strengthening detection tooling while keeping the current format mix unchanged was an adequate response (i.e., treating Option B as sufficient rather than merely a trade-off survivor).

**Why abandoned:** Chain C5's theoretical-limit argument shows detection reliability has a shrinking, not fixed, ceiling as AI capability improves, and current independently-measured false-positive rates (GT-3/GT-4) already disproportionately misclassify non-native speakers. Deploying this as the primary backstop trades a known, worsening equity harm for an illusory sense of security, and forgoes the fidelity gains available for AI-augmentable disciplines (chain C4).

**What it ruled out:** Saves the reader from treating "better detectors" as a viable long-term integrity strategy; the ceiling argument (C5) is why, not merely "detectors are currently imperfect."

### Dead End 3: A single, discipline-agnostic policy applied uniformly

**What was tried:** Considered a single blanket rule ("all exams become open-book take-home" or "all exams stay closed-book timed") applied identically across every department.

**Why abandoned:** GT-8? establishes that skill types differ sharply in AI-substitutability (embodied/live-observed vs. textual/synthesizable), so a uniform rule is construct-invalid for at least one class of skill regardless of which direction it is set (chain C1).

**What it ruled out:** Saves the reader from expecting a single-sentence, discipline-agnostic policy to be the deliverable; chain C1 is the structural reason a portfolio approach is required.

# 6. Conclusion

This analysis concludes with a differentiated, function-matched assessment portfolio, detailed below.

**Recommended approach:** Do not perform a blanket swap of timed written exams for take-home assessments. Instead, adopt a differentiated, function-matched assessment portfolio: (a) keep frequent, low-stakes, closed-book retrieval checks for foundational/declarative knowledge, not as the primary high-stakes certification instrument; (b) move most high-stakes certification of higher-order competence to synchronous, observed formats (oral defense, live practical/clinical performance, in-person problem-solving) that are AI-resistant by physical-channel control rather than by detection; (c) redesign take-home/project work for AI-augmentable disciplines to explicitly require and grade AI-augmented judgment — critique, direction, verification of AI output — rather than banning or ignoring AI use (chain C6).

**Key insight:** The "timed exam vs. take-home" question is a category error: it conflates assessment *format* with assessment *function*. A blanket take-home swap fails not because AI use is illegitimate, but because AI-text detection has a structural, shrinking ceiling that cannot durably anchor a certification claim (chain C5), while timed closed-book conditions carry an independent pedagogical value — retrieval practice — that a swap would also discard (chain C1). A simple "ban AI vs. allow AI" framing conflates these two separate failures.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This recommendation accepts a real feasibility cost — the differentiated portfolio scored only 2/5 on implementation feasibility against the status quo's 4/5, requiring funded, phased redesign rather than a costless policy memo — and it knowingly trades today's detector-bias equity harm for a new, foreseeable equity risk in synchronous formats (disparate impact on anxious, ESL, and neurodivergent students) that must be mitigated by design, not assumed away (chain C6).

**Strongest objection:** The strongest objection to this recommendation is that it may simply relocate inequity rather than resolve it: moving high-stakes certification into oral/practical synchronous formats could reproduce, for a different subgroup, the same kind of disparate impact biased AI detectors currently produce for non-native English speakers (chain C6's third-order extension). This does not overturn the recommendation, because the two risks are not symmetric: the detection-based approach's flaw is an information-theoretic ceiling that gets strictly worse as AI capability improves (chain C5), while the synchronous-format equity risk is a foreseeable, engineerable implementation problem — addressable by accommodations, calibrated rubrics, and monitored pass-rate disparities named explicitly as preconditions of rollout, not as an afterthought (chain C6).

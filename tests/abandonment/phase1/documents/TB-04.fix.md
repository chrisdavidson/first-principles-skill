Speed is necessary but not sufficient.
**What it ruled out:** Any pure speed-ranking heuristic (C4, C6).

### Dead End: Recommend "do all three, always" as a universal answer
**What was tried:** Since A1 established the levers aren't mutually exclusive, default to recommending all three simultaneously in every case.
**Why abandoned:** Contradicts C6's finding that eligibility gates (legal authority, fiscal capacity) can rule a lever out entirely for a specific city regardless of desire to combine, and dodges the actual question asked ("what should determine the choice") by refusing to make the criteria explicit. Non-exclusivity (A1) is not the same claim as "always combine all three" — the correct synthesis is the conditional, fact-gated portfolio in C7, not a blanket recommendation.
**What it ruled out:** A non-responsive "always do everything" answer (A1, C6, C7).

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not pick a single "best" lever. First apply the eligibility gates from chain C6 — does the city already hold legal tolling authority, and can it sustain either a transit operating subsidy or a capital program — to determine which levers are even admissible within 3 years. Then size each admissible lever's achievable 3-year contribution against this city's actual monocentricity, transit spare-capacity, and trip-distance facts (chains C2–C4), not against Stockholm/Dunkirk/Seville headlines. Combine the admissible levers whose facts are favorable into a sequenced portfolio sized to sum to 20%, front-loading whichever lever is legally fastest and locking in at least one physical lane-capacity reallocation to protect the result against rebound after month 36 (chain C7).

**Key insight:** A large percentage ridership or elasticity response is not the same thing as a large percentage car-trip reduction — Dunkirk's 65–125% bus-ridership growth removed only about 3.7% of baseline car trips because bus started from a 5% base against car's 67% (chain C3). Cities that cite Dunkirk, Stockholm, or Seville as proof a lever "works" are usually citing the percentage-growth headline, not the base-rate arithmetic that determines whether that growth is big enough, relative to the car base it must displace, to reach 20% (chain C3).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Congestion pricing trades the fastest, most revenue-positive gains (chain C2, chain C6) for regressive equity effects and, absent existing home-rule authority, a real risk of multi-year authorization delay that can consume the entire window (chain C6). Free transit trades broad political popularity and progressive equity effects (chain C6) for a large recurring subsidy and a crush-loading risk that can cap or reverse gains if spare capacity is absent (chain C3). Protected cycling trades the lowest fiscal risk and the only durably rebound-resistant mechanism of the three (chain C5) for the slowest build-out and the smallest 3-year ceiling in cities with long commutes or unfavorable geography (chain C4).

**Premise:** It is month 36. The city's commuter car trips have not fallen by 20%, whichever single lever or portfolio was chosen. What caused this failure?

**Causes** (generated from the commuter/rider, transit-operator, and city-budget/political-actor viewpoints, unfiltered):
- Cordon boundary was drawn so most commutes bypass it (poor monocentricity fit).
- Legal authorization from a state/national government stalled past year one.
- Toll price was politically watered down before launch, below the level needed to move the inelastic peak-commute segment.
- Revenue-earmarking fight delayed launch or diverted enforcement funding.
- Commuters shifted travel time (off-peak) rather than mode, so trip counts didn't fall.
- Existing transit had no spare capacity; new free-fare riders caused crush-loading, degrading service for existing captive riders.
- Fare revenue loss wasn't backfilled, forcing service cuts that offset ridership gains elsewhere.
- Baseline transit mode share was too low for even a large ridership increase to translate into a meaningful car-trip percentage dent.
- Free fare disproportionately induced new trips (previously walked/cycled/stayed home) rather than shifting car trips.
- Cycling network was built too slowly or with gaps, never reaching the connectivity threshold for commuters to trust a door-to-door route.
- City's geography/climate made cycling structurally unattractive for most commutes.

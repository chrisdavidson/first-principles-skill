# Would "publish your training data sources" actually stop discriminatory hiring AI?

**Short answer: no — not as stated. The rule targets the wrong layer of the problem, is trivially satisfiable with uninformative boilerplate, and where real employment-discrimination law has actually been operationalized (NYC, the EU), regulators reached for a different mechanism entirely. That divergence is itself the tell.**

Below is the reasoning, organized against your seven points, with the ground truths and chains that carry the weight of the verdict made explicit so you can see where it would break if a premise were wrong.

---

## 1. What goal is this actually aimed at?

Strip the framing and there are at least three distinct goals hiding under "transparency," and they are not the same problem:

- **Goal A — reduce discriminatory outcomes.** Fewer qualified candidates rejected because of race/sex/age/disability proxies baked into the model.
- **Goal B — enable accountability/auditing.** Give some third party (regulator, journalist, researcher) enough information to check A independently of the vendor's say-so.
- **Goal C — give individual candidates recourse.** Let a specific rejected person find out whether *they* were disadvantaged and challenge it.

A rule can serve one of these without touching the others. "Publish training data sources" is pitched as serving A, but as I'll show, it's mechanically aimed at B-in-name-only, and does nothing for C. Keeping these three separate matters because the whole appeal of the rule rests on quietly assuming A follows from B, which is exactly the link that needs checking, not assuming.

## 2. What does "publish training data sources" actually mean?

This is the crux, and the question is right to insist it not be conflated. There are at least four readings, with very different consequences:

| Interpretation | What's disclosed | Discrimination-relevant? |
|---|---|---|
| (A) Provenance/names | "Common Crawl, LinkedIn public data, in-house applicant records 2015–2024" | No — names carry no information about composition or outcomes |
| (B) The data itself | Raw resumes/records | Yes in principle, but illegal in most jurisdictions (privacy) and commercially catastrophic (licensed/proprietary data) — this option is not actually available |
| (C) Data composition/demographics | "X% of training resumes came from applicants under 40, Y% self-reported gender…" | Partially — tells you about the training pool, not what the model learned to do with it |
| (D) Outcome/impact statistics | Selection/interview/offer rates by protected class, tested against a disparity threshold | Yes — this is the only one that measures the thing you actually care about: what the model *does*, not what it *saw* |

The plain-language reading of "publish training data sources" is (A). That's the version I'll analyze as the rule, because that's what the words say — and it's worth being explicit that (C) and (D) are different rules wearing the same headline, not variants of this one.

## 3. Does (A) actually give anyone what they need?

Trace the causal chain from rule to goal:

**GT1** (reported-by-delegate — via secondary summaries, not the statute text itself): NYC Local Law 144 requires an independent *bias audit* measuring selection-rate impact ratios by race/sex/ethnicity, with a public summary of *audit results* — not a list of training data sources. [Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/services/audit-assurance/articles/nyc-local-law-144-algorithmic-bias.html), [Littler](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/new-york-city-adopts-final-regulations-use-ai-hiring-and-promotion-extends)

**GT2** (reported-by-delegate): The EU AI Act classifies hiring/recruitment tools as high-risk (Annex III); Article 10 requires training data to be "relevant, representative, and free of errors" that could cause discriminatory outcomes, with data-governance documentation — a quality-and-process obligation running to the regulator/auditor, not a public source-list. [artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/), [DPO-consulting](https://www.dpo-consulting.com/blog/high-risk-ai-systems)

**GT3** (reported-by-delegate): The EEOC's four-fifths rule treats a protected-group selection rate below 80% of the top group's rate as evidence of adverse impact — an *outcome* test, indifferent to how the tool was built. [eSkill](https://www.eskill.com/resources/glossary/four-fifths-rule), [Berkshire Associates](https://www.berkshireassociates.com/blog/this-month-in-i/o-the-uniform-guidelines)

**GT4** (definitional, no citation needed): Knowing the *names* of training sources tells you nothing about demographic composition, label definitions, feature weighting, the objective function, or the output distribution. "What was the map" and "what did the machine build from it" are different questions.

**Chain (HIGH confidence — rests only on GT4, a definitional fact):**
GT4 (source names ≠ composition/outcome data)
→ a candidate, journalist, or regulator reading "trained on Common Crawl + LinkedIn + applicant records" learns nothing about whether the model penalizes employment gaps, certain name patterns, or zip codes
→ detecting that requires either statistical testing of the model's *actual outputs* (an audit, per GT1/GT3) or access to the data plus its labels — neither of which "source" disclosure provides
→ **the rule produces transparency-in-name, not transparency-that-enables-action.**

This is the load-bearing finding, and it doesn't depend on anything uncertain — it follows from what a source list logically contains, independent of any empirical claim about regulators or markets.

## 4. Second-order effects

Running these through both an actor lens (who reacts) and a time lens (what changes over cycles):

- **Cosmetic compliance is not a risk, it's the rational equilibrium.** Because a source list is compatible with true-but-empty boilerplate ("proprietary applicant database, licensed third-party data, publicly available web data"), the cheapest way to comply and the way to comply are the same thing. Legal signs off, the political box is ticked, nothing about the model's behavior changes. (Confidence: HIGH — this follows directly from GT4.)

- **Compliance cost is regressive by construction.** Determining what counts as a "source," documenting it, absorbing the legal risk of a wrong or incomplete disclosure — these are largely fixed costs per product line, and a fixed cost is a smaller share of revenue for a large vendor than a small one. This is arithmetic (GT5), not a disputed empirical claim.

- **Foundation-model tools break the premise entirely.** A resume screener built by fine-tuning or prompting a general-purpose LLM has "training data" that is mostly the base model's web-scale pretraining corpus — often undisclosed by the model developer itself, sometimes for active copyright-litigation reasons. The deploying employer or HR-tech vendor frequently doesn't know or control that data (GT6). So compliance collapses to "we used a third-party foundation model," which is maximally uninformative, or the vendor must extract warranties from a foundation-model provider who has every incentive to withhold and none to disclose.

- **Predicted market effect (MEDIUM confidence — this is the one genuinely uncertain step):** the entities best positioned to absorb compliance cost *and* indemnify foundation-model provenance risk are the largest incumbents. Over a few product cycles this plausibly pushes the market toward fewer, larger, more litigation-hardened vendors — the opposite of the accountability/diversity the rule is aimed at. I'm flagging this MEDIUM rather than HIGH because it rests on an assumed elasticity of vendor behavior to compliance cost that I haven't measured — it would be confirmed or refuted by watching actual vendor consolidation after implementation, and the headline verdict below doesn't depend on it.

## 5. Inversion: what would a discrimination-focused regulator actually build?

If the real goal is stopping discriminatory outcomes rather than being seen to regulate AI, the necessary ingredients are: outcome statistics broken down by protected class and tested against a disparity threshold (GT3); an independent audit of the model's *actual behavior* on realistic inputs, not its training inputs (GT1); a channel for individual recourse so a specific rejected candidate can request the relevant impact-ratio data; ongoing re-auditing, since models and applicant pools drift and a point-in-time source list is stale the moment it's posted; and enforcement triggered by audit findings, not by whether the disclosure format was complete.

None of that requires knowing dataset names. And this is exactly where NYC LL144 and the EU AI Act's Article 10 regime land (GT1, GT2) — because when a regulator is actually anchored to employment-discrimination law, outcome auditing is the tool that fits the statute (Title VII / four-fifths rule, GT3). A source-list rule is the shape you get when the design goal is a legible, low-verification-cost box to check — not the shape you get when the design goal is stopping discrimination. I ran this as an explicit weighted trade-off across four candidate disclosure designs (source names / raw data / demographic composition / outcome audits), scoring actionability, cost, legal feasibility, gaming-resistance, and fit with foundation-model tools: outcome-audit disclosure wins by a wide enough margin (78 vs. 59 vs. 47 weighted points) that no single criterion's weight, moved alone, changes the ranking. Raw-data disclosure is knocked out entirely — it fails on privacy/trade-secret law regardless of score.

**Theoretical ceiling, even for the good version:** outcome auditing has a hard limit too. It can catch large, population-level disparate impact; it structurally cannot catch harms too small to move the statistics — small protected subgroups, intersectional characteristics, disability/neurodivergence proxies not tested for. That's a limit of statistical power, not of disclosure design, and it's worth naming so the "better rule" isn't oversold either.

## 6. Pre-mortem — three years later, still failing

Generating causes from each affected party's vantage point:

- **Compliance officer:** "We published a source list, legal signed off, nobody told us what else counted." → *ambiguous compliance bar* — fatal, since there's no outcome standard to fail.
- **Rejected candidate:** "The source list told me nothing about why I was rejected or whether my zip code hurt me." → *no individual recourse* — costly but survivable; fixable by a later amendment.
- **Regulator:** "We have no outcome metric to check the disclosure against, so there's nothing to enforce beyond 'did they post something.'" → *no outcome-linked enforcement trigger* — fatal.
- **Foundation-model vendor:** "We can't disclose pretraining data for competitive and copyright reasons, so customers pass through 'proprietary mix.'" → upstream opacity is legally incentivized, not solved by a downstream mandate.
- **Smaller competitor:** "We couldn't absorb the compliance/legal-review cycle and exited the SMB hiring-tool market; the market consolidated around vendors who could indemnify." → costly at 3 years, plausibly fatal to market diversity at 10.

The tripwire that would have caught this at design time didn't require deep study — just comparing the drafted text against the two live analogues (NYC LL144, EU AI Act) and noticing neither uses source-list disclosure as its operative mechanism. That comparison is a day's work, and its absence from the rule as posed is itself diagnostic of what the rule was actually optimizing for.

## 7. Verdict

**No, the rule as stated would not achieve its aim**, and the reasoning doesn't require any contested empirical premise to reach that conclusion — it falls out of what a source list logically contains (GT4) plus what's trivially satisfiable under that definition (Chain 4). The market-consolidation prediction (Chain 3) strengthens the case but is a MEDIUM-confidence add-on, not load-bearing for the headline.

**The gap between this rule and one that would work:**

1. Replace source-name disclosure with mandatory **outcome/impact-ratio audits** against protected classes, at deployment and on a recurring basis, by an independent auditor.
2. Add **individual recourse** — a rejected candidate can request the impact-ratio data relevant to the role they applied for.
3. Tie **enforcement to audit findings**, not to whether the disclosure paperwork was complete.
4. Write an explicit rule for **foundation-model-based tools** that fixes the obligation on the *deploying entity's actual hiring-relevant behavior*, regardless of what's traceable in upstream pretraining data — because it's the behavior that harms candidates, not the lineage.
5. If provenance disclosure still has independent value (research reproducibility, copyright accountability), keep it — but name it as a **separate goal** from discrimination prevention. Conflating the two is what makes the rule look like it's solving a problem it structurally cannot touch.

**Caveat on confidence:** the empirical facts about NYC LL144 and the EU AI Act above come from secondary summaries (Deloitte, Littler, artificialintelligenceact.eu, DPO-consulting), not the primary statutory text — I did not open the ordinance or the Regulation itself, so treat GT1–GT3 as reported-by-delegate rather than verified at source. If you want the verdict re-run against the primary legal text rather than summaries, that's a bounded follow-up, not a reason to doubt the definitional chains (1, 2, 4) the headline verdict actually rests on.

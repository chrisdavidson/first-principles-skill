## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given that this move's costs (two job changes, a school change) are certain and stipulated, but its benefit (meeting some need related to aging parents) is not yet specified, what should actually determine whether this family pays those certain costs — as opposed to the emotionally salient but often wrong factors ("family comes first," fear of a future emergency, guilt) that typically get weighted instead?

**Success criteria:**
1. The answer names the specific factors that are load-bearing for this class of decision, distinguished from factors commonly over-weighted (emotionally vivid worst cases) or under-weighted (financial/career risk, reversibility) — checkable against §6's Key Insight.
2. The answer yields an actionable, sequenced decision procedure a real family could run with their own numbers, not just a list of considerations — checkable against §6's Recommended Approach.
3. The answer explicitly names which currently-unspecified facts (parents' functional status and wishes, job-market specifics, children's ages/stage) must be resolved before the abstract trade-off converts into a concrete choice — checkable against the Assumptions Table's flagged unknowns and chain C5.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Supporting work: five-whys (causal mode) — "why move closer?"

*Symptom:* the family is considering relocating to be closer to aging parents. *Why?* Worry about the parents' wellbeing as they age. Counterfactual test: absent all worry, would relocation be considered? No — worry is necessary. Lateral scan of what causes the worry, at one level, surfaces four distinct branches: (a) a specific observed decline or event (fall, diagnosis, missed medication); (b) general anticipatory anxiety with no specific trigger; (c) social/cultural expectation ("good children live near parents"); (d) a positive desire for closeness/grandparent bonding, independent of any deficit. Counterfactual-tested: (a) is necessary when present and bottoms out at an actionable, in-control corrective — get a concrete assessment. (b) and (c) survive without relocation (managed by information and by naming the value choice explicitly) and are contributing conditions, not root causes. (d) is not a problem at all; it is a value to be weighed on its own terms. This drill directly grounds chain C1 and A3/A6's Challenge verdicts.

*Options (always including the status quo):* **A** — full immediate relocation (home purchase, both quit and move); **B** — stay, strengthen non-relocation support (professional local care, scheduled visits, financial support, sibling coordination); **C** — staged/trial relocation (rent, one adult secures a job first, defined 12–18-month review point, remote-compatible employer, move timed to a school break); **D** — the parents relocate to the family instead.

*Must-have (gating, not scored):* a concrete plan exists for meeting the parents' *actual, named* care need — not knowable in the abstract given this scenario's stipulated open variables; treated here as a due-diligence precondition (per C1) rather than an elimination criterion, since no option can be scored against an unspecified need.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Using parental age as the decision trigger

**What was tried:** Treating a numeric age threshold (e.g., "parents over 75") as the signal that relocation should be considered.
**Why abandoned:** Age is a poor proxy for functional care need (A3, Discard); two same-age parents can have wildly different functional status, and the five-whys drill shows the actual driver is far more often a specific event, anticipatory anxiety, or social expectation than age itself.
**What it ruled out:** Any age-based rule-of-thumb as the analysis's operative criterion — replaced by chain C1's requirement to verify the specific need (via A14).

### Dead End: Treating proximity as a single variable to be maximized

**What was tried:** Reasoning as if "closer is always better," implying the same household or same city is the ideal target.
**Why abandoned:** Chain C2's theoretical-limit finding shows proximity's marginal value saturates once within the latency threshold the *specific identified tasks* require; beyond that, further closeness mostly buys emotional value — a separate, legitimate axis (weight 2 in the trade-off), not an extension of care-need fit.
**What it ruled out:** A13 ("closer is always strictly better") as a general decision rule; replaced by "proximate enough for the specific named tasks."

### Dead End: Treating the decision as a single binary (move / don't move)

**What was tried:** Framing the whole analysis as a yes/no choice about relocating.
**Why abandoned:** This is a false dichotomy (A5, A6, Discard) — at least four structurally different options exist (stay + support, staged/trial move, full move, parents relocate), and collapsing to binary would have hidden the option (staged/trial, C) that competes closely with the winner in the trade-off (C5).
**What it ruled out:** Analyzing only "move vs. don't move" without the staged and parents-relocate alternatives, which would have understated both the winner's margin and its fragility.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Run this as a sequenced procedure, not a single yes/no vote (chains C1, C2, C3, C4, C5): (1) before treating "aging" as a trigger, get a concrete, named picture of the parents' functional/medical status and their own stated preference for help (chain C1; A8, A14); (2) explicitly separate the needs-based case for closeness from the values-based case (desire for connection, cultural obligation) and weigh each on its own terms rather than blending them (chain C1); (3) size any proximity commitment to the specific tasks that actually require low latency, not to an undifferentiated fear of distance (chain C2); (4) require verified — not intended — dual employment viability and a bounded, reversible structure (rent first, staged timeline, defined review point) before treating any move as final (chains C3, C5); (5) time any required school change to a natural break and verify destination-school quality, treating children's disruption as a design parameter rather than a blanket veto (chain C4); (6) re-run the weighted trade-off with the family's own scores once (1)–(5) are known, since the generic scoring here shows "stay and strengthen support" and "staged relocation" are close competitors whose ranking flips on how heavily the family weights relational closeness for its own sake (chain C5).

**Key insight:** This class of decision is usually resolved by weighing the wrong pair of things against each other — a vivid worst-case emergency image against a vivid image of family togetherness — while the factors that actually determine the outcome are almost entirely unemotional and procedural: which specific task needs low latency (chain C2), whether both incomes survive the transition (chain C3), whether the structure is reversible (chain C5), and whether the parents themselves want the specific form of help on offer (chain C1). Proximity is a dial to be sized to a diagnosed need, not a moral default.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Choosing to verify-then-size-then-stage (the recommended approach) costs time and emotional discomfort — an explicit, possibly uncomfortable conversation with aging parents about their actual wishes and status, and with employers about remote/staged options — that a fast, values-driven "we're just moving" decision avoids (chain C1). This analysis's generic scoring also cannot resolve the single most consequential trade-off — how much weight to place on emotional/relational proximity versus financial/reversibility risk — which chain C5's flip test shows is exactly what decides between the top two options. Deferring the decision to a verification process has a real cost if the five-whys branch (a), a genuine already-occurring decline, turns out to be the actual driver: no chain — flagged assumption only.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

**Dead End: Using parental age as the decision trigger.** Age is a poor proxy for functional need; two same-age parents can have very different status. Ruled out by A3's Discard; replaced by C1's requirement to verify the specific need.

**Dead End: Treating proximity as a single variable to be maximized.** Chain C2 shows proximity's marginal value saturates once within the latency threshold the specific tasks require; beyond that, further closeness mostly buys emotional value, a separate axis. Replaced by "proximate enough for the specific named tasks."

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Run this as a sequenced procedure, not a single yes/no vote: (1) before treating "aging" as a trigger, get a concrete, named picture of the parents' functional/medical status and their own stated preference for help; (2) explicitly separate the needs-based case for closeness from the values-based case (desire for connection, cultural obligation) and weigh each on its own terms; (3) size any proximity commitment to the specific tasks that actually require low latency, not to an undifferentiated fear of distance; (4) require verified — not intended — dual employment viability and a bounded, reversible structure (rent first, staged timeline, defined review point) before treating any move as final; (5) time any required school change to a natural break and verify destination-school quality; (6) re-run the weighted trade-off with the family's own scores once (1)–(5) are known, since the generic scoring shows "stay and strengthen support" and "staged relocation" are close competitors whose ranking flips on how heavily the family weights relational closeness for its own sake.

**Key insight:** This class of decision is usually resolved by weighing the wrong pair of things against each other — a vivid worst-case emergency image against a vivid image of family togetherness — while the factors that actually determine the outcome are almost entirely unemotional and procedural: which specific task needs low latency, whether both incomes survive the transition, whether the structure is reversible, and whether the parents themselves want the specific form of help on offer. Proximity is a dial to be sized to a diagnosed need, not a moral default.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Choosing to verify-then-size-then-stage costs time and emotional discomfort — an explicit, possibly uncomfortable conversation with aging parents about their actual wishes, and with employers about remote/staged options — that a fast, values-driven "we're just moving" decision avoids. This analysis's generic scoring also cannot resolve the single most consequential trade-off — how much weight to place on emotional/relational proximity versus financial/reversibility risk — which is exactly what decides between the top two options. Deferring the decision to a verification process has a real cost if a genuine, already-occurring decline turns out to be the actual driver.

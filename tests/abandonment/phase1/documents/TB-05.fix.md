## Trade-off analysis (Phase 4): five regulatory designs

**Options** (status quo included, per procedure): (a) outcome/bias auditing, (b) human-in-the-loop / right-to-explanation, (c) certification/conformity assessment, (d) training-data-source disclosure as proposed, (e) status quo.

**Must-haves:** no must-haves are applied as knockouts — a regulator can enact a toothless rule, so nothing here is legally impossible to implement; jurisdictional reach and detection-informativeness are instead treated as weighted criteria below, because the comparison is meant to rank effectiveness, not exclude options as unenactable.

**Criteria, weights locked before scoring, anchors stated:**
1. Detects actual discriminatory outcomes (w=5): 1=no statistically actionable signal; 5=direct, statistically valid selection-rate-disparity measurement.
2. Technical executability across real deployments (w=4): 1=infeasible/non-substantive for most deployment types; 5=feasible for nearly all, regardless of architecture.
3. Compliance cost proportionate to who holds the capability (w=3): 1=burden on the party least able to produce the information; 5=burden on the party that controls it.
4. Enforceability/verifiability by the regulator (w=4): 1=self-reported, unverifiable without deep technical capacity; 5=independently, cheaply verifiable.
5. Actionability for the rejected applicant (w=2): 1=no usable case-level information; 5=specific, case-level explanation.
6. Regulatory/political cost of adoption (w=1): 1=novel, contested, litigation-prone; 5=already precedented.

**Scores and weighted totals** (criterion order 1–6):

| Option | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | Weighted total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Outcome auditing | 5 | 4 | 3 | 4 | 2 | 4 | **74** |
| (b) Human-in-the-loop/explanation | 2 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 5 | 3 | **56** |
| (c) Certification/conformity assessment | 3 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 2 | 2 | **55** |
| (d) Disclosure (as proposed) | 1 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 3 | **29** |
| (e) Status quo | 1 | 5 | 5 | 1 | 1 | 5 | **51** |

Key score rationale, cited: (a)-criterion-1=5 and (a)-criterion-4=4 rest on GT-1/GT-2's demonstrated audit mechanism; (a)-criterion-5=2 reflects that aggregate statistics don't explain an individual rejection (an honest limitation, not suppressed). (d)-criterion-1=1 and (d)-criterion-4=2 rest directly on chain C6's finding. (d)-criterion-2=2 rests on chain C4's Fermi estimate. (d)-criterion-3=1 rests on A16. (c)-criterion-2/6 reflect GT-7/GT-8's 2027 EU AI Act applicability date — a certification ecosystem is still being built, not yet operating.

**Flip test:** (a)'s lead over runner-up (b) is 18 points. Decomposing by criterion, the largest per-unit-weight swing favoring (b) is criterion 5 (applicant actionability), where (b) beats (a) by 3 points/unit weight at current weight 2. Solving for the weight that flips the ranking: total lead = 3·w1 + 8 (crit.4, fixed) − 3·w5 + 1 (crit.6, fixed), with w1=5 fixed elsewhere ⇒ flipping via w5 alone requires w5 ≥ 8 — outside the 1–5 scale. Flipping via decreasing w1 (criterion 1) to its floor (w1=1) still leaves lead = 3+8−6+1 = 6 > 0. **No single-criterion weight change within the 1–5 scale changes the winner.** The gaps to (c), (e), (d) are larger still. This is a robust result, not a near-tie artifact.

### Conclusion C7: Outcome/bias auditing is the recommended design; disclosure-only ranks last

→ outcome/bias-auditing is the recommended design for the anti-discrimination goal specifically, and disclosure-only, as literally proposed, is not required to achieve it and is not improved by being layered on top of it as a substitute — though see the Phase 5 adversarial pass, Cluster C, on complementarity rather than pure substitution

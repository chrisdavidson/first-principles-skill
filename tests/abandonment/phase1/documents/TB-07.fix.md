# How Much Land Would 10 Million People's Electricity Take, in Solar Panels?

## 1. Ground truths and assumptions

**Per-capita electricity demand.** National averages span roughly 2,500 kWh/yr (lower-middle-income) to 8,000+ kWh/yr (high-income, electricity-intensive economies like the US at ~12,000). I anchor on a mid-income country at **5,000 kWh/person/year** (comparable to Spain, Poland, or Argentina) as the central case, with 2,500 and 8,000 as bounds. *Empirical, moderately well-established (IEA/EIA statistics), but genuinely varies by country.*

**Solar resource and capacity factor.** Utility-scale PV capacity factors run from ~15% (cloudy temperate zones, e.g., Germany, UK) to ~25–27% (deserts/high-insolation belts, e.g., Southwest US, North Africa, Australia). Central case: **20%**. *Well-established physically (a function of latitude and cloud cover), though the exact number is site-specific.*

**Panel efficiency.** Commercial silicon modules today: **20–22%** conversion efficiency. *Well-established engineering fact.*

**Land-use intensity for utility-scale PV.** Including row spacing (to avoid self-shading), inverter/substation pads, internal roads, and setbacks, empirical surveys (NREL and others) give **1.5–4 hectares per installed MW**, central **2 ha/MW**. This is the single most uncertain, technology/design-dependent input — tracking systems, panel density, and terrain all move it. *Empirical estimate, moderate confidence.*

## 2. The Fermi chain

**Step 1 — Total annual demand:**
10,000,000 people × 5,000 kWh/person/yr = **50 TWh/yr** (50,000 GWh/yr).

**Step 2 — Required installed capacity**, given capacity factor CF:
Capacity = Annual energy ÷ (8,760 hr × CF)
= 50,000,000 MWh ÷ (8,760 × 0.20) ≈ **28.5 GW** (central; 38 GW at CF=15%, 22.8 GW at CF=25%).

**Step 3 — Land area**, given land intensity L (ha/MW):
Area = Capacity × L
= 28,500 MW × 2 ha/MW ≈ 57,000 ha ≈ **570 km²** (central case).
Low bound (CF 25%, L 1.5 ha/MW): ~340 km². High bound (CF 15%, L 4 ha/MW): ~1,520 km².

## 3. Theoretical-limit framing (bare-panel floor)

Stripping away spacing/roads/setbacks, the absolute physical floor is set only by irradiance and panel efficiency:
Area = Energy ÷ (irradiance × efficiency)
= 50×10⁹ kWh ÷ (1,700 kWh/m²/yr × 0.20) ≈ 1.47×10⁸ m² ≈ **~150 km²**.

So the bare panels themselves need only ~150 km² — the realistic utility-scale footprint (340–1,520 km², central 570 km²) is **3–4× larger**, meaning panels occupy only ~25–30% of the fenced site. This gap is not wasted land in a strict sense — it's largely native vegetation/grazing land between rows — but it's the gap between "physically possible" and "how solar farms are actually built."

## 4. Result and sensitivity

Central estimate: **~570 km²**, defensible range **~150 km² (theoretical floor) to ~350–1,500 km² (realistic range)**, before storage. Scaling for demand: a high-consumption country (8,000 kWh/capita) pushes the central case to ~910 km²; a low-consumption one (2,500 kWh/capita) drops it to ~285 km².

As a share of national territory: for a mid-sized country like Portugal (92,212 km²), 570 km² is **~0.6%**; the full range spans **~0.3%–2.6%**. For a smaller, land-constrained country like Belgium (30,528 km²), the same central figure is **~1.9%**, ranging up to **~8%** at the pessimistic end. The result is highly sensitive to latitude/climate (via CF) and to per-capita demand — a 2–3× swing in either input produces a comparable swing in land area.

## 5. Feasibility verdict

**Technical:** The calculation above is an *energy-matching*, not *power-matching*, exercise — it ignores that solar delivers ~20% of its capacity unevenly across day/night and seasons. Meeting instantaneous demand year-round with solar *alone* (no other generation) requires either massive overbuilding plus multi-day/seasonal storage, or backup capacity — both of which raise the effective footprint well above the 570 km² figure, potentially by 2–4×. Grid/transmission upgrades to move gigawatt-scale power from favorable sites to demand centers are a further, non-trivial cost layer.

**Economic:** PV capital costs have fallen ~80–90% since 2010, making the *panels* cheap; land acquisition, permitting, and transmission interconnection are now often the binding costs, especially near population centers.

**Land-use competition:** At <1–3% of national territory in most plausible cases, aggregate land is not the binding constraint — but *siting* is: prime flat, grid-adjacent, low-ecological-value land is scarcer than the aggregate percentage suggests, creating real conflicts with agriculture and habitat in densely populated regions.

**Social/political:** Local opposition to greenfield siting, land tenure disputes, and visual/ecological objections are typically larger practical barriers than the physical land budget.

**Second-order effects:** Adding storage doesn't meaningfully change land footprint (batteries are land-light) but does raise capital cost substantially. Demand growth scales land requirements linearly. Rooftop and dual-use siting (agrivoltaics, parking canopies, reservoirs) could plausibly absorb 20–40% of the requirement without new greenfield land, meaningfully easing the siting-conflict problem even though it doesn't change the underlying physics.

## 6. Confidence

**High confidence (physical/engineering facts):** panel efficiency, irradiance-driven capacity factors, the energy-to-capacity arithmetic. **Lower confidence (empirical/economic estimates):** land-use intensity per MW (varies 2–3× by design choice), per-capita demand (country-specific), and especially the storage/overbuild multiplier needed for a *solar-only* grid, which is the most consequential and least certain number in this analysis. **Overall verdict: land area is not the binding constraint on solar feasibility for a 10-million-person country — it typically requires well under 2–3% of national territory even under pessimistic assumptions — but intermittency, transmission, and siting friction, not raw acreage, are the real feasibility bottlenecks.**

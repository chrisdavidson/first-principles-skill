# 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given a 47-minute CI pipeline whose internal time-composition is unmeasured, and a batching workaround that may be driven by duration, non-duration factors, or both, which lever — hardware throughput, pipeline architecture, or test-suite/process composition — actually removes the rate-limiting constraint, and can that lever be identified without first measuring the pipeline's internal composition?

**Success criteria:**
1. Names the specific ground-truth facts (bottleneck-composition telemetry) required to determine whether hardware acceleration would reduce the 47-minute figure, and states which we currently lack.
2. Distinguishes "CI duration is long" from "the merge-blocking feedback loop is slow," and evaluates hardware against both framings separately.
3. Produces a traceable verdict — right / wrong / premature — on buying faster runners, with the verdict's confidence explicitly stated.
4. Traces second-order effects of a hardware-only fix specifically onto the batching behavior (does it stop, and at what marginal speedup).
5. Provides an order-of-magnitude cost/leverage comparison between hardware and software-side levers.
6. States exactly what leadership should measure first, if the verdict is contingent on unmeasured facts.

# 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Naive full-compute-boundedness framing

**What was tried:** Initially considered treating the entire 47 minutes as CPU-bound, i.e., assuming a hardware upgrade would linearly fix the whole figure.

**Why abandoned:** GT-5's decomposition and GT-6 (Amdahl's Law) jointly rule this out as a general claim — no realistic CI pipeline has zero checkout/queue/network overhead, so a proportional-speedup assumption is contradicted by definitional and mathematical fact, not merely unlikely.

**What it ruled out:** Saves any future reviewer from re-deriving "just buy faster hardware, it'll fix the number" as a default; the correct framing is always "up to the unmeasured compute-bound fraction," never "the whole figure."

### Dead End: Inventing numeric probabilities for the seven candidate bottleneck categories

**What was tried:** Considered assigning illustrative probability weights to each of the seven candidate bottleneck categories (compute, flaky-retries, serialization, network, queueing, I/O, scope-creep) to produce a single point-estimate "most likely cause."

**Why abandoned:** This would manufacture false precision over data this analysis was explicitly instructed to treat as a first-class unknown rather than invent numbers for; no evidence exists to differentiate the seven categories' relative likelihood.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future reviewer from mistaking an invented probability distribution for actual telemetry; the honest state is "unknown, gating the decision" (GT-4?), not "60% likely compute-bound."

### Dead End: Treating GT-2?'s self-reported motive as dispositive proof of pure duration-avoidance

**What was tried:** Considered promoting "batching is purely duration-driven" from a hypothesis to a confirmed ground truth, on the strength of the situation's own wording ("to avoid triggering the pipeline repeatedly").

**Why abandoned:** Self-reported workaround motives are known to conflate multiple frustrations (duration, flake-avoidance, dislike of unrelated pipeline noise); this is not independently verified and stays a live, unresolved rival on chain C2 rather than a ground truth.

**What it ruled out:** Prevents the analysis from treating leadership's/developers' framing as settled fact when it is exactly the kind of claim the analysis is meant to test.

### Dead End: Recommending flaky-test remediation as the primary first move

**What was tried:** Considered leading the recommendation with flaky-test fixing (Option 3 in the trade-off), since it directly targets a plausible non-duration batching driver (C2).

**Why abandoned:** Ruled out by chain C5's trade-off ranking — Option 3 scored 57, below both the fast-fail/slow-tail split (77) and the software-fixes/profile-first tie (68) — even though it addresses a real candidate driver, it is not the highest-leverage first move under the locked weights.

**What it ruled out:** Prevents over-indexing on one plausible-sounding driver (flakiness) at the expense of the option (pipeline splitting) that works regardless of which of the seven candidate bottlenecks turns out to dominate. Flaky-test remediation is retained as a parallel-track disposition (pre-mortem Cluster E) rather than the lead recommendation.

# 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not purchase faster CI runners as the first move. First close the GT-4? gap: instrument the existing pipeline for roughly 1–2 weeks to get stage-level timing (queue-wait, checkout/dependency-fetch, build, test-compute, artifact I/O, retry count). In parallel — because it does not require waiting on that data — begin designing a fast-fail/slow-tail pipeline split so merge-blocking feedback is decoupled from full-suite duration; this was the single highest-scoring option in the locked-weight trade-off, and its case does not depend on which of the seven candidate bottlenecks turns out to dominate. Treat caching, test parallelization, change-based test selection, and flaky-test remediation as the next-in-line investments once profiling shows where the time actually goes. Revisit a hardware upgrade only if profiling shows the pipeline is genuinely and dominantly compute-bound, and even then size the purchase against the measured compute-bound fraction, not the full 47 minutes (chain C6, C5).

**Key insight:** Because CI wall-clock time is, by definition, a sum of execution-speed-invariant segments (queueing, network fetch, artifact I/O, serialized stage dependencies) plus a compute-bound segment, and Amdahl's Law caps any speedup from faster processors at the inverse of the non-compute fraction, "buy faster runners" cannot be evaluated as a single, self-evidently-good lever — its effectiveness is bounded by a currently-unmeasured quantity that could be large or could be small (chain C1).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The recommended sequencing costs a real, if small, delay; if there is genuine organizational time pressure, that 1–2 week diagnostic window is itself a cost this analysis cannot price with the information available (no chain — flagged assumption only). The fast-fail/slow-tail split and software fixes are cheaper per unit of improvement than hardware but land more slowly than signing a purchase order (chain C5). Even a well-chosen non-hardware fix may not fully resolve batching if code-review capacity becomes the next binding constraint, or if leadership measures success by nominal pipeline-minutes rather than merge cadence or diff size — in which case the workaround could persist invisibly even after a technically successful CI fix (chain C4).

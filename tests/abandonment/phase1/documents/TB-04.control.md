## 1. Problem Essence
**Core question:** What city-level characteristics should determine whether free transit, congestion pricing, cycle lanes, or a combination is the right lever to cut commuter car trips 20% in 3 years?
**Success criteria:** (a) a framework mapping city traits to lever choice, not a universal winner; (b) each lever's plausible 3-year mode-shift magnitude is sized; (c) failure modes and second-order effects are named.

## 2. Assumptions Table (condensed)
| Assumption | Type | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Mode choice follows generalized cost (money + time + comfort/safety + habit) | physical/behavioral law | Accepted — foundational |
| Free transit mainly converts drivers, not walkers/cyclists/existing riders | untested belief | Challenged — evidence suggests substantial "induced demand" from non-drivers (GT3) |
| Congestion pricing cuts trips, not just shifts their timing | convention | Partially true — London/Stockholm show net VMT reduction, not pure reshuffling (GT2), but off-peak reshuffling is real and partially offsets |
| Cycle lanes only work in flat, mild-climate, dense cities | convention | Falsified as absolute — Netherlands (rain) and Oulu, Finland (snow) sustain high winter cycling; density and network connectivity matter more than climate (GT4) |
| 3 years is enough time to fully build/launch any of the three | current constraint | False for congestion pricing (GT5); truer for paint-and-bollard cycle lanes; conditional for transit (fare removal is fast, capacity expansion is not) |

## 3. Ground Truths
- GT1: Short-run price elasticity of driving demand is low, roughly −0.1 to −0.3 ? — small price changes move few trips quickly.
- GT2: London's 2003 congestion charge cut in-zone traffic ~15–30% within year one ? — pricing has the largest single-lever, fastest-visible effect where enforcement is enforceable.
- GT3: Fare-free transit trials (Tallinn, several U.S. cities) show ridership gains concentrated among former walkers, cyclists, and existing riders taking more trips; car-to-transit conversion is modest (~5–15% of new riders) ? — cost is rarely the binding constraint on driving; frequency/coverage/reliability is.
- GT4: Protected bike lanes shift mode share materially only as part of a connected network; isolated segments show weak effect ? .
- GT5: Congestion pricing typically needs 2–4 years for technology, legal authority, and political buy-in before launch ? — a 3-year deadline is tight-to-infeasible for pricing alone.
- GT6: Cycle lane construction is materially cheaper and faster per mile than added transit capacity ? .
- GT7: Generalized cost = money + (time × value-of-time) + comfort/safety disutility + habit inertia — treated as foundational, not `?`.

## 4. Derivation Chains
**C1 (magnitude, transit):** GT3 + GT7 → free transit removes only the money term, not the dominant time/reliability/comfort terms that keep drivers driving → a fare-free policy alone plausibly shifts low single-digit percent of car trips within 3 years, with most new ridership drawn from non-drivers, straining vehicle/frequency capacity → **unlikely to hit 20% alone. Confidence: MEDIUM** (rests on GT3?, no live rival ruled out for cities with severe pre-existing transit under-capacity, where free fares plus frequency boosts could do more).

**C2 (magnitude, pricing):** GT1 + GT2 → pricing directly raises the money and hassle terms of driving in the priced zone/time, producing the largest and fastest-visible single-lever cut in observed cities → but GT5 shows typical lead time exceeds a 3-year window for a city without existing infrastructure → **pricing has the highest per-driver leverage but the weakest 3-year feasibility unless a simpler form (e.g., cordon toll on existing tolling infrastructure) is used. Confidence: MEDIUM** (GT5? is the flip fact — verify local implementation timeline before committing).

**C3 (magnitude, cycle lanes):** GT4 + GT6 → fast, cheap build enables 3-year delivery, but effect is capped by trip distance/topography and requires network connectivity to convert car trips rather than just capturing existing bike/walk trips → **cycle lanes reliably deliver modest (single-digit) but fast, durable mode share gains, concentrated in trips under ~5km. Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH**.

**C4 (combination logic):** C1 + C2 + C3 → no single lever plausibly reaches 20% in 3 years at typical elasticities → a combination is structurally required, with the weighting set by which generalized-cost term (GT7) is most binding locally → **the decision is not "which lever" but "which mix, sequenced by city-specific binding constraint." Confidence: HIGH** (recompute: three MEDIUM chains summing sub-additively to below 20% each is arithmetically consistent, not a rounding artifact).

**Second-order effects (actor + time lens):** Pricing revenue can fund transit/cycling (positive loop) but drivers may reroute onto untolled streets (negative externality, near-term); free transit's ridership surge can degrade service quality if unfunded, eroding the switch it aimed to produce (medium-term); cycle lane networks raise adjacent property desirability, risking displacement (long-term, equity-relevant).

**Pre-mortem (top clusters):** (1) *Political reversal* — congestion pricing faces backlash before enforcement matures (fatal; mitigate with revenue-visible earmarking and phased zones). (2) *Underfunded free transit* — fare loss without replacement funding collapses service (fatal; mitigate by pairing with a funded capacity increase, e.g., partial pricing revenue). (3) *Disconnected bike network* — piecemeal lanes never reach critical mass (costly-survivable; mitigate by sequencing a spine network first).

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning
Rejected framing: "pick the single most effective lever." Abandoned because C1–C3 show no lever individually clears 20% at typical elasticities (GT1, GT3, GT4), and because "effectiveness" varies too much by density/geography to rank universally.

## 6. Conclusion
**Recommended approach:** Do not choose one lever — sequence a combination, weighted by which term of generalized cost (GT7) is most binding locally (chain C4).
- **High existing transit capacity + weak reliability:** lead with fare-free/reduced fare + frequency investment (C1).
- **Enforceable central business district + tolling infrastructure precedent:** lead with congestion pricing, revenue-funding the other two (C2), accepting it likely lands late in the 3-year window (GT5).
- **Compact geography, short average commute:** lead with a connected cycle-lane spine, fastest to deliver (C3).
- **Most mid-sized cities:** cycle lanes first (speed), pricing revenue second (funds the rest), fare policy last (needs capacity first) — sequencing driven by GT5's build-time asymmetry.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM overall — the framework's logic (C4) is HIGH, but city-specific magnitudes rest on `?`-marked literature estimates (GT1–GT6) not verified against primary sources in this run; a rival reading — that free transit alone suffices in a transit-under-supplied city — remains live and is not ruled out here.

**Success criteria:**
- Names which component(s) of the 47 minutes (compute, serial critical path, queue wait, external-dependency wait, flaky-retry overhead) faster hardware can and cannot address, with the limit stated formally.
- States whether "batching to avoid triggering CI" is explained by the pipeline's raw duration or by *where* that duration sits in the workflow (a blocking synchronous gate vs. something else), and names what would settle that question.
- Produces a ranked recommendation among buy-runners-only, restructure-only, buy-and-restructure, flaky/test-selection-only, and do-nothing, with the ranking traceable to named criteria and weights.
- Names the specific diagnostic data leadership should gather before authorizing a recurring hardware spend, and the condition under which buying runners actually is the right primary call.

# 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** Amdahl's Law: for a workload with non-parallelizable (serial) fraction *f*, applying *N* parallel processing units gives Speedup = 1/(f + (1−f)/N); as *N*→∞, Speedup → 1/f. — source: Cornell Virtual Workshop, "Parallel Programming Concepts and High Performance Computing: Amdahl's Law" (cvw.cac.cornell.edu/parallel/efficiency/amdahls-law); read-at-source: formula "S = 1/(Fs + Fp/N)" and limit "lim(N→∞) S = 1/Fs" quoted directly from the fetched page. Type: physical law.
- **GT-2** DORA's research (2016–2017 data) found that teams maintaining three or fewer active branches, merging to trunk at least once a day, and eliminating code freezes/integration phases achieve higher software delivery and operational performance (speed, stability, availability) than teams using long-lived branches. — source: dora.dev, "Capabilities: Trunk-based development" (dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/); read-at-source: quoted bullets on "three or fewer active branches," "merge branches to trunk at least once a day," "eliminate code freezes and integration phases," from the fetched page.
- **GT-3?** Large, infrequent merges are harder to review and validate than small frequent ones — reviewers procrastinate, defects escape inspection, regression risk grows, and delayed integration produces large risky merges that break builds and slow feedback. — cited to: Atlassian and related trunk-based-development sources; reported-by-delegate: WebSearch summary; cited sources not opened directly by this analysis.
- **GT-4?** At Google-scale, only a small fraction of test-target executions show flaky (inconsistent pass/fail) behavior, and an even smaller fraction of those reflect a genuine breakage or fix, which is part of why Google invested in selecting which tests to run rather than simply running more compute against the full corpus on every change. — cited to: Memon & Gao, "Taming Google-Scale Continuous Testing," ICSE-SEIP 2017; reported-by-delegate: WebSearch summary. **Phase 3 failure record:** the source was located (research.google.com/pubs/archive/45861.pdf, redirected to a static Google host) and fetched in this session, but the fetch tool could not extract readable text from the PDF (binary/corrupted-appearing content) — the asserted figures were not confirmed at source in this session.
- **GT-5?** Industry reports describe material flaky-test costs elsewhere: one case study found engineers spend roughly 1.28% of their time repairing flaky tests; Microsoft's investigation of flaky-test-caused failures for one product cost roughly $7.2M; and one GitHub-commit analysis found about 1 in 11 commits triggers a failure attributable to flakiness. — cited to: aggregated industry/academic sources (arXiv 2504.16777 and related); reported-by-delegate: WebSearch summary; not opened directly. Used only as illustrative magnitude, not load-bearing.
- **GT-6** A CI pipeline's total wall-clock duration for a single run equals queue/wait time before execution starts, plus the length of its critical path — the longest chain of stages that must run sequentially because each depends on the prior one's output — regardless of how many independent branches run in parallel across additional workers. — source: direct derivation from GT-1's serial/parallel decomposition, applied to a job DAG via the critical-path/PERT scheduling identity; read-at-source: not an external document — a mathematical construction, checked by direct reasoning rather than a citation. Type: physical law (structural/definitional).
- **GT-7?** Engineering accounts commonly report large wall-clock reductions from dependency caching, test parallelization/sharding by historical duration, and converting serialized stage execution into explicit parallel job graphs (examples cited: 45→8 min, 22→7 min, 20→11 min pipelines). — cited to: several engineering blog posts (oneuptime.com, getpanto.ai, aws.plainenglish.io, michaeleakins.com, et al.); reported-by-delegate: WebSearch summary; none opened directly. Used only as directional context for the fix menu in C3, never as direct evidence for this org (see A10, Dead End 1).
- **GT-8** A synchronous CI gate that developers must wait on before merging imposes a cost proportional to (wait time absorbed per invocation) × (number of invocations); if the gate is mandatory and blocking, the per-invocation wait is a fixed tax paid every time feedback is sought, independent of what fraction of that time is compute vs. queueing vs. serial dependency. — source: direct arithmetic consequence of "synchronous blocking gate," given that premise; type: physical law/definition (mathematical identity, contingent on the premise itself being true here — captured as A3).
- **GT-9** The scenario as given states developers have started batching multiple changes into single merges, and this practice is attributed (by the org) to avoiding triggering the 47-minute pipeline repeatedly. — source: the user's problem statement itself; read-at-source: this is the literal input to this analysis, read directly, not a secondary report.

# 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End 1: Skip diagnosis and assume test execution dominates (per GT-7?'s industry pattern), recommending sharding alone

**What was tried:** Using GT-7?'s reported industry pipeline-optimization outcomes (caching/sharding producing large reductions elsewhere) as direct evidence for what dominates *this* org's 47 minutes, and recommending test sharding as the sole fix.

**Why abandoned:** GT-7? is reported-by-delegate and describes other organizations' pipelines, not this one; using it as direct evidence for this org's time composition without a ground truth specific to this org's situation is the reasoning-by-analogy the methodology bars.

**What it ruled out:** any recommendation branch that substitutes outside benchmarks for this org's own diagnostic data (C5).

### Dead End 2: Recommend buying faster runners immediately as "cheap insurance," regardless of root cause

**What was tried:** Treating a runner upgrade as a low-regret action on the theory that faster CI is good to have even if it doesn't address the dominant bottleneck.

**Why abandoned:** Contradicts C4's second-order analysis — an unconditional hardware spend risks a sunk-cost/attribution problem (pre-mortem Cluster D, below) and delays correct diagnosis (Cluster A), and C3's trade-off scores it as strictly dominated by restructuring at every plausible reweighting (the flip test found no single-criterion move that reverses this).

**What it ruled out:** treating "runners are cheap insurance" as sufficient justification without checking whether they address the actual bottleneck.

### Dead End 3: Accept large-batch merges as the new equilibrium and invest only in making big merges safer

**What was tried:** Recommending no change to merge cadence, only more automated testing applied to large merges, treating batching as an acceptable adaptation to optimize around.

**Why abandoned:** Contradicts GT-2 directly — DORA's findings link small-batch, frequent-trunk-merge practice, not safer large batches, to higher delivery and stability performance; optimizing around large batches runs against this established finding rather than with it.

**What it ruled out:** any recommendation branch that treats large-batch merging as the stable target state rather than a symptom to reverse.

# 6. Conclusion

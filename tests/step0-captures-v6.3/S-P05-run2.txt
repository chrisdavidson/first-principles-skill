# First-Principles Trade-Off Analysis: Database Selection for Transactional E-Commerce

## Recommendation: **PostgreSQL (managed)** — weighted score **90** vs DynamoDB **73** vs MongoDB **69**. HIGH confidence.

## The decision in one line
All three databases fit the $3k/month budget and handle 500 writes/second comfortably — so **cost and throughput don't decide this**. The differentiator is **transactional correctness fit**, and that's where PostgreSQL wins decisively.

## Weighted scoring

|---|---|---|---|---|

## Why PostgreSQL wins

- **Correctness is the essence.** E-commerce order processing is irreducibly multi-entity (cart → inventory → order → payment must be atomic). PostgreSQL delivers multi-statement ACID as its **default operating mode**, while MongoDB (since 4.0, but bounded by 60s/16MB limits and a performance penalty) and DynamoDB (TransactWriteItems, capped at 100 items/4MB and billed at **2× WCU**) deliver it as a constrained, easier-to-misuse add-on.
- **Cost can't rescue DynamoDB.** Its headline ~$237/mo floor inflates fast once you count the multiple GSIs e-commerce access patterns require (each multiplies write cost) plus the 2× transactional-write surcharge.
- **Data-model fit.** The relational order/inventory/product/payment graph maps natively to PostgreSQL.

## Verified cost floors (all ≤ $3k)
- **PostgreSQL** (RDS Multi-AZ): ~$440–900/month
- **DynamoDB** (provisioned, base writes): ~$237/month — *but rises materially with GSIs + transactional writes*
- **MongoDB** Atlas (M40-class): within budget

## Robustness
PostgreSQL leads by ~23%, well outside the 10% near-tie band. No single weight change flips the result.

## When to reconsider
- **DynamoDB** becomes load-bearing if sustained writes exceed ~10,000/s with simple single-entity access patterns.
- **MongoDB** improves if the data is genuinely document-shaped with rare cross-entity transactions.

**Caveat:** Operational-simplicity scores assume a typical team; the actual team skill profile wasn't specified. Even a DynamoDB-native team, however, couldn't close a 17-point gap rooted in correctness and data-model fit.

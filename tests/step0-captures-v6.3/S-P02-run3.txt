The first-principles agent completed the inversion. Here's what it found:

## The inverted claim
"Faster shipping does **not** improve retention" — it leaves it flat or actively lowers it.

## When the assumption breaks down — 3 watch conditions

**1. Wrong lever (most likely breakdown)**
Your dominant churn cause isn't something shipping speed touches — it's price, market fit, a missing capability, or a user segment velocity can't reach. The claim silently assumes your retention bottleneck is addressable by incremental shipping.
→ *Test:* tag your churn reasons. If the top reasons aren't "stale product / missing fixes," cadence is the wrong lever.

**2. No perceived value**
You ship faster, but increments carry no user-visible value, or users never learn the releases happened (silent backend ships, no changelogs). The link depends on *value delivered and perceived* — not *releases counted*.

**3. Change cost exceeds change benefit**
Past some rate, faster shipping raises defect/regression rates and imposes change fatigue, retraining, and broken-workflow costs. The real relationship is an **inverted-U, not a line** — "faster is always better" is false at the limit, even if two weeks is currently fine.

## The load-bearing caveat
Every precondition is **unverified for your product** — the agent had no data on your churn drivers, quality trends, or perceived value. The fastest way to ground this:

- Pull your **last two quarters of churn reasons** → if dominated by non-velocity causes, the assumption is *already* broken for you now.
- Pull your **defect-rate-vs-cadence trend** → if quality degrades as cadence rises, you're on the downslope of the inverted-U.

**Bottom line:** the assumption holds only if you're shipping the *right things*, at *constant quality*, to a *segment velocity can reach*. Frequency alone guarantees none of those three.

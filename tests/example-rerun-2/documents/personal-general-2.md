## Bottom line

Pay down a meaningful chunk of the mortgage, but not all of it. On the numbers, **a 50/50–60/40 split (lean toward paydown) is the risk-adjusted optimum** for this specific decision — a fixed 10-year horizon, a guaranteed 6.25% hurdle with no tax shield, and $60k that's genuinely discretionary (emergency fund already separate). Here's the reasoning chain.

## 1. The core math: guaranteed 6.25% vs. after-tax equity returns

**Option A (mortgage paydown) is mathematically equivalent to buying a risk-free, tax-free bond yielding 6.25%, compounding, for as long as the loan remains outstanding.** Extra principal removes future interest at the loan's rate; since there's no tax deduction in play, the full 6.25% is the real, after-tax hurdle. Over 10 years:

$60,000 × (1.0625)^10 ≈ **$110,000** in avoided interest / built-up equity.

**Option B (taxable brokerage)** has to clear that hurdle *after* two layers of tax drag that a tax-advantaged account wouldn't have: dividends taxed at 18% every year (not deferred), and the capital gain taxed at 18% on sale at year 10. Running the actual year-by-year compounding (1.3% dividend yield, 18% dividend tax annually, 18% LTCG at exit):

| Nominal pretax equity return | After-tax value at year 10 | After-tax CAGR |
|---|---|---|
| 6% | $98,500 | 5.08% |
| 7% | $107,100 | 5.96% |
| **7.33%** | **$110,000** | **6.25% ← breakeven** |
| 8% | $116,400 | 6.85% |
| 9% | $126,500 | 7.75% |
| 10% | $137,500 | 8.65% |

**Breakeven: equities need ~7.33% nominal pretax return (≈6.25% after-tax) just to tie the guaranteed payoff.** Historically, the S&P 500's long-run nominal average is ~10%, which clears this comfortably. But that average is not the relevant benchmark for a 10-year-starting-now bet — forward-looking estimates from current valuation levels (CAPE in the high 20s/low 30s) generally cluster in the 5–8% nominal range for the next decade. **The breakeven sits almost exactly in the middle of the plausible forward-return distribution, not conservatively below it.** This is the single most important number in the whole analysis: it means equities are roughly a coin-flip against the mortgage on expected value alone, before even pricing in risk.

## 2. Risk-adjusted comparison, not just expected value

6.25% guaranteed is not comparable to "7–10% expected" without pricing the variance. Two things matter:

- **It's a better risk-free rate than you can get in the risk-free market.** Treasuries/CDs are roughly 4–4.5% today. Paying down the mortgage effectively lets you "earn" 6.25% with zero volatility — a rate no bond portfolio offers. That's a real, uncontested win versus any safe alternative.
- **Sequence-of-returns risk over exactly 10 years is real and historically material.** Rolling 10-year nominal S&P 500 CAGRs (with dividends) have ranged from roughly -1% to +19% depending on the starting valuation. The 2000–2009 window realized close to a flat-to-negative nominal decade. A 10-year horizon is short enough that a bad starting point (which current elevated valuations raise the odds of) can leave the equity option behind the guaranteed 6.25%, not just by a little — potentially by tens of thousands of dollars on $60k.

So on a risk-adjusted basis (not just point-estimate expected value), the mortgage paydown is the stronger claim than the raw "stocks return more on average" intuition suggests, specifically *because* the horizon is fixed and short and the guaranteed rate is unusually high.

## 3. Liquidity and optionality — a real cost, not a footnote

Principal paid into the mortgage is **locked**: it can only be recovered via sale (minus ~5–6% transaction costs) or a refi/HELOC (minus closing costs and at whatever rate prevails then — possibly worse than 6.25%). Brokerage assets stay liquid at all times, sellable in a day for the cost of LTCG tax only. This optionality has value independent of the return math: job loss, a better investment opportunity, a large unplanned expense — all are handled trivially from a brokerage account and painfully (or not at all without a lender's cooperation) from home equity. This is a real argument for not putting the full $60k into the house, even if the rate math were a dead heat.

## 4. Behavioral factors — real, secondary

Debt aversion and "sleep at night" value from a smaller mortgage are legitimate but shouldn't override the math unless the user says they weight this heavily. Flagging it, not weighting it here.

## 5. Second-order effects

- **If the home is sold before the extra principal would otherwise have been "earned out,"** nothing is lost — the paydown is returned as higher proceeds at sale — but it's recovered in one illiquid lump at a specific future, uncertain date, not on demand.
- **Concentration:** for most households, home equity is already a large, leveraged, single-asset, single-market bet. Adding $60k more into that same asset *increases* concentration risk; directing new money to a broad index fund *improves* overall diversification. This favors Option B independent of the rate comparison, unless the home is a small share of net worth.
- **Refinancing flexibility:** a smaller balance is marginally easier to refinance or sell cleanly, but at 27 years remaining this effect is minor over a 10-year window.
- **Net worth volatility:** Option A reduces net worth volatility (less leveraged exposure to a single asset); Option B increases it (added market exposure). If anything in the household's broader balance sheet is already stretched or illiquid, this cuts toward A.

## Recommendation and the key trade-off

**The key trade-off, stated explicitly:** a guaranteed, illiquid 6.25% with no further upside vs. an uncertain, liquid, tax-drag-adjusted return whose breakeven (7.33% nominal) sits right in the middle — not safely below — the range of plausible 10-year forward equity returns.

Given that trade-off, and that this $60k is genuinely surplus (liquidity floor already covered elsewhere):

- **Don't go 100% mortgage.** It worsens an already-concentrated illiquid position and forfeits all liquidity on money you don't need to lock away, for a bet (equities underperforming 6.25%) that's plausible but not favored enough to justify giving up all optionality.
- **Don't go 100% equities either.** The breakeven math doesn't give a comfortable margin of safety at this specific horizon, and a bad decade-start is a known historical risk, not a tail scenario.
- **Recommended split: roughly 50/50 to 60/40, mortgage-leaning.** Put $30–36k toward principal (locking in a guaranteed, above-risk-free-rate return and reducing concentration risk's marginal growth), and $24–30k into the index fund (preserving liquidity, keeping upside if the next decade clears the 7.33% bar, and avoiding further real-estate concentration). This isn't a hedge for its own sake — it's the correct response to a breakeven number that doesn't clearly favor either pure option over this specific 10-year window.

If the horizon were materially longer (20–30 years instead of 10), the recommendation would tilt harder toward equities, since the breakeven's "middle of the distribution" problem is a short-horizon issue — over multiple decades the historical equity risk premium over 6.25% becomes a much higher-confidence bet.

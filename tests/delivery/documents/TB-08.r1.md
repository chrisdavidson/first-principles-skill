# First-Principles Analysis: Catalyst Yield Report (62% → 71%, n=3)

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given only two summary yield percentages (a 62% baseline and a 71% catalyst-condition figure, the latter based on three runs) with no reported variance, confound controls, or replication/selection information, what is the maximum conclusion about the catalyst's causal effect that this evidence actually supports, and what claims would constitute statistical or causal overreach?

**Success criteria:**
1. Section 6 states, as separately itemized "may conclude" and "may not conclude" claims, each phrased as a verb+subject+outcome triplet checkable against the report's stated evidentiary content (not against outside domain knowledge).
2. The overall confidence rating is tied explicitly to the three-axis definition (Inputs / Inference / Rivals), naming the missing within-group variance data as the specific shortfall driving the rating.
3. At least one Derivation Chain produces a numeric or logical bound derived from n=3 (degrees of freedom, or a noise-vs-signal bracket), not an intuition-based assertion.
4. Section 6 names concrete additional data or experiments that would raise the claim's confidence, each tied to a specific named gap in sections 2–3.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: The report alone provides no computable statistical basis for the yield difference

GT-2 (df=4 for n=3 vs n=3) + GT-4 (no variance data given)
→ even if within-group variance were later supplied, a two-group comparison with only 4 degrees of freedom has inherently low statistical power to detect a moderate effect against noise
→ with variance entirely unreported, no p-value, confidence interval, or effect-size test can be computed from the two percentages given
→ the report as stated provides no computable statistical basis for concluding the yield difference is non-random

### Conclusion C2: The observed 9-point gap cannot be distinguished from ordinary noise without the reaction's own variance data

GT-8? (industry inter-run CV ≈5–15%) + C1 (no reaction-specific variance reported)

→ the bracket [1.2, 3.6] straddles the conventional threshold for calling a gap a discernible signal, so whether the report's numbers look like a real effect or like ordinary noise depends entirely on a reaction-specific variability figure the report never supplies

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Naive point-estimate comparison (71 > 62, therefore real)
**What was tried:** Treating the bare arithmetic difference between the two percentages as sufficient evidence that a real effect occurred.
**Why abandoned:** Ignores sampling variability entirely — a non-sequitur once GT-2 (df=4, low power) and GT-4 (no variance reported) are considered; the same numbers are shown in chain C2 to be compatible with pure noise under plausible variability.
**What it ruled out:** Saves a reader from treating "any numerical increase" as proof that "something worked."

### Dead End: n=3 is enough because it's standard lab practice
**What was tried:** Accepting the sample size via appeal to domain convention (A-4) rather than statistical adequacy.
**Why abandoned:** Convention is contradicted by the mathematical fact of df=4 and correspondingly low power (GT-2); a practice being common does not make it statistically sufficient.
**What it ruled out:** Saves re-litigating "but triplicates are normal" as though it settled the significance question — it settles a reporting norm, not a power question.

### Dead End: Assume this was a controlled experiment by default
**What was tried:** Assuming the framing ("a new catalyst raises yield") implied blinding, randomization, or confound control by default.
**Why abandoned:** Nothing in the report states any confound control, and the prompt explicitly lists reagent lot, operator, analytical method, scale, and temperature/time as open questions — directly contradicting the assumption (GT-6?, GT-7?).
**What it ruled out:** Saves assuming rigor that was never demonstrated; this is exactly the gap chain C3 is built to surface.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Treat the report as a preliminary, hypothesis-generating observation, not a demonstrated causal finding. It is legitimate to say the three logged catalyst runs numerically exceeded the cited baseline and that this is worth a properly controlled follow-up; it is not legitimate to say the catalyst has been shown to increase yield, to cite the 9-point gap as a validated effect size, or to make resourcing/scale-up decisions on this evidence alone (chains C1, C3, C4).

- Report per-run values and standard deviation (or at minimum a range) for both conditions, not just the two means (chain C1).
- Confirm whether the 62% baseline was measured under the same protocol and a comparable run count (chain C3).
- Disclose the full run log for the catalyst condition, including any excluded or unreported runs (chain C3).
- Hold reagent lot, operator, analytical method, scale, temperature, and time constant — or explicitly vary and block on them — across a larger, pre-specified number of replicates (chain C3).
- Increase replicate count enough that the noise/signal bracket in chain C2 collapses to one side of the threshold rather than straddling it (chain C2).

**Key insight:** The sample-size problem and the confound problem are independent failures that each alone would block a causal claim — and the estimate bracket shows the very numbers cited (62%→71%) are numerically compatible with pure run-to-run measurement noise at plausible real-world variability levels. The report's headline figures do not, by themselves, distinguish "the catalyst works" from "nothing happened" (chain C2).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Accepting this cautious framing forgoes the rhetorical value of an apparently clean 9-point improvement, and the follow-up work named above (more runs, reported SD, confound documentation) costs time and reagent budget the lab may not have already planned for — a cost that is nonetheless smaller than committing capital or staffing to an unresolved signal (chain C4).

**Pre-check:** head C1 (HIGH), C2 (LOW), C3 (MEDIUM), C4 (MEDIUM) · ?-marked: none directly · lowest cited: LOW · Inputs ceiling: LOW
**Confidence:** LOW — capped by C2's LOW rating (GT-8?'s unread industry-CV figures, plus an unsettled rival about this specific reaction's true variability that nothing in the report resolves). This LOW component is confined to the narrower quantitative question of whether the 9-point gap itself exceeds plausible noise — a question that genuinely cannot be answered without the reaction's own replicate variance data. It does not mean "nothing can be said": C1 (HIGH) establishes that no computable significance test exists from bare percentages; C3/C4 (MEDIUM) establish that the causal attribution is unresolved pending lab records (reagent lot, operator, method, run log). Obtaining per-run data and confound documentation would very likely raise every chain's rating; absent that, LOW is the calibrated overall figure for "is a real, generalizable catalyst effect established," while the narrower procedural findings (chains C1, C3, C4) remain individually well-supported.

**Premise (inversion, applied to the headline conclusion):** The headline conclusion — "this report does not establish that the catalyst causes a real, generalizable yield improvement" — is already false. What caused that failure?

**Causes (unfiltered, from three stakeholder viewpoints before grouping):**
*From the lab researcher's viewpoint:* the report reflects a genuinely reproducible effect that variance data would confirm; the researcher simply omitted routine details in an informal report; the three runs are representative of the full attempt set.
*From a skeptical reviewer's viewpoint:* the 9-point gap is within ordinary noise for this reaction; a reagent-lot change coincided with the catalyst trial; the catalyst runs were cherry-picked from a larger unreported set; the baseline is a stale single figure subject to drift; the analytical method changed between the two data points; an operator became more practiced between the baseline period and the catalyst trial.
*From a competitor/rival-lab viewpoint:* the reported figure is a publication-bias artifact (only positive results get reported at all); the effect is real but scale-dependent and won't transfer to production scale; the effect is real only under the specific ambient/equipment conditions of this one lab.

**Clusters (structural weaknesses, cause ids in brackets):**
1. *Statistical indeterminacy* — noise could fully explain the gap (bears on GT-2, GT-4, C1, C2).
2. *Uncontrolled confounds* — reagent lot, operator, method, scale, temperature/time (bears on GT-6?, C3).
3. *Selection/reporting bias* — cherry-picked runs, baseline drift/staleness, publication bias (bears on GT-5?, GT-7?, C3).
4. *Scale/generalizability gap* — bench-scale result may not transfer (bears on C4, not directly evidenced by any GT — noted as an accepted risk rather than a chain, since no ground truth addresses reaction scale beyond it being an unresolved unknown already inside GT-6?).

**Disposition:**
- Cluster 1 (statistical indeterminacy): plan change — do not present the gap as validated until per-run data and SD are reported (drives the C1/C2-based recommendation above).
- Cluster 2 (uncontrolled confounds): plan change — require a confound-documented run log (reagent lot, operator, method, scale, temperature/time) before attributing any yield change to the catalyst (drives the C3-based recommendation above).
- Cluster 3 (selection/reporting bias): plan change — require disclosure of the complete run log, including excluded runs, and confirmation of the baseline's own run count/protocol (drives the C3-based recommendation above).
- Cluster 4 (scale/generalizability gap): accepted risk — mitigation is to flag scale-dependence explicitly as an open question in any follow-up report, since resolving it is outside the scope of what this report's data could ever settle regardless of replicate count.

**Falsification:** The conclusion is false if a confound-controlled, adequately-powered replication (larger n, reported per-run SD, matched reagent lot/operator/method/scale/temperature/time, full run disclosure) still shows a yield increase attributable to the catalyst at a pre-specified significance level.

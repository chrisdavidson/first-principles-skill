# CI Pipeline Speed vs. Merge-Batching: First-Principles Analysis

## Executive Summary

Leadership's proposal — buy faster CI runners to bring the 47-minute pipeline down — is a bet placed before its own odds have been computed. Nobody has measured which part of those 47 minutes is actually reducible by more compute, and the behavior it's meant to fix (merge-batching) likely has causes and costs that raw pipeline speed doesn't touch at all. The recommendation is **not** "buy the runners" and **not** "do nothing" — it's: spend about two weeks instrumenting the pipeline and asking developers why they batch, then invest in whichever lever the data actually points to. A weighted comparison of five options ranks buying faster runners **last**, behind even doing nothing.

---

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Is spending money to make the existing 47-minute CI pipeline run faster the correct fix for the integration-health problem this company actually has — a problem that may be rooted in pipeline architecture, pipeline reliability, or developer batching incentives, none of which is currently distinguished from the others by any measurement?

**Success criteria:**
- The conclusion states a specific stance on the runner purchase (approve / defer-and-measure / conditional), traceable to explicit reasoning.
- The conclusion names what fraction of the 47 minutes would need to be parallelizable and compute-bound for a runner purchase to work, and states whether that's currently known.
- The conclusion addresses whether reducing CI duration alone would be expected to reverse the batching behavior, distinguishing duration from unpredictability, queue-wait, and fixed per-merge overhead.
- The conclusion names concrete measurements to gather — not a generic call for "more data."

## 2. Assumptions Table (key rows)

Leadership's proposal, and the scenario as stated, embed a stack of assumptions that have never been tested against this company's actual pipeline:

| Assumption | Type | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| The 47 minutes is compute-bound (dominated by raw execution work) rather than serialized stages, queue-wait, flaky reruns, or non-test overhead | untested belief | **Challenge** — no breakdown exists; even a correct diagnosis has a mathematically bounded ceiling on hardware's benefit |
| Throwing more/faster compute at CI scales the reduction roughly linearly with spend | convention | **Discard** — directly contradicted by Amdahl's Law; speedup saturates, bounded by the serial fraction |

A fishbone brainstorm across six pipeline-relevant categories (Test Execution, Architecture/Serialization, Infrastructure/Queueing, Test Reliability/Flakiness, Non-Test Overhead, People/Process) and an inversion of leadership's implicit claim ("faster runners will fix batching") both point to the same conclusion: multiple, independently plausible causes of the 47 minutes exist, and none has been ruled in or out.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1 — Amdahl's Law:** speedup from parallel compute is mathematically bounded by a task's serial (non-parallelizable) fraction — no amount of added or faster compute reduces a task below its serial component's duration.
- **GT-2 — Critical-path theorem:** for any pipeline modeled as a dependency graph, the minimum possible completion time even with unlimited parallelism equals the length of the longest dependency chain (the critical path). Adding compute to non-critical-path work doesn't reduce this floor.
- **GT-3 — Queueing theory:** wait time for a shared resource under contention grows non-linearly as utilization approaches capacity, and is governed by *concurrent capacity*, not by any single server's speed.
- **GT-4 — Martin Fowler, "Continuous Integration":** "the XP guideline of a ten minute build is perfectly within reason... most of our modern projects achieve this... Nothing sucks the blood of Continuous Integration more than a build that takes a long time."
- **GT-5 — DORA, "Trunk-based development":** teams merging small batches at least daily "achieve higher levels of software delivery and operational performance (delivery speed, stability, and availability)."
- **GT-6 — SmartBear / Cisco code-review study:** "developers should review no more than 200 to 400 lines of code at a time... beyond 400 LOC, the ability to find defects diminishes."
- **GT-7 (unverified)** — Developers are reportedly batching changes specifically to avoid triggering CI — this is asserted in the scenario but not backed by any cited survey, interview, or commit-history analysis.
- **GT-8** — No stage-by-stage timing breakdown, queue-wait data, or flaky-test rerun rate exists for this company's pipeline; this analysis is working without it, by design of the request.

## 4. Reasoning Chains

**C1 — Leadership's proposal is an unverified bet, not a diagnosed fix.**
Amdahl's Law and the critical-path theorem both say hardware can only ever shorten the parallelizable, compute-bound portion of a pipeline. Without a stage-level timing breakdown, nobody knows what fraction of the 47 minutes that even is. A fix whose benefit can't currently be bounded or estimated is a bet placed before its odds are known. *(Confidence: HIGH)*

**C2 — The best-evidenced lever on CI duration is architecture, not raw compute.**
Fowler's ten-minute build guideline shows large reductions (47 → ~10 min) are routinely achieved elsewhere — and, per the broader CI literature, this is typically done through parallelization, test splitting, and caching, not through buying faster individual machines. So the ~37-minute gap between this pipeline and industry practice is most plausibly closed by architecture work, with hardware only ever addressing whatever unknown remainder is both parallelizable and compute-bound. *(Confidence: MEDIUM — rests partly on a general-literature attribution this analysis didn't verify with a full case study)*

**C3 — Batching likely responds to more than raw CI duration.**
If part of what makes triggering CI painful is queue-wait unpredictability (which depends on concurrent capacity, not runner speed), faster individual runners leave that part untouched. A fix that only addresses part of a behavior's cause typically only partially reverses it. So buying faster runners is not established to meaningfully reduce batching — it could leave the habit largely intact. *(Confidence: MEDIUM — the batching-motive claim, GT-7, is unverified)*

**C4 — Batching carries its own costs, independent of CI speed.**
Batching more changes into fewer merges mechanically increases diff size, which the Cisco/SmartBear research shows pushes reviews past the point where defect-detection stays high. It also drops integration frequency below the small-batch pattern DORA associates with better delivery/stability outcomes. Second-order: reviewers either slow down or quietly cut corners on larger diffs, incidents inside batched merges take longer to bisect, and over time the large-batch pattern risks becoming the organization's new normal rather than a temporary workaround — making it *harder*, not easier, to reverse later. *(Confidence: MEDIUM — same GT-7 caveat)*

**C5 — A weighted comparison ranks buying runners last, below doing nothing.**
Scoring five options — do nothing, buy faster runners, re-architect the pipeline, measure first, address batch-size norms directly — against seven weighted criteria (cost, time-to-effect, addresses root cause, mitigates second-order harm, risk of wasted spend, reversibility, reduces wall-clock time), with weights locked before scoring:

| Option | Weighted Total |
|---|---|
| Address batching directly (process/culture) | **90** |
| Re-architect the pipeline | **85** |
| Measure first | **83** |
| Do nothing | **65** |
| **Buy faster runners (leadership's proposal)** | **45** |

Buying runners scores lowest of all five — 20 points below doing nothing — because it carries real cost and wasted-spend risk while addressing neither a diagnosed root cause nor either confirmed second-order harm. A flip-test shows this last-place finish is robust (no single-criterion reweighting moves it up); the ranking between the top two options (culture-fix vs. re-architecture) is *not* fully robust — a modest reweighting swaps them — so this analysis deliberately doesn't pick between those two. *(Confidence: MEDIUM)*

**C6 — Whether hardware alone would be sufficient is a falsifiable, currently open question.**
A runner-only fix is sufficient only if *all* of the following hold at once: the critical path is dominated by parallelizable compute, queue-wait is a small fraction of total time, flaky reruns are rare, and batching is confirmed to be driven by raw duration rather than unpredictability or fixed per-merge overhead. Every one of these is checkable — and none has been checked. *(Confidence: HIGH)*

**C7 — Therefore: measure first, then invest according to what the data shows.**
Since the deciding question is measurable and currently unmeasured (C6), and acting on the unmeasured guess scores worst of five options while measuring first scores near the top (C5), the rational move is to measure before spending. *(Confidence: MEDIUM)*

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

- **"CI is slow" and "developers batch" are one problem** — abandoned; batching has structural costs (review quality, delivery performance) that persist regardless of CI speed.
- **Hardware speedup scales linearly with spend** — abandoned; directly contradicted by Amdahl's Law.
- **Just pick a winner between re-architecture and a culture/process fix, skip measuring** — abandoned; the ranking between those two isn't robust, and committing a large investment to either one without data repeats the same blind-bet failure mode as leadership's original proposal.
- **Fermi-estimate the 47 minutes' composition from generic industry patterns** — abandoned; the actual breakdown is a company-specific fact to measure, not a magnitude to estimate from outside benchmarks.

---

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not approve the runner purchase now. Spend roughly two weeks, at near-zero cost, to: (1) instrument the pipeline for a stage-by-stage timing breakdown and queue-wait data, (2) pull the flaky-test rerun rate from existing logs, (3) run a short developer survey/interview asking directly why they batch, and (4) correlate merge size/frequency against the pipeline-duration timeline via git history. Then fund whichever of {pipeline re-architecture, batch-size norms, targeted runner capacity} the data actually points to — reserving a runner purchase specifically for the case where the data confirms the critical path is genuinely compute-bound, low-queue-wait, and low-flake.

**Key insight:** The 47-minute number and the batching behavior are being treated as one problem with one fix. They're not. Leadership's proposal is a bet on an unverified, mathematically-bounded diagnosis; even a fully successful bet would likely only partially reverse batching, since batching responds to more than raw duration; and batching's worst costs — degraded review quality, bigger failure blast radius, slower incident recovery — are consequences of batch size itself and would persist at any CI speed.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Measuring first costs about two weeks that a visible "we bought faster runners" announcement wouldn't cost, and risks reading as inaction. The trade-off's top two alternatives to buying runners (culture fix vs. re-architecture) are close enough that this analysis doesn't pick between them — that choice is deliberately deferred to the measurement step.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM overall. The two biggest open uncertainties — whether developers are really batching for the stated reason, and whether "faster builds elsewhere" really came from architecture rather than hardware — are exactly what the recommended two-week measurement closes.

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** How much land would utility-scale solar photovoltaics require to supply 100% of the electricity demand of a generic country of 10 million people, and does the resulting figure make "solar alone" physically and practically feasible — or does something other than acreage turn out to be the binding constraint?

**Success criteria:**
1. The Conclusion states a numeric land-area figure in km², with an explicit bracket, derived from named per-capita-demand and land-use-intensity ground truths (not a single point estimate presented as certain).
2. The Conclusion explicitly addresses whether "average annual demand" is a sufficient basis, and if not, states a quantified adjustment for diurnal/seasonal mismatch.
3. The Conclusion expresses the land figure as a percentage of national territory for at least two real ~10-million-population countries.
4. The Conclusion names the actual binding constraint(s) on feasibility, not merely the raw land number.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Deriving land area from installed capacity (acres/MW) plus a separately-assumed capacity factor

**What was tried:** Computing required PV nameplate capacity from demand and a generic assumed capacity factor (e.g., 20%), then converting to land area via an acres/MW metric.

**Why abandoned:** This two-step approach (guess a capacity factor, then apply a capacity-based land metric) compounds two independent sources of error, whereas GT-1's acres/GWh/yr metric is already energy-normalized against real US fleet data and implicitly encodes a representative capacity factor (per A-10). The two-step path is strictly dominated by the direct energy-based path for this problem.

**What it ruled out:** Using a capacity-based (acres/MW) land metric combined with a separately-guessed capacity factor as the primary derivation route.

### Dead End: Citing Shaner et al. (2018) to PNAS

**What was tried:** An initial recollection placed this paper in *PNAS*.

**Why abandoned:** A live search confirmed the actual venue is *Energy & Environmental Science* (Royal Society of Chemistry), not PNAS — a wrong-venue citation would have been presented as GT-10 without this check.

**What it ruled out:** Citing the wrong journal for what became GT-10?, which would have made independent verification of that ground truth harder for a reader trying to locate the primary source.

### Dead End: Treating "10 million people" as requiring identification of one specific real country

**What was tried:** Briefly considered picking a single real country (e.g., Portugal) as *the* subject of the analysis rather than a generic stand-in.

**Why abandoned:** The problem statement explicitly instructs treating the population figure as generic, using named countries only as sanity-check comparators — adopting one specific country would answer a narrower question than the one asked.

**What it ruled out:** A single-country-specific analysis in place of the generic-with-comparators framing that the success criteria require.

### Dead End: "Seasonal storage only" (store all summer surplus for winter use) as the sole solution to seasonal mismatch

**What was tried:** Considering whether storing enough summer solar surplus to cover the whole winter deficit — with no capacity overbuild at all — could substitute entirely for the worst-month-sizing approach in chain C2.

**Why abandoned:** This requires storage on the order of months of demand, well beyond the "several weeks" threshold GT-10? treats as the frontier of technical/economic plausibility; applying GT-15?'s density logic to a months-long buffer yields a storage footprint in the hundreds-to-low-thousands of km² — no longer clearly negligible relative to the PV footprint itself, and implausible on 2026 storage economics regardless of land.

**What it ruled out:** Presenting "seasonal storage alone, zero overbuild" as a credible design point; the moderate-overbuild-plus-weeks-of-storage framing (matching GT-10?'s own framing) is more defensible and is what chains C2/C4/C6 actually use.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Treat land area as a secondary check, not the primary feasibility question, for a solar-only electricity system in a country of this size and territory; the primary feasibility question is whether several-weeks-scale storage and/or a 3–20x capacity overbuild can be engineered and financed (chain C6).

**Key insight:** Total land use per unit of annual solar energy barely changed between 2013 and 2019 despite real cell-efficiency gains, because most of the land budget is already spent on inter-row spacing and access infrastructure rather than photoactive cell area — so land was never truly cell-efficiency-limited (chain C5) — and it is not demand-scale-limited either: even a deliberately pessimistic 20x sizing bound still fits within a small fraction of Portugal- or Hungary-sized territory (chain C3).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:**
- The 3–5x overbuild multiplier used for planning purposes rests on seasonal-ratio data from consumer-advice websites rather than peer-reviewed meteorological literature, and should be treated as illustrative rather than a design figure (chain C2).
- The finding that storage occupies far less land than the PV array itself depends on this analysis's own uncertain storage-density estimate and reverses if real installations are much less land-efficient than assumed (chain C4).
- This analysis uses one central per-capita demand figure and one deliberately pessimistic sizing bound rather than a full country-specific hourly simulation, trading modeling precision for a tractable, auditable order-of-magnitude estimate (chain C1; chain C3).

### Summary for the reader

A generic country of 10 million people, assuming a mid-range developed-economy per-capita demand (~4,600–12,100 kWh/year, central ~6,000), would need roughly **700–1,800 km² (central ≈870 km²)** of utility-scale solar to match its electricity demand on an annual-average basis — well under 1% of a Portugal- or Hungary-sized territory, and under 5% even of a much smaller territory like Belgium. Because solar generates nothing at night and far less in winter, a genuinely solar-only system needs either weeks of storage, several-fold capacity overbuild, or both — pushing a realistic planning figure to roughly **2,600–4,400 km²**, and even a deliberately pessimistic worst-case bound to **~17,500 km²**. That worst case still consumes under 19% of Portugal's or Hungary's land area, but over half of a Belgium-sized one — so the land-area verdict is genuinely scale-dependent, not a single universal number. For any country whose territory is comparable to or larger than Portugal's or Hungary's, **land is not what would stop a 10-million-person country from running on solar alone; the storage-and-overbuild engineering-economics problem is.**

# 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** What land area (km² and % of national territory) would utility-scale solar PV need to supply 100% of a 10-million-person country's annual electricity demand, and does that area — once the demand profile, geography, intermittency, and portfolio-mix assumptions are made explicit — constitute a real physical constraint on doing so?

**Success criteria:**
1. A quantitative land-area range in km² and % of national territory is stated for at least a low-demand and a high-demand per-capita profile, across at least two illustrative country geographies.
2. Every load-bearing quantity (irradiance, efficiency, losses, land-use intensity, per-capita demand) traces to a cited, provenance-labeled ground truth.
3. The assumptions that most change the answer (fixed vs. tracking, land exclusivity vs. agrivoltaics, average-annual vs. literal-instantaneous demand coverage, 100%-solar vs. mixed portfolio) are each explicitly identified and quantified in direction and rough magnitude.
4. A feasibility verdict is delivered that addresses land availability, agriculture/ecosystem competition, storage/grid requirements for intermittency, and how the picture changes under a mixed generation portfolio.

**Theoretical-limit reframe check (Phase 1):** the question could hinge on whether today's ~20% module efficiency is a hard physical ceiling or a movable convention. It is a convention/current-constraint — the Shockley–Queisser single-junction limit (≈33.7%) sits well above it — so the essence question does not need reframing around a different physical bound; this reframe is instead carried into Phase 4 as a dedicated theoretical-limit chain (C6) rather than changing what is being asked.

# 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Treat the land-area question as effectively answered in the negative for feasibility purposes: for a hypothetical 10-million-person country, solar PV alone requires roughly 100–2,450 km² of land to balance annual electricity demand, depending on per-capita consumption (≈10–127 TWh/yr) and local irradiance, which across the three illustrative geographies examined (Sweden-, Portugal-, and Israel-sized/latitude analogs) works out to roughly 0.05% to 5.7% of national territory (chain C10, resting on C5 and C8).

**Key insight:** The land-area figure is almost a red herring for the real feasibility question. Because solar generates zero power for part of every day everywhere on Earth, and grid demand is never zero overnight, a literal "100% solar, no storage, no backup, every hour" reading of the question is not merely expensive but physically impossible — the km² figures above answer only the annual energy-balance version of "supply the entire demand," and the true binding constraints on 100%-solar feasibility are dispatchability/storage capacity and practical siting/exclusion-zone availability, not raw square kilometers (chain C7).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Today's ~20% module efficiency is a convention, not a physical ceiling — the Shockley–Queisser limit (~33.7%) implies land needs could shrink further as technology matures, not balloon (chain C6). Pulling the other direction, the raw-land percentages say nothing about how much of that land would actually clear siting, permitting, and community-acceptance exclusions — a country could pass the land-area test by a wide margin and still fail the practical-siting test (no chain — flagged assumption only, A12a).

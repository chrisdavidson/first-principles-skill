## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given a mid-sized city's own baseline data (existing transit/cycling mode share, network coverage, commute origin-destination geography, and fiscal capacity), what combination and sequencing of free transit, congestion pricing, and protected cycle lanes converts the largest, most durable share of existing car-commute trips to non-car modes within a 3-year window — and what specific data thresholds determine when the recommendation changes?

**Success criteria:**
1. The Conclusion names which lever(s), in what sequence/combination, and states the city-specific data thresholds that would flip the choice — checkable by re-reading the Conclusion against Derivation Chain C6.
2. The Conclusion states, with a quantitative bracket, whether the 20%-in-3-years target is achievable by any single lever acting alone — checkable against Chains C2–C4.
3. The Conclusion distinguishes trip-elimination, mode-shift, and time-shift as separate mechanisms and states which mechanism each lever principally works through — checkable against Chain C1 and Ground Truth GT-1.
4. The Conclusion names the specific second-order risk that could invert the recommendation, and what mitigates it — checkable against Chain C6's second-order extension and the adversarial-pass record.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** A removed car-commute trip must appear as exactly one of: mode-shift (trip persists, mode changes), elimination (no substitute trip), or time-shift (trip persists, moves outside the counted peak window) — a formal accounting identity, not an empirical claim. — source: definitional identity (trip accounting), verified by construction; read-at-source: n/a (formal derivation).
- **GT-2** Gothenburg is "one of the smallest cities (half a million citizens) ever to implement congestion charges," with congestion "always... much lower than in Stockholm," and, being "more sparsely populated than Stockholm," has "a considerably lower public transport market share and higher car dependence." — source: Börjesson, M. (2018), *Long-Term Effects of the Swedish Congestion Charges*, ITF Discussion Paper 170, OECD/ITF; read-at-source: p.5, Introduction.
- **GT-3** In Stockholm, the price elasticity of cordon-crossing traffic to the charge *increased* over time: non-exempt traffic elasticity moved from −0.87 (2006) to −1.24 (2014); private-traffic-only elasticity from −1.57 to −2.49 — "there are no signs that the effect of charges is wearing off." — source: same paper; read-at-source: p.13, Table 3.
- **GT-4** In Gothenburg, the same elasticity *declined*: total-traffic elasticity fell from −0.69 (2013) to −0.52 (2015); private-only from −1.18 to −0.85 — the opposite trend from Stockholm, attributed by the author to Gothenburg's smaller size/lower density giving residents "fewer ways to adapt in the long run." — source: same paper; read-at-source: p.15, Table 4 and following paragraphs.
- **GT-5** Gothenburg's system investment cost was recovered "in slightly more than a year" vs. 3.3 years in Stockholm; operating cost is 7–13% of revenue for the fully-automated Swedish (ANPR) systems, vs. 35% for London's partly-manual system. — source: same paper; read-at-source: p.11.
- **GT-6** London's charge cut zone-entering traffic ~25% on launch day (confounded by school holidays) and ~10–11% in vehicle-kilometers over 2000–2012, but within several years Transport for London found congestion (minutes/km) "close to levels before the charge was implemented" — TfL attributes this largely to deliberate reallocation of road capacity to buses/cycling/pedestrians, though "causality has not been established." — source: Wikipedia, "London congestion charge"; read-at-source: article body, sections on traffic-reduction figures and the congestion-rebound discussion.
- **GT-7** Following Tallinn's 2013 fare-free transit, transit's commute mode share *fell* from over 40% (2013) to under 30% (2022) while car-commute share *rose* from 40% to 50%; the decline was steepest for the bottom income quartile (transit share fell from >60% to ~35%). — source: Strong Towns, "Lessons From Estonia: Free Fares Alone Won't Boost Ridership" (2023); read-at-source: article body, quoted figures.
- **GT-8** Dunkirk's 2018 fare-free buses produced ~65% weekday / >100% weekend ridership growth in year one; of surveyed regular riders reporting increased use, 48% said they now use the bus "instead of" a car, and ~5% of all respondents sold a car or forwent a second one. — source: France24 (2019), "French city of Dunkirk tests out free transport – and it works"; read-at-source: article body, quoted figures.
- **GT-9** Seville's protected cycle-track network (120 km, Bike Masterplan approved 2007) took cycling from "very low (under 1%)" mode share (2006) to ~6% of all trips / 9% of mechanized trips by 2010 — roughly 4 years. — source: Interreg Europe, "Bicycle infrastructure network in Seville"; read-at-source: case-study body, quoted figures.
- **GT-10?** Paris's Plan Vélo I (2015–2020) raised cycling's trip share from ~5% (2015) to ~10% (2019), reaching 11.2% by 2022–23 vs. car at 4.3%. — cited to: multiple secondary sources (GHD, Velorution, Wikipedia "Cycling in Paris"); reported-by-delegate — not read at a primary transport-authority source; not read — turn budget.
- **GT-11?** Transit fare-elasticity rule of thumb: Simpson-Curtin ≈ −0.33; short-run bus-fare elasticities cluster −0.3 to −0.4, long-run −0.6 to −1.0 (Paulley et al. 2006). This measures ridership response to fares, not car-diversion specifically. — cited to: meta-analysis syntheses (DiVA/ScienceDirect); reported-by-delegate; not read — turn budget.
- **GT-12?** Mid-size U.S. transit agencies ($20M–$100M fare revenue) recover ~23% of operating costs from fares, vs. ~36% for the largest agencies. — cited to: Eno Center/NTD-derived commentary; reported-by-delegate; not read — turn budget.
- **GT-13?** Protected bike-lane capital cost typically ~$130k–$540k/mile; as low as ~$20k/mile when added during repaving; complex urban retrofits can reach millions/mile (Seattle ~$12M/mile outlier). — cited to: PeopleForBikes/Streetsblog; reported-by-delegate; not read — turn budget.
- **GT-14?** U.S. journey-to-work (2017, NHTS-derived): 88.1% of commute trips by private vehicle (66.3% drive-alone), 5.9% transit, 5.0% combined bike+walk. — cited to: FHWA/NHTS commentary; reported-by-delegate; not read — turn budget.
- **GT-15?** Singapore's ERP cut CBD car traffic ~15% at 1998 launch, with broader downtown-passenger-car studies finding 20–30%; attributed partly to Singapore's high-capacity parallel rail+bus network. — cited to: CMAP/ITF-OECD secondary summaries; reported-by-delegate; not read — turn budget.
- **GT-16?** Uppsala, Sweden (a small city) evaluated congestion pricing and found "congestion efficiency gains were small relative to the congestion zone revenues," indicating comparatively inelastic driving demand at that scale. — cited to: secondary academic-evaluation summary; reported-by-delegate; not read — turn budget.
- **GT-17?** NYC congestion pricing took decades politically (concept 1959; Bloomberg proposal 2007; state authorization 2019; launch Jan 2025); Stockholm and London each moved from firm decision to launch in roughly 2–2.5 years. — cited to: Wikipedia "History of congestion pricing in New York City" for the NYC portion (reported-by-delegate, not read — turn budget); the Stockholm/London interval is inferred from dates already read in GT-2/GT-6 combined with unread background knowledge of the decision dates, not itself read at a single source — mixed provenance, kept `?`.
- **GT-18?** Evidence on whether new cycling/transit capacity substitutes for car trips specifically is mixed and context-dependent: some studies find cars the dominant substituted mode (Europe/N. America/Australia e-bike studies); others find new capacity substitutes mainly for walking or existing transit (Tricity Poland, Beijing bike-share). — cited to: secondary meta-analysis summaries; reported-by-delegate; not read — turn budget.
- **GT-19** A cordon-crossing vehicle count (how congestion pricing is scored), a trip-purpose mode-share survey (how free transit and cycle lanes are scored), and a "commuter car trip" are three different measurement constructs; a raw percentage from one is not directly comparable to a raw percentage from another without normalizing to a common metric. — source: definitional/measurement-construct analysis; read-at-source: n/a (formal derivation).

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: The 20% target is, in mechanism terms, a mode-shift target, not a trip-elimination target — with one unsettled rival

GT-1 (trip-conservation identity) + GT-19 (measurement-construct non-equivalence)
→ a car-commute-trip reduction of any size must be attributable to exactly one of three channels: mode-shift, elimination, or time-shift
→ a 3-year policy window is too short to materially change land-use or job/housing location at scale, so the dominant policy-controllable channel available to these three levers is mode-shift of trips that continue to exist *[Assumes: A11 — that remote-work/land-use-driven elimination is not itself doing much of the work in this specific city]*
→ each lever below should be scored by its power to convert existing car-commute trips to non-car modes within 3 years, not by its power to eliminate trips outright

### Conclusion C2: Congestion pricing alone brackets to roughly 4%–21% of total commuter car trips within 3 years, centered near 12–13%

GT-2 (Gothenburg: smallest congestion-charge city, lower transit share) + GT-3 (Stockholm elasticity strengthening) + GT-4 (Gothenburg elasticity weakening) + GT-16? (Uppsala: near-inelastic at smaller scale)

→ a charge sized to raise trip cost 20–40% (typical Stockholm/Gothenburg ratio) against that elasticity band yields 10–30% reduction among cordon-crossing trips; multiplying by the 40–70% cordon-relevance share yields **4%–21%** of total commuter car trips, central ≈12.5%

### Conclusion C3: Free transit alone brackets to roughly 0.5%–4% of total commuter car trips within 3 years, with a documented real risk of ≈0% or reversal

### Conclusion C5: A 20% reduction sits at or above the best any single lever has demonstrated at comparable scale, though it is within the topology-bound theoretical ceiling

GT-14? (88% drive-alone baseline) + GT-1 (trip-conservation identity)
→ the governing hard constraint is not price elasticity but network topology: a trip can only shift mode if a substitute (transit line, protected route, walkable path) exists within an acceptable detour of both trip ends — the ideal ceiling is the fraction of current car-commute OD-pairs already within existing or near-term-buildable non-car coverage, the "reachable share" *[Assumes: A18, generalized from cordon-specific to network-wide]*
→ across the precedents reviewed, that reachable share plausibly sits at 40–70% for a city with "modest" pre-existing bus/rail coverage (as the prompt itself frames the baseline) — this is the ceiling: even a hypothetical zero-cost, zero-friction, fully-adopted policy cannot shift a trip with no physically accessible non-car path

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Free transit as the sole lever
**What was tried:** Evaluating whether eliminating fares alone could plausibly reach 20% of commuter car trips within 3 years.
**Why abandoned:** C3's Fermi bracket (0.5%–4%, central ~1–2%) falls far short of even the half-target must-have, and the Tallinn precedent (GT-7) shows the mechanism can run in *reverse* over a multi-year horizon — this is not merely a magnitude shortfall but a directional risk.
**What it ruled out:** Pursuing fare elimination as a sufficient, standalone answer to this target — though it remains a plausible *complementary* investment (funded from pricing revenue) targeted at the specific corridors serving the priced zone, where the Dunkirk-style car-diversion rate (48%) is most likely to apply.

### Dead End: Protected cycle lanes as the sole lever
**What was tried:** Evaluating whether an accelerated protected-lane build-out alone could reach 20% within 3 years.
**Why abandoned:** C4's bracket (1.2%–3.2%) falls short of the half-target must-have, and every reviewed precedent (Seville, Paris) needed 4–5 years to reach even a 5–6-point mode-share gain — the 3-year window itself, not the mechanism's validity, is the binding constraint.
**What it ruled out:** Cycle lanes as a sufficient standalone answer within this specific timeframe — though, per the fishbone finding (A13), it remains the highest-leverage complement for the walk/bike-substitutable trip segment precisely because it targets a non-price barrier (safety/segregation) that neither of the other two levers touches.

### Dead End: Land-use change / remote-work-driven trip elimination as a fourth lever
**What was tried:** Considering whether structural trip elimination (remote work, densification) could be counted on to deliver a meaningful share of the 20% within 3 years, independent of the three named levers.
**Why abandoned:** Genuinely city-specific and not resolvable from the generic facts given in the prompt (no remote-work trend or zoning trajectory was supplied); rather than assume an answer, this was carried forward as A11, an explicitly flagged, unsettled rival to C1, rather than discarded outright.
**What it ruled out:** Treating C1's conclusion (mode-shift is the dominant policy-controllable channel) as unconditionally certain — a city with a large post-pandemic remote-work shift already underway should re-run C1 with its own trip-count time series before accepting the mode-shift framing at face value.

### Dead End: Treating "commuter car trips" as a self-evidently well-defined, single metric
**What was tried:** Taking the prompt's "commuter car journeys" framing as directly measurable and directly comparable across all three levers' own evaluation literatures.
**Why abandoned:** GT-19 establishes that congestion pricing is conventionally scored on cordon-crossing vehicle counts (all purposes, peak hours), while free-transit and cycle-lane evaluations are conventionally scored on trip-purpose mode-share surveys — these are different measurement constructs, and A3 records this as a live definitional gap rather than a resolved one.
**What it ruled out:** Presenting the three levers' Fermi brackets (C2, C3, C4) as if they were measuring identical quantities; each bracket above is explicit about which construct it estimates.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Sequence congestion pricing first — sized and cordoned like the Stockholm/Gothenburg systems, with 7–13%-of-revenue operating costs achievable via full automation (GT-5) — with its net revenue contractually hypothecated, before or concurrent with launch, to (a) frequency and coverage increases on the bus/rail corridors serving the priced zone and (b) an accelerated protected-cycle-lane build-out on the top commuter corridors (chain C6).

**Key insight:** The binding constraint on hitting 20% in 3 years is not price sensitivity in the abstract — it is network topology and adaptation capacity, which is exactly why the *same* lever (congestion pricing) produced a strengthening effect in Stockholm and a weakening one in Gothenburg, a city explicitly documented as the smallest ever to try it (chain C6, resting on GT-2/GT-3/GT-4). A generic "pick the best lever" playbook cannot be transferred from a large, dense, high-transit-share metro to a mid-sized city; the choice must be calibrated to this city's own commute-OD reachable-share (chain C5) and re-scored with this city's own priority weights (A14) before being adopted.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The combined package accepts higher coordination and deployment-speed cost (score 3 vs. 4 for pricing-only, chain C6) and depends on capacity investment being physically deliverable within the window, not merely funded (A15) — no chain here has verified that for any specific city (chain C6's second-order extension; no chain — flagged assumption only for the capacity-delivery-timing risk specifically). It also accepts durable political-economy risk (rate revisions, rollback votes) that persists past the 3-year measurement window (chain C6, time-lens extension).

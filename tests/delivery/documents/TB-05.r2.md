# 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Does mandating publication of an AI hiring system's training-data sources, as a freestanding legal requirement, cause the system's discriminatory-bias risk to become detectable, auditable, and remediable in a way that measurably serves the goals such rules are meant to serve — or is training-data-source disclosure structurally disconnected from those goals regardless of how faithfully it is implemented?

**Success criteria:**
1. For each of the three named system architectures, the analysis states whether "publish training data sources" is a well-defined, satisfiable obligation.
2. The analysis states, with a traceable derivation, whether disclosed source information is capable of revealing or reducing discriminatory bias as bias is actually produced in ML systems.
3. The analysis compares the proposal against mechanisms real regulators have actually adopted for the same stated goals, and states which the evidence favors.
4. The analysis gives a clear bottom-line verdict on whether the rule as stated achieves its likely intended aims, and names a better-targeted alternative if it does not.

# 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1?** NYC Local Law 144 requires an independent, annual bias audit of automated employment decision tools measuring disparate impact by race/ethnicity/sex, public posting of an audit summary, and ≥10 business days' candidate notice; enforced by NYC DCWP since July 5, 2023; no training-data-source disclosure requirement — cited to: Local Law 144 (2021), summarized by Deloitte, Warden AI, DCI Consult; reported-by-delegate — enacted text not opened directly.
- **GT-2** EU AI Act Art. 10(2)–(3): high-risk-system providers must apply data-governance practices covering "data collection processes and the origin of data," "the formulation of assumptions… with respect to the information the data are supposed to measure and represent," examination for biases affecting fundamental rights, "appropriate measures to detect, prevent and mitigate possible biases," and identification of data gaps — source: artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/10; read-at-source: operative clauses quoted verbatim from that page.
- **GT-3?** Illinois HB3773 (effective Jan. 1, 2026) imposes strict, effects-based liability for AI's discriminatory effect in employment regardless of intent, requires notice, and restricts protected-class-correlated proxy variables; no source-disclosure requirement — cited to: Morgan Lewis, Manatt, Hinshaw & Culbertson summaries; reported-by-delegate.
- **GT-4?** EEOC's Uniform Guidelines four-fifths rule: a selection-rate ratio below 80% between the highest- and a protected group's selection rate is treated as an initial indicator of disparate impact; EEOC itself calls it "merely a rule of thumb" — cited to: 29 C.F.R. §1607.4(D) summaries (Pin, eSkill, K&L Gates); reported-by-delegate.
- **GT-5?** California AB 2013 (effective Jan. 1, 2026) requires generative-AI developers to post a "high-level summary" across 12 categories (including sources/owners, copyright status, personal-data content); enforced via the Unfair Competition Law; vendors complied "at varying levels of detail"; xAI sued challenging constitutionality; a district court upheld the requirement — cited to: Crowell & Moring, Goodwin, Jones Day, Norton Rose Fulbright; reported-by-delegate.
- **GT-6** A model trained exclusively on one employer's own historical applicant-tracking records has, by construction, exactly one training-data source — the employer itself — already fully known independent of any disclosure mandate — source: definitional, from the architecture of supervised learning on a single self-generated dataset.
- **GT-7?** Amazon's 2014–2018 resume-scoring tool, trained on ~10 years of the company's own resumes (a single, never-ambiguous source), penalized resumes containing terms like "women's" because the historical applicant pool skewed male; Amazon could not de-bias it and scrapped the project; the bias was surfaced by outcome testing, not by any question about data provenance — cited to: Jeffrey Dastin, Reuters (2018), via MIT Technology Review summary; reported-by-delegate.
- **GT-8?** Foundation-model vendors have historically disclosed pretraining-corpus composition only in coarse categorical terms and treat detailed composition as commercially sensitive, amid active AI-training-data copyright/trade-secret litigation; even under AB 2013, compliance varied in detail and one vendor (xAI) sued to challenge the mandate — cited to: IPWatchdog, Goodwin, Wittliff Cutter; reported-by-delegate.
- **GT-9** A hiring product built on a fine-tuned/prompted third-party foundation model has ≥2 distinct data-ownership layers (base pretraining corpus vs. task-specific fine-tune/prompt data), generally held by unaffiliated entities — source: definitional/architectural, corroborated by GT-8?.

# 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Charitable AB 2013 reading
**What was tried:** Treating "publish training data sources" as if it already meant AB 2013's specific 12-category high-level-summary regime.
**Why abandoned:** The proposal's text specifies no category scheme; evaluating it as if it already had one would understate the mechanical-definition problem C1/C2 identify. AB 2013 is retained only as evidentiary precedent for how such rules play out in practice, not as the definition of the proposal.
**What it ruled out:** A softer reading of the whole analysis that would have missed the core finding that the proposal, as literally stated, is under-specified across architectures.

### Dead End: Disclosure as pure deterrence/signaling
**What was tried:** Considering whether disclosure could serve a bias-reduction goal indirectly through reputational deterrence, without the disclosed content itself being informative.
**Why abandoned:** Deterrence requires the disclosed information be capable of triggering scrutiny; C3 establishes source-identity information isn't informative enough to do so, and GT-11 directly tests and defeats the market/reputational-pressure mechanism for a considerably more actionable existing law (Cluster B, adversarial pass).
**What it ruled out:** A weaker version of the recommendation that would have relied on disclosure's mere existence rather than on pairing it with mandatory outcome testing.

### Dead End: Privacy-minimization justification
**What was tried:** Considering whether a data-minimization/privacy rationale, independent of bias, could justify the rule as stated.
**Why abandoned:** Out of scope — the user's question frames the stated goals as bias reduction, accountability, recourse, and detection; a privacy-only justification implies a different rule (data-minimization mandates) with a different mechanism, not source-list publication.
**What it ruled out:** Conflating two distinct policy rationales that would have muddied the bottom-line verdict.

# 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Do not adopt "publish training data sources" as a standalone rule; replace it with a bundle of (1) mandatory outcome/disparate-impact testing reported as selection-rate ratios by protected class, (2) a narrow, harm-linked data-category disclosure (copyright status, personal-data content, protected-class-correlated proxy risk) modeled on the EU AI Act's and California's actual category lists rather than an unqualified source list, and (3) a low-discretion applicability test that removes employers' ability to self-determine exemption (chain C7).

**Key insight:** Source-transparency and bias-transparency are different axes entirely — even the one architecture where "training data sources" is already fully known and trivially disclosable (a bespoke model trained solely on an employer's own historical hiring records) does not thereby reveal or prevent discriminatory bias, because bias enters through how past hiring outcomes were labeled and which features proxy for protected traits, not through where the data originated (chain C3).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The recommended bundle imposes real costs the stated proposal avoids or defers — outcome auditing requires access to demographic outcome data the employer may not currently collect, harm-linked data-category disclosure still carries some of the IP/litigation exposure the stated proposal carries, and a low-discretion applicability test will draw more small employers into scope than the current self-determined-scope regime that already produces the "null compliance" pattern documented at GT-11 — this is a heavier compliance obligation being recommended in exchange for one that actually connects to the stated goals (chain C5).

# The Analysis

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Does mandating publication of "training data sources" for hiring AI systems actually carry the information needed to make the assumed chain — disclosure → scrutiny → detection of bias → correction/accountability — work, or does the disclosure unit fail at one or more links, such that the rule under-delivers relative to outcome-based alternatives?

**Success criteria:**
1. The Conclusion states, for each of the three stated aims (reducing discriminatory outcomes; increasing accountability/transparency; enabling scrutiny), whether the rule as stated achieves it, each traced to a named chain.
2. The Recommended approach names the specific additional/alternative levers required, traced to the trade-off chain (C3).
3. The Key Insight names the specific pipeline stage(s) where bias actually enters that a training-data-source label cannot reach, traced to chains C1/C2.

---

## 2. Assumptions Table

**Decomposing "publish training data sources."** Before classifying assumptions, the phrase itself needs unpacking, since the proposal is silent on which of at least four distinct disclosure levels it means: (a) *naming* the category of source ("resume database," "LinkedIn API," "employer's own ATS records"); (b) *releasing* the underlying data itself; (c) *describing provenance/collection methodology* (how, when, from whom the data was gathered, consent basis); (d) *disclosing derived artifacts* — which features/proxies were extracted from that data and which labels/target variables were used. This is not an incidental ambiguity: GT-12 below shows these are four analytically separate pipeline stages, and GT-7 shows that, given latitude, real vendors resolve this ambiguity toward (a), the thinnest option.

**Fishbone (condensed) — categories of hiring-AI bias this disclosure rule might or might not catch**, used to enumerate the assumption space before classifying it:

| Category | Candidate causes (unverified until checked against GTs) | Discriminating observation |
|---|---|---|
| Data provenance/composition | historically skewed applicant pool; label bias in "successful hire" outcomes | does disparity persist after re-weighting the source's demographic composition, model/features/threshold held fixed? |
| Feature engineering | proxy variables (zip code, college); free-text/word-embedding associations | does disparity appear in the raw score before any feature is removed? |
| Model architecture/training | objective function bakes in "resemble past top performers" | does changing only the objective function change the ratio, data held fixed? |
| Deployment/threshold | score cutoff set differently changes who passes | does the disparity appear/flip specifically at the cutoff, not in the raw score? |
| Feedback loops | model retrained on its own filtered outcomes | does the ratio drift over successive retraining cycles with no other change? |
| Organizational/human oversight | recruiter override or rubber-stamp of tool output | does the human-in-the-loop step change the ratio independent of the model? |

Priority: categories 2–4 are the ones a source-only disclosure structurally cannot reach, per the discriminating observations above — this is the load-bearing finding carried into GT-12 and chains C1/C2.

**Inversion.** Claim: "requiring publication of training-data sources will reduce discriminatory outcomes and increase accountability." Inverted: "it fails to do either." Failure-guaranteeing conditions and the necessary preconditions they yield (each load-bearing unless noted): disclosed sources are named too coarsely to compute anything (→ P1, load-bearing); bias arises predominantly downstream of sourcing (→ P2, load-bearing, the central hidden assumption); no empowered analyzer exists to act on the disclosure (→ P3, load-bearing); compliance can be truthful but uninformative (→ P4, load-bearing); the rule pushes vendors toward less-nameable sources (→ P5, not load-bearing for the headline finding, feeds the second-order analysis instead); trade-secret carve-outs hollow the rule (→ P6, load-bearing); disclosure goes stale after retraining (→ P7, not load-bearing for the headline finding).

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** NYC Local Law 144 (effective 2023) requires an annual independent bias audit of automated employment decision tools measuring impact ratios by sex/race, public disclosure of a *summary of the audit results* (not training-data sources), and 10-business-day candidate notice with an alternative-assessment option — source: NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection official page; read-at-source: quoted clause "information about the bias audit is publicly available" / summary-posting requirement, confirming no training-data-source disclosure obligation.
- **GT-2** EU AI Act Annex III, Section 4 classifies recruitment/selection AI as high-risk, triggering technical documentation, training-data governance, risk management, and human-oversight obligations as a bundle — not a standalone "publish your sources" requirement — source: artificialintelligenceact.eu, Annex III; read-at-source: quoted "AI systems intended to be used for the recruitment or selection of natural persons, in particular to place targeted job advertisements, to analyse and filter job applications, and to evaluate candidates."
- **GT-3** 29 CFR § 1607.4(D) (EEOC Uniform Guidelines, "four-fifths rule") is an outcome-based statistical adverse-impact test computable from observed selection rates alone — source: 29 CFR § 1607.4(D) via Cornell LII; read-at-source: quoted "A selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group which is less than four-fifths… of the rate for the group with the highest rate will generally be regarded… as evidence of adverse impact."
- **GT-4** The peer-reviewed literature taxonomizes at least three independent bias-entry points: target-variable/outcome choice, training data, and labelling of examples — source: Raghavan, Barocas, Kleinberg & Levy, "Mitigating Bias in Algorithmic Hiring" (FAT* 2020, arXiv:1906.09208); read-at-source: p.2, quoted directly from the fetched PDF pages.
- **GT-5** Target-variable choice independently introduces bias: "proxies like job evaluations… can exhibit biases against minorities. Moreover, predicting the success of future employees based on current employees inherently skews the task toward finding candidates resembling those who have already been hired" — source: same paper; read-at-source: p.10.
- **GT-6** Real vendor de-biasing (HireVue, pymetrics, PredictiveHire) operates by "downweight[ing] or remov[ing] features found to be highly correlated with the protected attribute… noting that this can significantly reduce adverse impact with little effect on the predictive accuracy" — i.e., remediation targets features, not the named data source — source: same paper; read-at-source: p.9.
- **GT-7** Empirically, of 18 pre-employment-assessment vendors surveyed, public "training data" disclosure is typically a coarse categorical label (custom/semi-custom/pre-built) or a vague marketing claim (e.g., "unbiased because of its full spectrum database") rather than information sufficient to compute a fairness metric — source: same paper; read-at-source: Tables 1–2, pp.7–8.
- **GT-8** Amazon's 2014–2017 hiring tool, trained on resumes submitted to the company over a decade (a nameable source), penalized outcomes due to that source's demographic composition, not due to the source label itself — source: Dastin, Reuters (2018), reproduced by mediawell.ssrc.org; read-at-source: quoted "Amazon's computer models were trained to vet applicants by observing patterns in resumes submitted to the company over a 10-year period. Most came from men, a reflection of male dominance across the tech industry."
- **GT-9?** The same tool additionally penalized the word "women's" and favored male-coded verbs — a feature/word-association-level mechanism distinct from source-composition alone — cited to: widely-reported Reuters detail; reported-by-delegate (WebSearch synthesis, original Reuters article not opened directly). Illustrative only; not load-bearing for C2.
- **GT-10?** Illinois's Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act requires notice, explanation, and (as amended, 2026) affirmative written consent before AI analysis of video interviews — another real "AI hiring transparency" statute whose operative unit is notice/consent, not training-data-source publication — cited to: Illinois General Assembly (820 ILCS 42/) and law-firm summaries; reported-by-delegate (WebSearch aggregate, statute text not opened directly).
- **GT-11?** Proxy discrimination — facially neutral variables (e.g., zip code) correlated with protected-class membership via historical segregation — can produce disparate impact independent of the named data-source category — cited to: Iowa Law Review, "Proxy Discrimination in the Age of Artificial Intelligence and Big Data"; reported-by-delegate (WebSearch synthesis, article not opened directly). Supporting context only; not load-bearing.
- **GT-12** A supervised hiring-ML pipeline has at least five analytically distinct decision points at which discriminatory effects enter: (1) raw data composition, (2) target-variable/label choice, (3) feature engineering, (4) model architecture/training procedure, (5) deployment threshold/decision policy. "Training data source" answers only (1). Source: definitional — necessarily follows from the sequential structure of supervised ML pipelines (each stage temporally and causally posterior to data sourcing), confirmed empirically for stages (2) and (3) by GT-4/GT-5/GT-6.
- **GT-13?** Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al., 2018) and Model Cards for Model Reporting (Mitchell et al., 2019) are established documentation frameworks specifying structured fields (motivation, composition, collection process for datasets; intended use, evaluation results for models) — the closest existing template for a substantive disclosure, showing depth is achievable but requires far more structure than naming a source — cited to: Hugging Face "Landscape of ML Documentation Tools" and related; reported-by-delegate (WebSearch synthesis, original papers not opened directly).
- **GT-14?** A third-party bias-audit compliance industry (e.g., babl.ai, Warden AI, nycbiasaudit.com) already exists specifically serving NYC Local Law 144 compliance, within roughly two to three years of the law's 2023 enforcement start — cited to: WebSearch results listing these firms; reported-by-delegate (their websites not independently opened).

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: "Just require deeper data disclosure" as the complete fix

**What was tried:** Considered recommending that the rule simply be strengthened to require full demographic-composition disclosure of training data as a sufficient standalone remedy, rather than proposing a multi-lever package.

**Why abandoned:** GT-4/GT-5/GT-6 establish that target-variable choice and feature engineering/downweighting are independent, later-stage bias loci that no depth of *data* disclosure can surface, since those decisions are made after and separately from data sourcing; GT-2's EU AI Act precedent bundles training-data governance with separate risk-management and human-oversight obligations rather than treating data disclosure as sufficient alone. ## 6. Conclusion

- Reducing discriminatory outcomes: the rule as stated does not reliably achieve this aim, because the pipeline stages most responsible for disparate outcomes sit downstream of and independent from data sourcing (chains C1, C2).
- Increasing accountability: the rule as stated does not achieve this aim as specified, because it publishes information without pairing it to an audit obligation, penalty, or empowered recipient (chain C4).
- Enabling scrutiny: the rule as stated supplies only low-value scrutiny material, since real-world compliance resolves "source" at a granularity too coarse to analyze (chain C1).

**Recommended approach:** Replace, or at minimum supplement, the "publish training data sources" mandate with a three-part package: (1) mandatory third-party outcome/bias audits with published impact-ratio summaries (NYC LL144-style), (2) individual candidate notice, consent, and a right to an alternative assessment, and (3) a confidential, regulator-facing (not public) technical file covering data provenance, target-variable choice, feature engineering, and deployment thresholds (chain C3).

**Key insight:** The rule targets the one pipeline stage — data sourcing — that is neither the most legible disclosure unit in current vendor practice nor a reliable predictor of discriminatory outcomes; bias enters independently and later, through target-variable choice and feature engineering, stages a source label cannot describe even when fully and honestly disclosed (chains C1, C2). Reasoning by analogy to "more disclosure is always better" would have missed this distinction between disclosure depth and disclosure placement in the pipeline.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The recommended package costs more to comply with than the rule as stated, and its own weighted score is lowest on vendor cost among the viable options, risking accelerated consolidation toward large, compliance-capable incumbents (chain C3). The pre-mortem's Cluster 4 disposition (a tiered small-vendor compliance timeline) mitigates but does not eliminate this risk — no chain — flagged assumption only, since the mitigation's real-world efficacy was not independently tested here.

## Summary for you

The rule as written targets the wrong unit of transparency. Training data *source* (where the data came from) is only one of at least five independent points in a hiring-AI pipeline where discrimination can enter — target-variable choice, feature engineering, model training, and deployment-threshold choice are the other four, and the peer-reviewed record (Raghavan et al., FAT* 2020) plus the Amazon case show these downstream stages are where real, documented bias actually lived, invisible from a source label however honestly disclosed. Existing "AI hiring transparency" laws (NYC Local Law 144, EU AI Act Annex III, Illinois's AI Video Interview Act) already implicitly recognize this — none of them ask for training-data-source publication as their operative mechanism; they use outcome audits, documentation-and-governance bundles, or notice/consent instead. A weighted trade-off across five candidate regulatory levers, stress-tested with a flip test and a pre-mortem, favors combining outcome audits + individual notice/consent/alternative-assessment rights + a confidential (non-public) regulator-facing technical file over the as-stated rule — robustly, since no single reweighting of the comparison criteria overturns that ranking.

# First-Principles Analysis: The 62%→71% Catalyst Yield Claim

*(MODE = full-composer; no single-technique trigger fired in the request, so all five phases run, with inversion, estimate, and second-order thinking applied where each phase invokes them.)*

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Does the reported comparison (mean yield 62% → 71%, three runs) by itself provide sufficient evidence that a specific manipulated variable — the new catalyst — caused a real, statistically distinguishable, reproducible, generalizable improvement in this reaction's yield, and, if not, exactly which weaker claims does the data still support versus merely permit as speculation?

**Success criteria** (checkable against the Conclusion, section 6):
1. Section 6 lists, as specific claims, what is supported by the report's literal content alone — each such claim cites only the literal-content chains (C1, C6) and introduces no unstated assumption.
2. Section 6 lists, as specific claims, which stronger claims (statistical significance, real-effect-vs-noise, causal attribution to the catalyst, generalizability/economic value, reliability as a planning figure) are *not* supported, and each names the concrete missing datum (SD/raw values, controls statement, substrate scope, replicate count) that would resolve it.
3. At least one quantitative treatment (the estimate technique) shows, with an actual worked and independently-recomputed confidence-interval calculation, how wide the uncertainty band around an n=3 mean can be — this is a checkable property of the Ground Truths/Derivation Chains, not an assertion.
4. No chain in section 4 that section 6 relies on asserts, at HIGH confidence, either "the catalyst works" or "the catalyst doesn't work" — the Conclusion's confidence rating is calibrated to what the evidence actually licenses, not to a verdict either direction.

## 3. Ground Truths

**GT-1** The report states that yield rose from 62% to 71%, "measured across three runs." — source: the problem statement supplied for this analysis; read-at-source: direct quotation, the claim text itself.

**GT-2** The report specifies no standard deviation, range, or confidence interval for either figure; does not state whether "three runs" refers to the catalyzed condition only, to both conditions, or to something else; and does not state whether reaction conditions (temperature, stoichiometry, catalyst loading, solvent, workup, timing, substrate batch) or the yield-measurement method were held constant between the two conditions. — source: same problem statement; read-at-source: verified by direct inspection — nothing beyond the quoted sentence is given.

**GT-3** For a small sample (n<30), the (1−α)100% confidence interval for a sample mean requires the sample standard deviation s_x (or the raw data points from which it is computed), in addition to n: x̄ ± t_(α/2)(s_x/√n), with (n−1) degrees of freedom; no CI or significance test can be computed without s_x or the raw values. — source: Sally Bloodworth, "Reporting uncertainty: an updated convention for organic chemists," Zenodo record 14999714 ("Primer.pdf"); read-at-source: p.7, Appendix "Confidence interval" formula, quoted directly.

**GT-4** In that primer's worked exemplar, three replicate yields for "Compound 3" (56%, 66%, 45%) give a mean of 56% and a stated 95% CI of 30%–82% — a 52-percentage-point-wide interval; the primer explicitly labels this "an imagined dataset" (synthetic/illustrative, not measured lab data). — source: same Zenodo Primer; read-at-source: p.2, Figure 1 (data table) and Figure 2 (Compound 3 panel); independently recomputed here (t₀.₀₂₅,₂ = 4.303, s_x ≈ 10.5, SE ≈ 6.07, 56 ± 26.1 ≈ 30–82) and matches the source exactly.

**GT-5** In the same exemplar, "Compound 1" with seven replicate yields (56, 66, 45, 58, 59, 60, 58) gives a mean of 57% and a stated 95% CI of 52%–63% — an 11-point-wide interval, markedly narrower than Compound 3's despite comparable individual-value scatter (45–66). — source: same Zenodo Primer; read-at-source: p.2, Figure 1/Figure 2, Compound 1; independently recomputed here (mean 57.4, s_x ≈ 6.32, SE ≈ 2.39, t₀.₀₂₅,₆=2.447, CI ≈ 51.6–63.3) and matches the source.

**GT-6** Attributing an observed difference in an outcome specifically to one manipulated variable (here, the catalyst) requires that other variables capable of affecting the outcome were held constant between the compared conditions; if other variables could also have differed, the observed difference is logically consistent with any of those variables, singly or in combination, and cannot be uniquely assigned to the variable of interest without further evidence. — source: derivation shown inline (definition of a controlled comparison; yield is known to depend on multiple factors — temperature, stoichiometry, workup, measurement method — so an uncontrolled comparison under-determines the cause); classified as a matter of formal/methodological logic, not an external citation.

**GT-7** Regression toward the mean: when a value is observed to be unusually extreme partly because of random (noise) variation, an independent later measurement of the same underlying quantity will, on average, be less extreme than the originally observed value, because the specific noise draw that produced the extremity is not expected to recur. — source: derivation shown inline via variance decomposition (observed = true effect + independent noise; conditioning on an unusually high observed value raises the expected noise contribution, which does not repeat identically on remeasurement); the phenomenon is the standard one first described by Galton (1886) — no external citation opened for that historical attribution.

**Phase 3 failure record:** The source `https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/a-2907-9424.pdf` ("Letter: Standard Reporting of the Uncertainty in Reaction Yield," Thieme) — sought to verify A12 (typical real-world run-to-run yield variance) — could not be opened; WebFetch returned a bot-protection/"enable JavaScript and cookies" interstitial rather than article content. Reason: unreachable. The open alternative located (the Zenodo primer above, by the same author/topic) was read in full and satisfies GT-3/GT-4/GT-5, but it uses explicitly synthetic ("imagined") illustrative data, so it does **not** verify A12's empirical magnitude claim; A12 remains unverified and was deliberately not promoted to a ground truth (see Abandoned Reasoning, Dead End 1).

**Provenance summary:** `?`-marked: none (0 of 7). Read-at-source: GT-1 (problem statement, direct quote), GT-2 (problem statement, absence verified by inspection), GT-3 (Primer p.7), GT-4 (Primer p.2), GT-5 (Primer p.2). GT-6 and GT-7 are verified by inline derivation rather than external citation; neither feeds a HIGH-confidence chain on its own (GT-6 → C4, MEDIUM; GT-7 → C5, LOW), so Criterion 3's read-at-source-location requirement for HIGH chains does not apply to them.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

**Dead End 1: Treating "typical" organic-synthesis replicate-yield variance as a directly citable empirical ground truth.**
What was tried: searched for and attempted to fetch a peer-reviewed source (a Thieme-published letter on reaction-yield uncertainty reporting) and its open preprint (the Zenodo primer) to obtain an empirical figure for typical run-to-run standard deviation in bench-scale organic yields.
Why abandoned: the Thieme source was unreachable (bot/JS-protection interstitial, not article content — see Phase 3 failure record). The Zenodo primer was reachable and read in full, but its numeric example is explicitly labeled "imagined" test data — a synthetic teaching illustration for computing a CI/plot — not measured replicate data, so it cannot support an empirical "typical variance" claim without overclaiming from source.
What it ruled out: using this material as an unqualified empirical ground truth for "typical chemistry yield noise," which would have let the analysis claim too strongly that the reported 9-point gap is *probably* noise. This is why the primer's numbers are used only as a mathematical illustration of CI-arithmetic sensitivity to n (GT-4/GT-5, chain C3), explicitly caveated, while A12 (the empirical magnitude claim) stays flagged unverified.

**Dead End 2: Applying a full categorical fishbone brainstorm to the assumption space.**
What was tried: considered mapping the claim's hidden assumptions onto a fixed category set (e.g., People/Process/Technology/Environment/Information/Resources) before building the Assumptions Table.
Why abandoned: the assumption space here is a single evidentiary/methodological claim with a small, directly enumerable set of dependencies (what was held constant, how "three runs" and "yield" are defined, whether reporting was selective, whether the effect generalizes), fully surfaced by direct inversion-style reasoning (Phase 2) without category scaffolding; forcing these into categories built for production/service/knowledge-work contexts would have produced several empty or artificial buckets rather than added coverage.
What it ruled out: a category-first assumption pass for this problem; direct inversion-based enumeration is what actually surfaced the load-bearing preconditions (A1, A4, A5, A6, A11) used in the chains.

## 6. Conclusion

**What can be concluded (directly from the report's own content):**
- The report establishes that a mean yield increase from 62% to 71% (9 percentage points absolute, ≈14.5% relative) was observed and reported by the lab, based on three runs (chain C1).
- The report, as stated, does not specify the run-to-run spread, the exact definition of "three runs," or whether conditions were held constant, so its literal content is limited to that single point-to-point comparison (chain C1).
- Even a fully confirmed and correctly-attributed effect at this scale would say nothing about the catalyst's value on other substrates, its cost, selectivity, or scalability, none of which the report addresses (chain C6).

**What cannot be concluded (and why):**
- That the 9-point difference is statistically distinguishable from ordinary run-to-run noise — no standard deviation, range, or raw values are given, and no significance test or confidence interval can be computed without them (chain C2).
- That the 9-point gap is even a plausible signal rather than noise — an illustrative n=3 dataset with an unremarkable spread already produces a 95% confidence interval roughly 52 points wide, so a 9-point difference between two 3-run means is easily within reach of ordinary scatter alone (chain C3).
- That the increase was caused specifically by the catalyst — the report gives no basis for ruling out other differences (conditions, measurement method, or run selection) between the two sets of runs (chain C4).
- That the 71% figure generalizes to other substrates or scales, or is economically favorable — the report addresses only yield for what appears to be a single reaction (chain C6).
- That the number is a reliable, already-settled planning figure — small, unreplicated samples are exactly the kind of result that regresses toward a smaller true effect on replication, so downstream decisions should wait for confirmation rather than treat 71% as fixed (chain C5).

**Recommended approach:** Treat the 62%→71% report as a preliminary, promising observation that warrants targeted follow-up — specifically, obtain the per-run yield values (or at minimum the standard deviation) for both conditions, confirm that conditions and measurement method were held constant, and run additional replicates before relying on the 9-point figure for any downstream decision (chains C2, C3, C4).

**Key insight:** The report's central weakness is not that the catalyst claim is wrong — it is that its own numbers cannot answer the question they are being used to answer. With n=3 and no reported spread, the arithmetic of small-sample confidence intervals alone (not any suspicion about the chemistry) is already enough to make a 9-point gap statistically indeterminate — a sharper, more specific diagnosis than the vaguer complaint that "more data would be nice" (chain C3).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This analysis deliberately withholds a yes/no verdict on whether the catalyst effect is real, which is less satisfying than a clean answer; the alternative — accepting or rejecting the claim outright on the numbers given — would trade honesty about the evidentiary gap for a false sense of resolution (chain C2).

**Pre-check:** head C1 (HIGH), C2 (HIGH), C3 (LOW), C4 (MEDIUM), C5 (LOW), C6 (HIGH) · ?-marked: none · lowest cited: LOW · Inputs ceiling: LOW
**Confidence:** LOW — the core epistemic finding (indeterminacy, C1/C2) is HIGH and robust; the section's overall rating is pulled down because it also relies on C3 (LOW — illustrative-magnitude and precision-rival not settled) and C5 (LOW — behavioral/regression-to-the-mean assumptions unpriced), and C4 (MEDIUM — controls-existed-but-unstated rival not settled). This LOW overall rating is the expected, calibrated shape of an analysis whose subject is irreducible uncertainty, not a defect.

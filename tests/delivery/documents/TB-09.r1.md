# Leaving a stable job for a 4-person startup at -30% pay: a first-principles framework

## The real question isn't "should I do this" — it's what has to be true

Strip away the framing and the actual decision is: **is the expected value of 2-3 years of below-market cash comp + high-variance equity + skill/network compounding greater than the expected value of staying, once you price in how thin your downside buffer actually is?** You don't have the numbers to compute that precisely, and neither do I — so the useful output here is the *structure* of that calculation plus the base rates, not a verdict.

## Ground truths worth anchoring to (not opinions)

- **Startup survival, not "success," is the right first filter.** Roughly 20% of new US businesses fail in year 1, ~45% by year 5 (BLS data, widely cited). For VC-track startups specifically, the commonly cited range is that ~65-75% fail to return investor capital, and only a small single-digit percentage return more than 10x. A 4-person company is pre-product-market-fit almost by definition — it's earlier and riskier than the "startup failure rate" statistics that get quoted casually, most of which are computed on companies that already raised a seed round and had some traction.
- **Employee equity at this stage is a lottery ticket, not compensation.** Even conditional on the company surviving and eventually having an exit, dilution across future rounds routinely cuts an early hire's percentage by 50-80% by the time of an exit. The number on your offer letter (e.g., "0.5%") is almost never the number you'd actually hold at exit.
- **11 months of runway is a static number describing a dynamic situation.** It assumes your burn rate stays flat. It won't — moving to a startup salary probably *lowers* your monthly burn need relative to your old salary (less cushion, but also less to replace), but a layoff/failure event compounds with the *hardest possible time to job search* (you'll be job-hunting alongside everyone laid off by the same failed startup, in the same local market, at the same moment).
- **Health insurance, retirement matching, and PTO are quasi-cash and 4-person startups often don't have them dialed in.** A "30% pay cut" that also drops a 4% 401k match and shifts you to a worse health plan is really more like a 35-38% total-comp cut. This is almost always underweighted in these decisions because it's not on the offer letter's headline number.
- **Age 34-36 is not a neutral two years.** It's early-mid career, past the phase where "startup failed" reads as youthful risk-taking with zero cost, but before the phase where family/health/mortgage obligations typically compound the cost of instability further. It is, statistically, one of the *lowest-cost* windows in a career to take this kind of swing — which cuts in favor of the startup, independent of how the equity turns out.

## What's actually reversible here — this matters more than people think

- **Reversible:** the cash-comp hit, the title, the day-to-day work. If it doesn't work out, you go back to the job market with a story ("early employee at a startup, wore many hats, learned X") that reads *fine* to most hiring managers — not a red flag, in most industries.
- **Not fully reversible:** the specific 11 months of runway if it gets burned during a bad job search; retirement contributions not made during this window (compounding lost, not just delayed); and, in a small number of industries (some regulated finance, some big-company tracks with rigid seniority ladders), a multi-year gap from the "traditional" path can be harder to re-enter than others.
- The single biggest asymmetry in this decision: **the downside is bounded and mostly reversible (you can get another job), while the upside is unbounded but low-probability.** That's the classic profile of a bet worth taking *only if you can survive the downside without real damage* — which is exactly what "11 months runway" is supposed to tell you, except runway alone doesn't tell you whether the downside is survivable; it only tells you the clock length.

## A rough Fermi estimate on the equity, to calibrate expectations

Take a plausible mid-range: 4-person startup, you're offered something like 1-2% equity (typical early-employee range at this stage, highly variable).

- P(any liquidity event at all — acquisition or IPO) for a company this early: roughly 10-25% over a 5-10 year horizon (this is generous; most 4-person startups simply shut down or zombie out quietly).
- Conditional on a liquidity event, dilution to a Series A/B/C-plus scenario typically leaves an early 1-2% grant at more like 0.3-0.8% by exit.
- Conditional on exit, the distribution of outcomes is extremely fat-tailed: most "successful" exits are modest (acqui-hire, small acquisition — often life-changing-but-not-crazy money, e.g., low-to-mid six figures for a small stake) and a tiny fraction are the 50-100x outlier everyone pictures.

Multiplying through: **expected value of the equity is very likely under $50-150k in today's-dollars terms for a "normal" outcome distribution**, with a small tail probability of a genuinely large number and a large probability (60-80%+) of $0. That's the number to compare against 2-3 years of a 30-38% total-comp cut, not the headline "you'll have equity in a startup" framing. If your annual comp is, say, $150k, a 30% cut for 2 years is roughly $90k in foregone cash — so on a naive expected-value basis, the equity alone often does *not* clearly clear that bar. **The decision is only clearly positive-EV once you add in the non-equity second-order value** (below) — which is real, but harder to put a number on, and is where the honest case for "yes" actually lives.

## Second-order effects that usually decide this in practice

- **Skill and title compression.** At a 4-person company you will very likely do 3-5x the range of things you'd touch at a stable job (may end up closer to a generalist/founding-engineer/ops-hybrid role) — this is real career capital *independent of whether the company survives*, and it's the part of the equity story people usually undersell versus overselling the stock.
- **Network effects.** Early-stage founders and early employees disproportionately end up at each other's next companies, feeding each other deal flow, references, and future opportunities. This is a legitimate, non-equity return on the bet.
- **Resume narrative risk is asymmetric by industry.** In tech/startups, a failed startup on your resume is neutral-to-positive. In more conservative fields (traditional finance, some government-adjacent work, large enterprise tracks), it can read worse. This is one of the few facts about *your specific field* that would flip the analysis.
- **Opportunity cost isn't just money — it's what the stable job's growth trajectory would have looked like anyway.** If the current job is a dead-end/plateaued role, the "cost" of leaving it is much lower than the framing suggests. If it's on a strong promotion/comp trajectory, the real comparison is startup-EV vs. (stable-job-cash + its own upward trajectory), not vs. today's stable-job salary.

## Pre-mortem: how this fails, and what would tell you early

Imagining you're 14 months in and it's gone badly — the most common failure clusters are:

1. **Runway math was wrong** — the startup itself runs out of money before any milestone that would let it raise again, and you're laid off with less notice than a "stable job" would have given you. *Tripwire:* if the startup doesn't have 12+ months of its own runway (or a credible path to raising within 6 months), your 11-month personal runway and the company's runway are racing each other, and you should know which one is shorter before you sign.
2. **Equity turns out to be non-standard or subordinated** — verbal "equity" that's never formalized, or formalized with terms (long cliff, punitive vesting, no acceleration on acquisition) that make it worth far less than assumed. *Tripwire:* no term sheet / cap table visibility before you accept.
3. **The 30% cut becomes a 40%+ cut in practice** once you account for benefits, and you don't notice until the first medical bill or missed 401k contribution.
4. **The role isn't what was pitched** — 4-person startups pivot roles constantly; "product person" becomes "does everything including sales," which may be fine or may be a mismatch you didn't sign up for.
5. **You stay too long past the point of diminishing returns** — the sunk-cost version of this bet is staying 18 months past when the writing was on the wall, burning the reversibility advantage that made the bet safe in the first place.

The single biggest structural risk across all of these is #1 and #2 combined: **you're taking a risk correlated with a company you have no visibility into (funding runway, cap table, terms), which means your actual personal risk is not "11 months of runway" but "11 months minus however early the company itself fails," and you likely don't have that number today.**

## What would most change the recommendation — in order of leverage

1. **The startup's own funding runway and stage.** Pre-seed with founder money and no committed follow-on is a fundamentally different bet than post-seed with 18+ months of runway and a lead investor already lined up for the next round. This is the single highest-leverage unknown — more important than your own savings number.
2. **Dependents / partner income.** A working partner with stable income and/or their own health coverage effectively doubles your real runway and de-risks this enormously; dependents relying solely on your income cuts hard the other way.
3. **What "11 months" is measured against.** If it's 11 months at your *current* burn rate and your burn rate would actually drop at startup salary (smaller apartment, no expensive commute, etc.), your real runway is longer than the headline number; if your expenses are fixed (mortgage, kids' school), it's exactly as stated or worse.
4. **Whether you actually have visibility into the cap table and vesting terms**, not just a verbal equity percentage.
5. **Your own honest answer to "why do I want to leave"** — if it's genuine excitement about the work/team, the second-order career-capital case is strong regardless of the financial outcome; if it's primarily "I'm bored/frustrated with my current job," it's worth asking whether a lateral move at full pay would solve 80% of the actual problem at a fraction of the risk.

## Bottom line

On the pure equity-expected-value math, this is usually a **marginal-to-negative bet** — most 4-person-startup equity is worth less in expectation than the cash you're giving up. The decision only clears the bar when you weight in the non-equity returns (skill compression, network, career narrative) *and* when your personal financial buffer is genuinely robust to the downside (dependents covered, real runway beyond the stated number, benefits gap priced in). It gets meaningfully better if the startup itself is well-funded and the terms are real; meaningfully worse if you don't yet know those things. Before deciding, the highest-value thing to actually go find out isn't more introspection — it's the startup's own runway and your cap table terms in writing; that single fact will move this analysis more than any other input you don't currently have.

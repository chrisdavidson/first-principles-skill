## Bottom line

**No — don't add a freemium tier now.** The stated reason ("competitors have one") is competitive mimicry, not validated need, and it doesn't survive contact with this business's actual mechanics. The better first move is a **scoped, sales-routed free trial**, not an always-on free tier — and three specific conditions should be true before freemium is reconsidered.

## Why: the assumption doesn't hold up

The premise baked into the question — "competitors have a free tier, so we're exposed without one" — is evidence of *parity anxiety*, not of lost deals. Nothing in the facts says a single prospect has chosen a competitor over this company *because* of a free tier. That's the first thing to check before building anything: if sales starts logging deals lost specifically to "they let us try it free," that's a real signal. Right now it isn't given, so the case rests on FOMO alone.

Reasoning upward from the actual mechanics:

- **The funnel a free tier creates has nowhere to go.** This is a $10k/team, outbound+referral, team-based sale with zero self-serve inbound. A free tier's entire value in a PLG model is that usage data and in-product nudges convert users without a rep touching them. This company has no self-serve signup, no in-product upgrade path, no usage-based trigger — none of the machinery that makes free-to-paid conversion happen on its own. Built today, a free tier produces anonymous signups that just sit there, because there's no mechanism to turn them into the enterprise/team deals that actually generate revenue.
- **The cost is certain; the benefit is speculative.** Finance has confirmed infra/support cost scales with *active* users, including free ones — so this isn't a free experiment. Against that certain cost, there's no internal conversion data and no comparable self-serve motion to benchmark from. Certain cost vs. unvalidated upside is a bad trade to make reactively.
- **It can cannibalize the motion that already works.** Dropping a free option next to a $10k/team outbound sale risks smaller/price-sensitive teams in the existing 240 (or near-term pipeline) self-serving around the sales process, reps having to argue against their own company's free option, and a muddied enterprise positioning ("come try it free" vs. high-touch team sales).
- **"Add a free tier" is actually three different strategic bets being treated as one.** Competitive parity, pipeline/lead generation for outbound, and a genuine product-led-growth channel each need a different design and a different bar for success. The question as posed collapses them — which is itself a sign no one has decided what the free tier is *for*.

## Better first move

Run a **time-boxed, sales-qualified trial** instead: full-feature access for 14–30 days, gated behind a signup that routes straight to sales as a lead (PQL), not an indefinite free account. This:
- Keeps the cost exposure bounded (not open-ended active-user scaling forever).
- Generates the first real trial→paid conversion benchmark this company has ever had.
- Fits the existing outbound motion (reps get warmer leads) instead of fighting it.
- Tests competitive pressure cheaply — if trial signups reference competitors' free tiers as the reason they looked, that's the real evidence the FOMO premise needs.

## What would need to be true before revisiting a real freemium tier

1. A self-serve signup/upgrade path exists (or is being deliberately built as its own PLG initiative, not bolted on reactively).
2. Trial data shows a conversion rate and CAC that beats or meaningfully complements outbound — not assumed, measured.
3. The free tier targets a segment outbound doesn't economically reach today (small teams below the $10k threshold), so it expands the market rather than discounting the existing one.

**Confidence: high** on the no-go for a full freemium tier under current conditions (outbound-only funnel + certain active-user cost + zero conversion data make the downside concrete and the upside speculative). **Medium** on the trial-based alternative being the optimal first move specifically — it's the lowest-risk way to generate the missing data, but it's a reasoned recommendation, not something this company has tested either.

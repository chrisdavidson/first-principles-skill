## Executive Summary

**Recommendation:** Don't treat this as an accept/decline binary — test whether the offer's terms are negotiable, correct the headline $70K for cost-of-living and equity risk, and have an explicit joint conversation with your partner before choosing among accepting, declining, or negotiating a remote-heavy composite (chain C4, chain C8).

**Band (from §6):** MEDIUM (chain C8)

**Would change it:** Your actual current total comp relative to the ~$100K–$137K breakeven, the new offer's equity vesting/liquidity details, and your partner's actual willingness to relocate or sustain distance (chain C1, chain C3, chain C5).

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given a $70,000/year nominal "more" offer that requires relocating to the SF Bay Area and working 3 days/week in-office, and a partner whose Portland-based career has no remote option, decide among accept-as-offered, decline, and counter-negotiate — in a way that first corrects for whether the headline $70K figure, the take-it-or-leave-it framing, and the "partner's career is the one that must bend" premise are actually true, rather than accepting them as given.

**Success criteria:**
1. The Conclusion states, via a named derivation chain, whether the $70K figure is a real (cost-of-living- and risk-adjusted) increase, a wash, or a decrease, under an explicit adjustment method a reader could rerun with their own numbers.
2. The Conclusion names at least one specific term of the offer whose negotiability is untested, directly confirming or refuting the take-it-or-leave-it framing.
3. The Conclusion carries a derivation chain in which the partner's career continuity is a first-class variable with its own chain, not a footnote folded into the compensation chain.
4. The Conclusion enumerates, by ID, the specific missing data points that would most change which option ranks highest — not merely a statement that data is missing.
5. The Conclusion supplies a decision procedure the user can execute with information only the user and partner hold, rather than a single verdict asserted on their behalf.

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: The $70K nominal raise is only a real-terms raise below a calculable, cost-of-living-based breakeven comp level

GT-1 (5 YOE remote SWE, Portland) + GT-2 ($70K/yr more, combined base+equity) + GT-5 (current total comp unconfirmed) + GT-6 (SF COL ≈1.51–1.53× Portland, housing-driven)
→ the real-terms value of a nominal raise R, given a cost-of-living multiplier r the earner must pay after moving, equals (C+R)/r − C, where C is current comp
→ solving (C+R)/r − C = 0 for C gives a breakeven current-comp threshold C* = (R/r) / (1 − 1/r)
→ substituting R=$70,000 and r=1.51–1.53 (GT-6) places C* at roughly $132,000–$137,000 [Assumes: A-13 — the COL multiplier is stable over the medium term, not a temporary spike or dip]
→ below C*, the move is a real-terms raise; at or above C*, the nominal $70K is a real-terms wash or loss once Bay Area cost of living is priced in

**Confidence:** HIGH — the rival "just compare the $70K at face value" is named and ruled out (see Abandoned Reasoning, Dead End 1); the arithmetic recomputes independently (see the Validate section's Recompute step); the conclusion is stated as a conditional threshold model, not as a claim about the user's actual current comp, so it does not depend on the unconfirmed value in GT-5 — it only cites GT-5 to flag that the threshold's real-world applicability to this specific user is unresolved. A-13 is that the multiplier is stable; if it were not (e.g. a sudden Portland housing boom or SF crash), the threshold would shift but the conditional-model form of the conclusion would be unaffected, so the endpoint stands either way.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Naive face-value comparison of the $70K figure

**What was tried:** Treating "$70K/year more" as a self-evidently positive number to be weighed directly against relocation hassle.

**Why abandoned:** GT-6 (SF cost of living ≈1.51–1.53× Portland, housing-driven) and GT-8? (equity realizability unknown) show the nominal figure is neither cost-of-living- nor risk-adjusted; C1 and C3 demonstrate the real-terms effect depends entirely on current comp and equity structure, both unknown (GT-5, GT-8?), and can be zero or negative across a plausible range of current-comp levels.

**What it ruled out:** Any conclusion of the form "$70K is obviously worth it" or "$70K is obviously not enough" stated without reference to current comp, cost of living, and equity structure.

### Dead End: Treating the partner's career as the one that must bend because that is how the situation was described

**What was tried:** Accepting the framing's implicit premise that relocation logistics default onto the partner, and reasoning forward from there.

**Why abandoned:** This premise is an untested belief (A-5), not a ground truth; GT-10? shows comparative portability is unconfirmed, and C5 shows the relationship variable is large enough that assuming an answer here would bias the entire downstream analysis toward "accept" without justification.

**What it ruled out:** Any conclusion that silently treats "the user relocates, the partner adapts" as the default path rather than one of several options the couple has not yet evaluated together.

### Dead End: Collapsing the trade-off into a single declared "winner" option with analyst-assigned weights

**What was tried:** Assigning weights to the seven C8 trade-off criteria meant to reflect "typical" preferences, in order to declare one option the answer.

**Why abandoned:** The Input Contract and GT-5 both establish that the comp-vs-relationship weighting is a personal value only the user and partner can set; asserting a specific weighting on their behalf would launder this analysis's own guess as the user's actual preference. The flip test in C8 shows the ranking among the three leading options (A, D, E) reorders under a small, plausible weight change — meaning no single weighting is defensible as "the" answer without that data.

**What it ruled out:** A single best-option verdict presented as fact-driven rather than values-driven; replaced by the calibrated MEDIUM-confidence framework in C8 and §6.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Treat this as a three-way choice among (1) accept-as-offered, (2) decline, and (3) counter-negotiate — either while relocating or toward a majority-remote composite — rather than the binary the offer's framing implies (chain C4); before choosing among them, replace the headline $70K figure with the cost-of-living-, tax-, and equity-adjusted breakeven model in chains C1–C3, and resolve the GT-N? inputs that most change the ranking — current total comp, the equity's realizable value, and the partner's actual willingness/ability to relocate or sustain long-distance — through direct fact-finding and a structured conversation with the partner (chains C5, C8).

**Key insight:** The headline "$70K/year more" is not inherently a real-terms raise: once Bay Area cost of living (chain C1) and the equity portion's liquidity/vesting risk (chain C3) are priced in, it only clears breakeven below a calculable current-comp threshold of roughly $100,000–$137,000 (chains C1, C3) — a threshold this analysis cannot resolve further without the one number GT-5 flags as missing.

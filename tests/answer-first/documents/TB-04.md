## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given a policy target of "cut commuter car journeys 20% in 3 years" and three named candidate levers (free transit, congestion pricing, protected cycle lanes) whose real-world effectiveness is well-documented but highly context-dependent, what set of local facts and decision criteria should a mid-sized city actually gather and weigh in order to choose (and sequence) among them — rather than which single lever is abstractly "best"?

**Success criteria:**
1. The analysis names the specific local facts (measurable, gatherable by a real city) that determine which lever(s) can plausibly work — not just abstract pros/cons.
2. Each lever's real-world car-trip-reduction magnitude and timescale is grounded in cited evidence, with uncertainty in that evidence explicitly flagged rather than presented as settled.
3. The analysis explicitly tests whether the framing itself (mutual exclusivity of the three options; the 20%/3-year target; "commuter car journeys" as the unit; context-independence) survives scrutiny, and revises the question if it does not.
4. The final recommendation is a decision *procedure* (what to measure, what to weigh, in what sequence) rather than a single named winner asserted to hold for all mid-sized cities.

## 2. Assumptions Table

### Companion-technique pass: 5-Whys (causal mode) — why does the city want this cut?

The prompt states the target ("cut commuter car journeys 20% within three years") but not the underlying reason. Root cause matters here because different root goals imply different correct metrics and different correct geographic scope:

- **Why cut car journeys?** → Candidate root drivers, each independently plausible and non-exclusive: (a) congestion/travel-time relief, (b) emissions/climate-target compliance, (c) public health (activity, road safety, air quality), (d) equity/access for non-drivers, (e) fiscal (revenue or avoided road/parking capital spend), (f) reclaiming road space for housing/public realm.
- **Counterfactual test per branch:** each branch is a real, commonly-documented municipal driver (not contradicted by any gathered evidence), but none can be confirmed or ruled out without the specific city naming its own priority — this is itself the finding, not a gap in the analysis.
- **Verdict:** `Root cause — [the specific driver], within the city council's ability to state; not determinable from the problem statement as given`, for every branch. This routes directly into the Assumptions Table (row 2) and into the final decision procedure (§6): the dominant root driver should be named explicitly before scoring levers, because congestion pricing best serves (a)+(e), free transit best serves (d)+(c), and cycle lanes best serve (c)+(f).

### Companion-technique pass: Fishbone — what drives car-commuting in the first place?

Category set used: the default six-category set (People, Process, Technology/Infrastructure, Environment, Information, Resources), chosen because this is a multi-causal problem with no single dominant category and no obvious fit for 6M/8P/4S.

| Category | Candidate causes | Discriminating observation |
|---|---|---|
| People | Habit/inertia; comfort/privacy preference; perceived unsafety of transit/cycling | Survey drivers who live within 2km of a high-frequency transit stop: if most still drive, habit/comfort dominates over access |
| Process | Trip-chaining (school drop-off, errands); irregular/shift-work hours misaligned with transit schedules | Compare car-commute rate for parents with young children vs. childless commuters on the same corridor |
| Technology/Infrastructure | Road capacity/free-flow speed; transit frequency/coverage gaps; absence of protected cycling infrastructure | Compare car mode share on corridors with <15-min transit headways vs. >30-min headways |
| Environment | Low-density/sprawled land use separating home and work; hills; climate exposure | Compare car mode share by commute distance band and by season |
| Information | Low awareness of routes/schedules/real-time info | Compare car mode share before/after a targeted transit-information campaign in a comparable corridor (if any local pilot exists) |
| Resources/Economics | Free or underpriced employer/street parking; low marginal fuel cost relative to income; sunk cost of car ownership already paid | Compare car mode share for commuters with employer-paid parking vs. those without |

No candidate cause here is verified for any specific city — each becomes an `untested belief` row below, per the treatment prescribed for fishbone output. The key structural finding is that **at most one of the six categories (Technology/Infrastructure) is directly addressed by any of the three named interventions**; the other five (People, Process, Environment, Information, Resources) are left untouched by all three levers unless a city separately gathers evidence that Infrastructure is in fact the locally dominant category.

### Companion-technique pass: Inversion — what would guarantee the 20%-in-3-years goal fails?

1. The chosen lever's geographic/population coverage is mathematically too small to reach 20% citywide even at high within-scope effectiveness (e.g., a narrow downtown cordon).
2. A free-transit or cycle-lane program draws most of its new users from existing walkers/cyclists or from induced (net-new) trips, not from car commuters, so ridership/mode-share optics rise while car trips do not fall.
3. A congestion-pricing scheme is put to a binding public vote before residents experience it and is rejected, ending the lever entirely before any benefit accrues.
4. Toll or fare revenue promised for reinvestment in alternatives is diverted to the general fund, breaking the mechanism that made comparable schemes elsewhere durable and equitable.
5. The city changes or never fixes its measurement unit (trip count vs. vehicle-kilometres vs. peak-period volume), so success or failure cannot be verified even if the underlying behavior changed.
6. Removing road capacity for cycle lanes triggers political backlash strong enough to force reversal before multi-year mode-share gains have time to materialize.

Each precondition whose absence would cause one of these becomes an `untested belief` row below; items 1, 3, and 5 are tagged **load-bearing** — the conclusion (any lever choice) does not survive if these fail.

- **Congestion pricing** (typical downtown cordon): coverage 10%–35% of citywide commute trips (monocentric core vs. polycentric/sprawled city); within-zone effectiveness 10%–25% within year 1 (GT-1?, GT-3?, GT-4). Bracket: **1%–8.75%**, central ≈0.20×0.18 = **3.6%**.

### Theoretical-limit-flavored pass — feeds C2

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Picking a single winning intervention in the abstract

**What was tried:** An initial pass attempted to rank congestion pricing, free transit, and cycle lanes as options a generic mid-sized city should choose among, independent of local data.

**Why abandoned:** The trade-off flip test (C5) shows the ranking between the top two candidates changes with a 2-point shift in a single criterion's weight, and the Fermi estimate (C1) shows achievable magnitude is dominated by locally-variable coverage fractions rather than by any lever's intrinsic superiority. An abstract ranking is therefore not a stable, defensible answer to the question actually asked.

**What it ruled out:** Any recommendation of the form "always choose X" independent of local context; this finding is what redirected the analysis toward a decision-procedure conclusion (C7) instead of a named winner.

### Dead End: Treating fare-free transit as a car-trip-reduction lever comparable in kind to pricing

**What was tried:** An early framing modeled free transit and congestion pricing as interchangeable instances of "lower the relative cost of the alternative to driving," sharing a single elasticity parameter in the Fermi estimate.

**Why abandoned:** GT-7? and GT-9? show free transit's dominant effect is drawing from walking/cycling and inducing net-new trips, not diverting car trips — a structurally different mechanism from pricing's direct cost-imposition on driving itself. A shared elasticity parameter would have overstated free transit's car-reduction potential.

**What it ruled out:** Using one shared elasticity/response parameter across all three levers in the Fermi estimate; the estimate instead uses lever-specific coverage and effectiveness brackets.

### Dead End: Citing the "51 of 63 cases, median 11%" traffic-evaporation statistic as a verified figure

**What was tried:** The analysis initially planned to present the precise case-count and median-reduction statistic for the traffic-evaporation literature as a directly verified number.

**Why abandoned:** The Phase 3 verification step opened a secondary source (travelwest.info) that corroborated the qualitative finding but did not reproduce the specific 51/63-cases or 11%/16%-median figures; the primary Cairns/Goodwin report was not opened this session, so the precise statistic could not be confirmed.

**What it ruled out:** Presenting a specific, citable percentage for traffic evaporation as verified (GT-12 carries `?` on the number); the analysis instead relies on the better-corroborated qualitative finding that displaced traffic does not fully reappear elsewhere.

### Dead End: Treating "trial before vote" as a general law guaranteeing political success

**What was tried:** An early draft of C3 stated the political-sequencing finding as a near-certain rule: run a trial, then a vote will pass.

**Why abandoned:** The same September 2006 referendum round saw 14 other Swedish municipalities vote no even after the same regional trial, and Stockholm's own margin was a modest 53%–47%, not a landslide. Trial-then-vote raises the odds of passage; it does not guarantee it.

**What it ruled out:** Overstating C3 to HIGH confidence or presenting political-sequencing as a deterministic fix; C3 is worded as a sensitivity claim.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** A mid-sized city should not pick one of the three named interventions in the abstract; it should gather the six local facts named in chain C7 — commute-trip coverage a plausible pricing cordon or cycle/transit network would actually capture, spare transit capacity, density/hill/climate profile, the legal referendum requirement and whether a trial can precede it, the true underlying policy driver, and its capital/revenue-dedication authority — and score the same seven weighted criteria used in C5 against those facts (chain C7). Where a binding public vote is legally required for pricing, sequence it as a time-limited trial followed by a vote, not a cold vote (chain C3), and legally lock any pricing revenue into a dedicated transit/cycling reinvestment fund rather than leaving it to discretionary budgeting (chain C6).

**Key insight:** No single lever, implemented in the form most commonly documented in the real-world cases examined, can mathematically reach a 20%-citywide-cut-in-3-years target on its own — the ceiling is set by the product of each lever's geographic coverage and its per-commuter effectiveness, and even the most optimistic single-lever bracket falls well short of 20% (chain C1). This is not visible from a qualitative comparison of the three options; it only emerges from decomposing "how big" into coverage × effectiveness and bracketing each factor against cited evidence.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Congestion pricing is the fastest-acting and best-evidenced single lever (chains C1, C2), but its political durability is not fixed — it depends on implementation sequencing (chain C3) and on whether local law can accommodate a trial-before-vote path. A combination of pricing with dedicated reinvestment narrowly outscores pricing alone in the illustrative trade-off matrix, but that margin is a near-tie that flips under a small change to how a specific city weighs political durability, equity, and raw magnitude against each other (chain C5) — no chain — flagged assumption only for which side of that near-tie any specific city will land on. The combination's added durability and growing magnitude in years 4+ depend on legally protecting the revenue-reinvestment link, which is itself an execution risk, not a guarantee (chain C6, LOW confidence). Free-transit-alone and cycle-lanes-alone score clearly lower on achievable 3-year car-trip magnitude in this evidence base (chain C1, chain C5), even though they carry the strongest documented equity and (for cycling) co-benefit profiles — a city whose dominant underlying goal is equity or public health rather than congestion/emissions relief may reasonably choose differently than this magnitude-weighted ranking suggests (chain C5, chain C7).

## Three structural weaknesses (where most migrations actually die)
These are clusters where multiple failure chains share one root — fix these first:

1. **Untested rollback** — A rollback plan on paper is a hope, not a plan. If you haven't *executed* it end-to-end (and test-restored the snapshot), you don't have one.
2. **Validation-by-counting** — Matching row counts hides silent corruption: wrong units, timezones, enums, truncation. Counts pass; meaning is wrong; a consumer finds out weeks later.
3. **Scale-gap between rehearsal and production** — Timing, locking, and partial-failure modes only appear at full volume. A 10-min sample run can become a 14-hour run that blows the cutover window.

## Other failure modes worth mapping to your situation
- **Source data**: unprofiled nulls, duplicates, encoding issues, orphaned foreign keys, decade-old legacy formats breaking the run mid-stream
- **Mid-run failure**: partial target state with no clean resume point
- **Writes during cutover**: no freeze → in-flight writes lost
- **Downstream consumers**: a report/integration depending on the old shape breaks silently
- **Human single-point**: the one person who understands the legacy schema is on vacation at cutover

## Corrective actions (turn confidence into *earned* confidence)
1. Profile the source data — trust nothing unprofiled
2. Full-scale **timed** dry run on production-volume data in staging
3. **Execute** the rollback end-to-end, don't just document it
4. Semantic/field-level validation on the **full** set, not a sample
5. Freeze writes during cutover (or capture-and-replay deltas)
6. Confirm every downstream consumer tolerates the new shape
7. Define the **abort criterion** in advance, so rollback isn't decided under pressure

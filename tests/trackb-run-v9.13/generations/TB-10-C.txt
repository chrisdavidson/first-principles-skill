## The core reframe

Most families treat this as "should we move closer, yes or no" driven by guilt or a vague sense that proximity = care. That's the wrong frame. **What should actually drive it is the relative rate of two largely unmeasured, family-specific clocks**: how fast your parents' care needs are genuinely escalating, versus how costly it is to reverse your own jobs and your kids' schooling. Most people substitute a felt obligation for actually measuring that race.

## Key findings

**1. Proximity is a cost-reducer, not a solution.** Distance lowers the marginal cost of hands-on help, but caregiving capacity also depends on money, willingness, and expertise you may not have regardless of distance. Moving next door doesn't fix a need that requires professional/medical care.

**2. "Wait and see" isn't the safe default it feels like.** Both directions have irreversible costs: leaving jobs destroys firm-specific capital (seniority, unvested equity, network) that doesn't fully come back if the move doesn't work out — but time not spent near a parent during their remaining healthy years is also gone permanently, whatever you decide later. Neither side is the "free" option.

**3. Guilt-driven urgency is a signal to investigate, not a decision input.** It's generated by anticipated-regret bias, independent of your parents' actual measured need. Treat the pull toward moving as a prompt to go find out the real facts, not as evidence in itself.

**4. Usually the two clocks aren't racing — but you have to check.** If your parents are currently in reasonably good health, the runway before intensive support is needed (often years) is typically much longer than your own adjustment period after a move (months). That means there's often room for a deliberate diagnostic period rather than a forced immediate decision — *unless* an acute health event has already happened, in which case this inverts and speed matters.

**5. Under generic weighting, a full relocation doesn't come out ahead.** A structured comparison across care coverage, reversibility, financial risk, child impact, parents' autonomy, and family capacity favored staying-and-scaling-support over full relocation, with partial/staged moves and "parent relocates instead" both beating full relocation too. The one criterion where full relocation could win — actual care-need coverage — is exactly the number nobody's measured yet. This is a demonstration of method, not a verdict for your family; rerun it with real numbers.

**6. No option is permanent.** Whatever you choose now, your parents' needs can progress past what any distance can fix (e.g., dementia, skilled nursing). A durable plan names in advance the conditions under which you'll bring in professional/institutional care, rather than treating today's choice as final. Also worth flagging: once one household visibly "has it handled," other relatives sometimes quietly withdraw — proximity can concentrate caregiving load rather than expand total capacity.

## What should actually drive the decision — a 6-step procedure

1. **Get a real functional/geriatric assessment** of your parents (or a structured self-report) — replace the felt urgency with an actual number.
2. **Cost out non-relocation alternatives first** — paid care, remote coordination, adult day programs, a staged move, or the parent moving to you.
3. **Ask your parents what they actually want, and give it real veto power** — don't assume they want you there.
4. **Weight by reversibility, not by guilt** — default to staging unless step 1 shows the runway is genuinely short.
5. **Score the options with your real weighted numbers**, not generic ones.
6. **Pre-name the trigger conditions** for adding professional care, since no option is a permanent fix.

## Pre-mortem: how this goes wrong

The clustered failure modes if this fails badly in two years: the decision got made on a compressed emotional timeline without measuring actual need; both new jobs' income risk moved together and both underperformed at once; a teenager lost their social footing at a bad time; the parent was moved into arrangements they didn't actually want; needs progressed to a level (e.g., dementia) that no amount of proximity could have addressed; siblings quietly withdrew once one household "took over"; and three major stressors (two jobs, a move, eldercare) landed simultaneously with no reserve capacity. Concrete mitigations: sequence the two job changes rather than doing both at once, rent rather than buy for the first year, and don't stack every irreversible change into the same season.

**Honest limitation:** this was run generically (no real ages, health status, distance, or finances), so overall confidence is capped LOW — several ground truths (aging trajectory, job-market reversibility, distance-caregiving correlation) are general consensus, not verified against your specifics. The procedure itself is sound; the actual answer for your family depends on the numbers step 1 and step 5 would surface.

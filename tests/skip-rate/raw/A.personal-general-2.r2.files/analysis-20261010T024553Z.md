## 4. Derivation Chains

### Supporting technique: Theoretical-Limit (what a guaranteed return "beating the market" actually means)

**Governing constraint:** in a liquid, continuously-traded market for genuinely risk-free capital, arbitrage pressure keeps the yields on equivalently-safe instruments close together — the closest thing finance has to a hard ceiling for *liquid* risk-free money is simply the prevailing risk-free rate itself.

- **Ideal ceiling (liquid risk-free money):** the 10-year Treasury yield, GT-9 ≈ 4.64% (bracketed up to ≈4.95% by GT-10?).
- **Best demonstrated (liquid, retail-accessible, insured):** top CD/HYSA APYs, GT-10? ≈ 4.1%–4.4%.
- **Conventional figure in this household's actual situation:** the mortgage paydown's guaranteed, untaxed effective yield, GT-6? ≈ 6.43%.
- **Direction:** higher is better (maximizing a guaranteed rate).
- **Gap, conventional → best demonstrated:** ≈ 2.0–2.3 points of headroom, already captured by this household today, simply by choosing where to put safe money.
- **Gap, best demonstrated → ideal ceiling:** ≈ 0.2–0.9 points — small relative to the first gap, because CD/HYSA rates already track the Treasury ceiling fairly closely (the market for liquid safe money is efficient).

**Reading:** none of the 6.25%-beats-4.6% gap is irreducible in a physical sense and none of it is a pricing anomaly either — it is the market-clearing price of *illiquidity*. A mortgage paydown cannot be resold for cash the way a Treasury note or CD can (A-5); the extra 1.5–2.3 points is compensation for that, not a free lunch. This reframes the "guaranteed return" conversation: the real question is never "does paying down debt beat a savings account" — it structurally always will, for this household, as long as GT-9/GT-10 hold — the real question is how much of the remaining money should be pushed past the risk-free ceiling entirely, into equities, for a *risk premium* rather than a liquidity premium.

### Dead End: Naive trade-off with generically-locked liquidity weights

**What was tried:** Ran the trade-off matrix across all four named options (A, B, status-quo cash C, and the 50/50 blend D) with Liquidity and Reversibility weights locked at 3 each, matching a generic household that has not yet decided where its "safety" money should live.

**Why abandoned:** The result (status-quo cash wins, 72 vs. 70/65/64) contradicts GT-1?, which establishes that this specific $60,000 carries no outstanding liquidity need — the emergency reserve is funded separately. The weights, though honestly locked before scoring, simply did not encode that stated fact. Re-locking the weights after seeing this result, with no justification beyond disliking the winner, would be exactly the post-hoc-reweighting failure mode the trade-off procedure exists to prevent — so this run is retired rather than patched, and a second run with a stated, GT-anchored justification for the specific weights changed (Liquidity and Reversibility only, per A-7) replaces it in chain C3.

**What it ruled out:** Confirms that a generic, undifferentiated liquidity-preference weighting is the wrong instrument for money the household has already classified as surplus-beyond-reserve, and saves a future analyst from re-running the naive version and being misled by its cash-favoring result.

### Dead End: 100% equities as the headline recommendation

**What was tried:** Considered recommending the full $60,000 to the index fund, since its central-case ($141,000) and held-to-step-up-basis ($159,000) outcomes both clear Option A's guaranteed $111,913 by wide margins, and a raw expected-value reading (A-4) favors it outright.

**Why abandoned:** Applying Inversion to this claim (see §2, A-4) surfaces three load-bearing preconditions: no repeat of a 2000-2009-style decade, no forced sale at a cyclical trough, and the household holding through volatility rather than panic-selling. GT-8 shows the first precondition has already failed once in the modern historical record, and the corrected trade-off's Downside-protection criterion — its single heaviest locked weight — penalizes pure equities (score 1 of 5) more than any other criterion rewards it. A recommendation whose central case is attractive but whose most important precondition has a demonstrated historical failure case is not one this analysis can respon­sibly hand down as the headline answer.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future analyst from treating the raw expected-return comparison (A-4) as sufficient justification, on its own, for an all-in equities recommendation.

### Dead End: 100% mortgage paydown as the headline recommendation

**What was tried:** Considered recommending the full $60,000 to principal, since it is the corrected trade-off's tiebreak winner (chain C3) and the single safest, simplest, most tax-clean option with no scenario producing a nominal loss.

**Why abandoned:** The tiebreak fires only because of an exact arithmetic tie (61 vs. 61) broken by a rule about which option's winning criteria rest on fewer unverified inputs — not because 100%-paydown's substantive dollar outcome is better. Chain C2 shows the blend's central-case outcome is materially higher than pure paydown's, and its worst-case outcome still clears the original $60,000. Treating a rule-based tiebreak as a decisive substantive preference would overstate what the arithmetic actually shows.

**What it ruled out:** Saves a future analyst from reading the trade-off's technical tiebreak winner as a strong, decisive recommendation rather than the genuinely close call (chain C3) it actually reflects.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Split the $60,000 roughly 50/50 between extra mortgage principal and the broad-market index fund as the default, shifting the split toward more paydown for a risk-averse household or one that may need this specific money back before year 10, and toward more equities for a household with high risk tolerance and a genuine intention to keep holding/investing well past year 10 (chain C3).

**Key insight:** The mortgage's guaranteed 6.25% return is not an argument for putting all $60,000 into the house — it is proof that none of this money belongs in cash, CDs, or bonds, since paying down the mortgage already beats every genuinely liquid risk-free alternative available today by 1.5 to 2.3 points (chain C1). Once that "safe bucket" question is settled, the real decision is only how much of the remainder to push past the risk-free ceiling into equities for the risk premium — not whether debt paydown "beats the stock market," which is the wrong framing to start from (chain C1).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This split forgoes some of pure equities' expected upside and some of pure paydown's absolute certainty and simplicity, and it leaves the equity half exposed to the downside bracket quantified in chain C2 (chain C2). It also leans on GT-1? — that the household's true emergencies are already covered by a reserve held separately from this $60,000 — so if that reserve is ever drawn down or found inadequate, the liquidity calculus behind chain C3's correction to the trade-off weights would need to be re-run (chain C1).

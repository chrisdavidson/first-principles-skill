# Thermal-to-Electric Ceiling for Molten-Salt CSP Towers

*First-principles analysis · 9 October 2026*

*Read with: [How to read this analysis](HOW-TO-READ.md) · [Working file](analysis-20261009T224310Z.md) · [All files](INDEX.md)*

## Executive Summary

**Recommendation:** The steam Rankine block at a 565°C-salt molten-salt tower is bounded not by naive Carnot (≈64%) or by the Curzon-Ahlborn finite-power benchmark (≈35-38%), but by a Carnot bound at achievable boundary temperatures: ≈58% dry-cooled to ≈62% wet-cooled (chain C1, C2, C3). Deployed practice (≈40-43% gross) leaves a ≈16-20 point gap, of which roughly 60-70% is recoverable with known technology and 30-40% is locked in by water-scarce siting (chain C1, C4).

**Band (from §6):** LOW

**Would change it:** Opening a primary source for GT-8? or GT-9? at the fixed-565°C-salt condition (chain C4) — the most load-bearing, least-verified figure behind the split; the 58-62% ceiling (chain C2) would likely survive such a revision.

## 1. Problem Essence

**Essence Statement:** What is the strict, physically-correct upper bound on thermal-to-electric conversion efficiency for a steam Rankine power block drawing heat from 565°C molten salt and rejecting heat to a real, finite-capacity heat sink — and of the resulting gap between that bound and what deployed molten-salt power-tower plants actually achieve, how much is closable with known, deployable engineering choices versus locked in by binding siting/material constraints?

**Success criteria** — a correct answer must:
1. Identify the correct reference cycle for a *real, finite-power* heat-rejection scenario (not naive Carnot using salt-tank temperature and ambient temperature), and justify why that reference is the right one rather than Carnot or Curzon-Ahlborn alone.
2. State a numeric ceiling with explicit hot-side and cold-side temperatures and the losses already folded into them (salt-to-steam approach, cooling-mode-dependent condenser temperature).
3. State a numeric current-practice efficiency range anchored to named, real deployed or near-deployed molten-salt tower plants.
4. Decompose the ceiling-to-practice gap into named, magnitude-estimated causes.
5. Classify each cause's share of the gap as recoverable (addressable by known engineering/economic choices, even if costly) or locked in (bound by a current siting, water, or material constraint), per the question's own framing — this is a graded engineering/economics classification, not a claim that only hard physics counts as "locked in."
6. Flag every place where precision is not available rather than inventing it.

## 3. Ground Truths

`?`-marked: GT-4, GT-5, GT-6, GT-7, GT-8, GT-9, GT-10, GT-11, GT-12, GT-13, GT-14 (11 of 14).

- **GT-1** — Carnot identity: for any heat engine operating between fixed reservoirs at temperature $T_h$ and $T_c$ ($T_h>T_c$, absolute temperature), the efficiency of any reversible cycle is $\eta_C = 1-T_c/T_h$, and no cyclic heat engine operating between those two fixed reservoirs — reversible or not — can exceed it. **Type:** physical law. **Provenance:** read-at-source — this is a textbook 2nd-law identity (not an empirical claim requiring a citation search); treated as foundational rather than flagged.
- **GT-2** — Curzon-Ahlborn efficiency at maximum power for an idealized *endoreversible* engine (internally reversible, with all irreversibility confined to finite-rate heat exchange at the two reservoir contacts under a linear/Newtonian heat-transfer law): $\eta_{CA} = 1-\sqrt{T_c/T_h}$. This is the efficiency *at the operating point that maximizes power output*, not a strict upper bound on efficiency at arbitrary (non-maximal) power. **Type:** physical law (specific finite-time-thermodynamics result, conditional on the stated model assumptions). **Provenance:** read-at-source — read-location: `ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1903.04381`, "The many avatars of Curzon-Ahlborn efficiency," equation and assumptions (endoreversible engine, Newton's-law heat transfer, average-power optimization over contact times) quoted directly.
- **GT-3** — Crescent Dunes (Tonopah, NV) molten-salt power tower heats its salt to 566°C (receiver/hot-tank temperature). **Type:** direct measurement / published design value. **Provenance:** read-at-source — read-location: IEEE Spectrum, "A Tower of Molten Salt Will Deliver Solar Power After Sunset," quoted sentence "The tower also heats its molten salt to 566 °C."
- **GT-4?** — Crescent Dunes steam cycle operates at 115 bar; nominal gross turbine capacity 110 MWe; cold salt tank ≈288°C (550°F) / hot tank ≈566°C (1050°F). **Type:** published design value. **Provenance:** reported-by-delegate — sourced via search synthesis citing the SolarPACES/NREL CSP project database and secondary trade press (GreenTech Media, IEEE Spectrum); the primary osti.gov record returned HTTP 404 on direct fetch and was not independently opened.
- **GT-5?** — Gemasolar (Spain, 19.9 MWe gross) uses a Siemens SST-600 two-cylinder reheat steam turbine with inlet conditions of 100 bar / 542°C. **Type:** published design value. **Provenance:** reported-by-delegate — sourced via search synthesis citing SAM case documentation and trade press (Modern Power Systems, nsenergybusiness); not independently opened in this analysis.
- **GT-6?** — A molten-salt tower design in the "SUPCON-class" literature uses steam parameters of 133 bar / 540°C with a stated power-block (gross) efficiency of 43%, via a two-stage turbine. **Type:** published design value. **Provenance:** unverified — **Phase 3 failure record:** source (`tib-op.org/ojs/.../919/2791/49162`, a SolarPACES conference paper) was opened via WebFetch twice; the PDF's text layer was not extractable (returned as compressed/encoded binary), so the asserted 43% figure and steam conditions could not be confirmed against the source text. - **GT-8?** — Sandia National Laboratories + Siemens Energy (2013) evaluated 14 subcritical/supercritical steam-cycle configurations for 150-200 MWe molten-salt towers, baselined against a 120 bar-a / 565°C-salt subcritical plant; supercritical options (up to 260 bar-a, same 565°C salt) produced more annual energy for the same heliostat field/receiver/storage and had LCOEs roughly 10% lower than the subcritical baseline. **Type:** published study finding. **Provenance:** reported-by-delegate — search synthesis of the Sandia publication listing/abstract; the full report was not independently opened. ## 4. Derivation Chains

### C1 — Establish the achievable hot-side temperature (not the salt-tank temperature)

```text
GT-1 (Carnot identity) + GT-3 (566°C salt, Crescent Dunes) + GT-5? (Gemasolar 542°C steam from 565°C salt)
→ heat cannot be transferred from salt to steam at a finite rate without a temperature drop, per GT-3 and GT-13?
→ the internal evaporator pinch is small (≈2.6-3°C, per GT-13?), so the dominant drop is the terminal temperature difference (TTD) at the superheater's hot end
→ the TTD observed across deployed and near-deployed designs ranges from ≈23°C (Gemasolar, 565→542°C) to ≈25-27°C (Crescent Dunes-/SUPCON-class, 565→538-540°C) [Assumes: A-2]
→ a representative achievable turbine-inlet steam temperature for a 565°C-salt plant is therefore approximately 538-545°C, not 565°C
→ this TTD is itself a design/cost choice, not a fundamental physical floor, since more heat-exchanger area narrows it at added capital cost
→ the correct hot-side boundary temperature for a real heat-rejection-scenario efficiency bound is therefore ≈540°C (813 K), not 565°C (838 K) as salt-tank temperature is commonly substituted directly
```

**Confidence:** MEDIUM — GT-5? (Gemasolar's 542°C steam-inlet figure) is reported-by-delegate and unverified; the verification that would remove it as a cause of this downgrade is an independently-opened primary source (SAM case documentation or the Siemens SST-600 datasheet) confirming the 542°C/100 bar figures at source, plus a larger-than-2-3-plant sample per A-2. GT-1 and GT-3 are unsuffixed and read at source, so neither is a cause of this downgrade.

### C2 — Derive the operative, finite-rate-corrected Carnot ceiling (the "law-permitted ceiling" for a real plant)

```text
C1 (achievable hot side ≈813 K) + GT-11? (CSP wet-vs-dry cooling penalty) + GT-12? (Noor III dry cooling)
→ a reversible Carnot engine at Th=838K(565°C salt)/Tc≈298K(25°C ambient) with zero pinch and zero condenser approach gives η=1-298/838≈64.4%
→ this 64.4% figure requires zero heat-exchanger approach losses everywhere and zero net power output, so it describes no real heat-rejection scenario
→ real plants instead reject heat through a finite-size condenser at a temperature set by siting and cooling technology, not by an idealized ambient reference
→ desert sites favor air-cooled condensers specifically because water is scarce, as at Noor III per GT-12?
→ an ACC's condensing temperature is governed by desert dry-bulb ambient (commonly ≈35-45°C) plus an ITD of order 20-25°C, giving a realistic dry-cooled condensing temperature of ≈60-67°C (333-340 K) [Assumes: A-9]
→ wet cooling at the same desert sites instead tracks the lower wet-bulb temperature (commonly ≈24-26°C) plus a cooling-tower-and-condenser approach of order 13-18°C, giving a realistic wet-cooled condensing temperature of ≈38-43°C (311-316 K) [Assumes: A-9]
→ wet cooling achieves this cooler sink at the cost of the water consumption GT-11? quantifies
→ applying GT-1 with Th≈813K and Tc≈333-340K (dry) gives a corrected Carnot ceiling of ≈58%
→ applying GT-1 with Th≈813K and Tc≈311-316K (wet) gives a corrected Carnot ceiling of ≈62%
→ this 58-62% band, not the naive 64% figure, is the operative law-permitted ceiling for a real, finite-power, cooling-mode-specific steam Rankine block drawing 565°C salt
```

**Confidence:** MEDIUM — capped by C1 (MEDIUM, named above). GT-11? (NREL/WorleyParsons dry-vs-wet cooling LCOE data) and GT-12? (Noor III dry cooling / salt range) are both reported-by-delegate and unverified; the verification that would remove them as causes of this downgrade is independently opening the NREL/WorleyParsons report (GT-11?) and the SolarPACES Noor III project record (GT-12?) at source. The representative desert ACC/wet-cooling design values (A-9) are engineering estimates, not site-specific measured figures, and no verification path beyond locating a named plant's actual cooling-system datasheet is available within this analysis.

### C3 — Correctly position Curzon-Ahlborn: a benchmark, not a ceiling, and a validating cross-check

```text
GT-2 (Curzon-Ahlborn formula) + C2 (operative ceiling, realistic T's)
→ evaluating η_CA=1-√(Tc/Th) at the C2 boundary temperatures gives ≈35.5% dry (1-√(337/813)) and ≈38% wet (1-√(313/813))
→ both figures sit below the ≈40-43% gross efficiency reported for deployed plants (GT-6?, GT-7?)
→ if Curzon-Ahlborn were a strict ceiling this would be a contradiction, but GT-2's own stated assumptions show it is not: it is the efficiency of an idealized endoreversible engine at its maximum-power operating point under a linear heat-transfer law, not a bound at arbitrary power
→ a real engine can trade power density for efficiency, moving up toward the true Carnot bound (C2) at the cost of a larger, more expensive heat-exchanger/turbine train per unit of heat throughput
→ CSP economics plausibly reward making exactly that trade, because the heliostat field and receiver dominate plant capital cost, making each unit of collected heat expensive relative to turbine/heat-exchanger capital [Assumes: A-4]
→ current practice (≈40-43% gross) sitting between η_CA (≈35-38%) and the corrected Carnot ceiling (≈58-62%) is therefore a validating cross-check on the C1/C2 framework, not evidence that its temperatures are wrong
```

### C4 — Decompose the ceiling-to-practice gap and classify recoverable vs. locked-in

```text
C2 (ceiling ≈58-62%) + GT-6? (SUPCON 43%, unverified) + GT-7? (NREL/Sandia baseline ~42% subcritical) + GT-8? (Sandia/Siemens 14-cycle supercritical study) + GT-9? (utility supercritical-gain literature) + GT-11? (dry/wet cooling penalty) + GT-14? (turbine/generator loss conventions)
→ the total gap between the operative ceiling (≈58-62%) and current deployed gross efficiency (≈40-43%) is approximately 16-20 percentage points
→ the infinite-regeneration-Rankine-converges-to-Carnot theorem shows that most of this gap is attributable to the finite regeneration stages and subcritical pressure used in deployed designs, not to a hard physical floor [Assumes: A-8]
→ the finding in GT-8? that supercritical pressure at the SAME 565°C salt temperature is both feasible and lowers LCOE by ≈10% shows this portion is economically addressable rather than physics-locked [2nd-order extension below revises an earlier, discarded classification — see §5]

→ this 8-12 point bracket is the single largest recoverable component of the gap
→ the turbine-stage isentropic-efficiency range in GT-14? (85-92%) is already near the practical ceiling for mature large steam turbines, leaving perhaps ≈1 further point of marginal, largely-exhausted headroom
→ the dry-vs-wet cooling penalty in GT-11? (≈3-8% LCOE), cross-checked in order of magnitude against the nuclear-ACC figure in GT-10?, translates to roughly 1-3 efficiency percentage points attributable to siting in a water-scarce desert
→ this 1-3 point cooling-mode component is locked in given the water constraint, not given physics
→ the residual 2-4 points reflects power-block parasitic loads and part-load/transient losses from variable solar input rather than constant full-load operation [Assumes: A-11]
→ roughly 1-2 of those residual points are addressable with better equipment and dispatch optimization, and roughly 1-2 are an irreducible floor inherent to any real, variable-input plant
→ aggregating across all components, approximately 10-13 points (≈60-70%) of the ≈16-20 point gap are recoverable with known, already-demonstrated technology, and approximately 5-8 points (≈30-40%) are locked in given current siting/water constraints
```

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** State the ceiling as a finite-rate-corrected Carnot bound, not naive Carnot and not Curzon-Ahlborn (chain C1, C2, C3): using the achievable steam turbine-inlet temperature (≈540°C / 813 K, after the unavoidable salt-to-steam approach loss) and a realistic, cooling-mode-dependent condenser temperature (≈333-340 K dry-cooled, ≈311-316 K wet-cooled), the operative law-permitted ceiling is **≈58% (dry-cooled desert siting) to ≈62% (wet-cooled)**, against an absolute, zero-power, unconditional bound of ≈64.4% and a Curzon-Ahlborn finite-power benchmark of only ≈35.5-38% (chain C2, C3).

**Key insight:** Curzon-Ahlborn is not itself the ceiling — it is the efficiency of a specific idealized engine *at its power-maximizing operating point*, and deployed plants (≈40-43% gross) already sit above it, which is expected once CSP's cost structure (expensive collected heat, cheap-by-comparison turbine/HX capital) is considered: the ceiling that actually bounds real plants is the Carnot bound evaluated at achievable boundary temperatures, approached asymptotically only as net power → 0 (chain C3).

**Gap and its decomposition:** Current deployed/near-deployed practice (Gemasolar-, Crescent Dunes-, SUPCON-class plants, all subcritical, 100-135 bar / ≈538-545°C) runs at roughly **40-43% gross**, ≈37-40% net power-block efficiency (chain C1, C4; GT-4?, GT-5?, GT-6?, GT-7?). The resulting gap to the operative ceiling is **≈16-20 percentage points**. Of that gap, chain C4 estimates **≈10-13 points (≈60-70%) are recoverable** with known, already-demonstrated technology — principally supercritical-pressure operation with fuller regenerative feedwater heating at the *same* 565°C salt temperature (Sandia/Siemens's own 14-cycle study found this both feasible and ≈10% cheaper on LCOE, yet it is not reflected in any plant built to date), plus a small, largely-exhausted turbine-refinement margin and better part-load dispatch. **≈5-8 points (≈30-40%) are locked in** given current constraints — dominated by the dry-cooling penalty imposed by siting in water-scarce deserts (recoverable only by consuming water, which is precisely the constraint these sites are sited to avoid) and an irreducible parasitic/part-load floor inherent to any real, variable-solar-input plant.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The recoverable share requires capital-intensive upgrades (supercritical boilers/turbines, more feedwater-heater stages) that no molten-salt tower has yet built at the relevant scale (all cited plants are ≤150 MWe); the locked-in share is "locked" only relative to the water constraint — wet cooling would recover roughly 1-3 of those points at the cost of water consumption these sites were specifically sited to avoid; a hybrid wet/dry scheme (already deployed at Crescent Dunes, GT-4?) can capture part of that recovery at a fraction of full wet-cooling's water draw, so the dry-cooling share of the "locked-in" category is better read as locked in only for a pure-ACC design, not as an absolute floor (adversarial-pass finding, Cluster 3).

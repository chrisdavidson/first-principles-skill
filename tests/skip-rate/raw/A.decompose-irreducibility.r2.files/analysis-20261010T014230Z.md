## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Does a 5 MWh molten-salt thermal energy storage (TES) tank operating between 290°C and 565°C actually achieve a full electricity-to-heat-to-electricity round-trip efficiency above 85%, and does that performance make it cost-competitive with a lithium-ion (Li-ion) battery system of the same nameplate MWh capacity — or does the claim bundle one figure that is physically unachievable with a cost comparison that silently changes units?

This question is reframed once at the level of fundamentals before any other analysis runs (companion technique: **theoretical-limit**, Phase 1 invocation): the entire dispute turns on whether ">85% round-trip" is a *convention* — a number that could in principle be pushed higher with better engineering — or whether it sits on the wrong side of a *hard physical bound* set by the second law of thermodynamics at these specific temperatures. If it is the latter, no amount of engineering improvement rescues the claim, and the analysis's job is to locate and quantify that bound rather than to debate plausibility.

**Success criteria:**
1. The analysis states, with a named physical law and the claim's own stated temperatures, whether a full electricity-to-electricity round trip above 85% is thermodynamically achievable at all — not whether it is merely unlikely.
2. The analysis states a realistically achievable round-trip efficiency band for this exact temperature range, grounded in at least one real deployed or published benchmark, and compares that band to the claimed figure.
3. The analysis states whether "cost-competitive… at the same nameplate capacity" survives once nameplate capacity is read as deliverable electric MWh (the only reading that makes the Li-ion comparison meaningful) rather than thermal MWh, and names the dominant cost driver this re-reading exposes.
4. The analysis does not force a single up-or-down verdict on the whole bundled claim where the two sub-claims (efficiency, cost) in fact diverge in how decisively they can be settled — it reports each sub-claim's own confidence separately.
5. The analysis identifies, by name, the specific unit or scope conflation most likely responsible for the claimed >85% figure, so a reader can recognize the same error if it recurs elsewhere.

### Dead End: Reading the >85% figure as round-trip exergy efficiency rather than energy efficiency

**What was tried:** Considered whether the claim could be rescued by interpreting "85% system efficiency" as an exergy (second-law) efficiency rather than a first-law energy efficiency — i.e., actual work output divided by the *maximum theoretical* work obtainable from the stored heat, rather than by the heat's raw energy content.

**Why abandoned:** Exergy efficiency is referenced against the Carnot (maximum theoretical work) benchmark itself, so an 85% exergy efficiency would mean achieving 85% of the ≈63–66% Carnot ceiling from C1 (≈53–56% of the raw stored heat) — a smaller, not larger, absolute electricity-to-electricity figure than the 38–45% already estimated in C2, and still nowhere near a first-law reading of ">85% system efficiency." This reading does not rescue the claim; it makes the physical-impossibility finding in C1 harder to escape, not easier.

**What it ruled out:** Any defense of the >85% figure via an exergy-efficiency reinterpretation. It reinforces, rather than weakens, chain C1's conclusion.

**What was tried:** Attempted to replace GT-8?'s general cost-scaling-law estimate with a specific, read-at-source manufacturer or project capex figure for a small steam or ORC turbine-generator set in the 0.6–2.5 MW range.

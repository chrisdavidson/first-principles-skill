## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Does a 5 MWh molten-salt thermal energy storage system operating between 290 °C and 565 °C achieve a true electricity-to-electricity round-trip efficiency above 85%, and — independently of that efficiency verdict — is a system of that technology and that size cost-competitive, on a same-nameplate-capacity basis, with a 5 MWh lithium-ion battery system?

**Success criteria:**
- The efficiency sub-claim and the cost sub-claim are resolved separately, not treated as one automatically following from the other (the claim's "making it cost-competitive" presents the second as contingent on the first, but the two must each be checked on their own evidence).
- Charging efficiency, storage (thermal-loss) efficiency, and discharge (heat-to-electricity) efficiency are treated as three distinct multiplicative stages, and the analysis states which stage(s), if any, a cited 85%+ figure could plausibly describe.
- The analysis states explicitly whether any cited 85-95%-class efficiency figure in the literature is computed on an electricity-only round-trip basis or a combined-heat-and-power (CHP) basis, since the claim's own wording ("round-trip electricity into heat and back") specifies the former.
- The discharge-side ceiling is grounded in an explicit Carnot-limit calculation using the temperatures the claim itself states (565 °C hot side, ambient rejection), not in analogy to other companies' marketing claims alone.
- The economic sub-claim is evaluated with the small-scale (5 MWh) cost-structure problem for power-cycle-based thermal storage made explicit — why the power-conversion hardware does not scale down the way battery hardware does.
- The analysis issues a confirm / partially confirm / refute verdict for (a) the efficiency sub-claim, (b) the economic sub-claim, and (c) the compound claim as stated.

No success criterion requires the answer to land on exactly one of a set of named options — this is a truth-evaluation of a compound factual/economic claim, not a choice between discrete alternatives, so the split-or-combination check (Phase 1 operation) does not apply here; this is recorded explicitly to satisfy the Phase 1 exit criterion rather than silently skipped.

## 2. Assumptions Table

## 3. Ground Truths

**Irreducibility test applied (five-whys, reduce-to-primitives mode):** "round-trip electricity-to-electricity efficiency" was decomposed into its constituent multiplicative factors — charging efficiency (electricity→heat) × storage efficiency (heat-in/heat-out over the storage duration) × discharge efficiency (heat→electricity) — and each factor was driven down to a physical law, a direct measurement, or an explicitly flagged unverified figure, rather than accepted as a single opaque "85%" headline number. GT-1 and GT-5 bottom out at physical law (Second Law of Thermodynamics; Joule heating). GT-2 and GT-4 bottom out at reported measurements/models of operating or simulated systems. This decomposition is what makes chains C1 and C2 possible.

- **GT-1** The Carnot efficiency limit for a heat engine operating between a 565 °C (838.15 K) hot reservoir and an ambient (≈25 °C / 298.15 K) cold reservoir is η = 1 − Tc/Th = 1 − 298.15/838.15 ≈ 0.644 (64.4%) — source: Carnot's theorem (Second Law of Thermodynamics), applied directly to the temperatures stated in the claim itself; provenance: physical law, derived directly in this analysis — no external citation to verify, no `?` suffix applicable
- **GT-2?** Current commercial steam turbines at CSP-class (≈565 °C) inlet temperature achieve gross thermal-to-electric conversion efficiency of roughly 38%, rising to roughly 45% for a supercritical design with ≈620 °C inlet — cited to: Reuters Events "Hot debate over thermal storage" / associated CSP-turbine-efficiency literature; reported-by-delegate (WebSearch synthesis) — cited source not opened by this analysis
- **GT-3?** The two-tank molten nitrate ("solar") salt CSP design point uses a cold tank at roughly 270-290 °C (above the salt's ≈240 °C crystallization point, plus safety margin) and a hot tank at 565 °C (the salt's thermal-stability ceiling) — cited to: DLR technical description of solar-salt CSP storage; reported-by-delegate — cited source not opened by this analysis
- **GT-4?** Thermal-storage-only round-trip efficiency (heat-in to heat-out, i.e. insulation/storage losses only, excluding any power-cycle conversion) of operating two-tank molten-salt CSP systems is reported in the 90-98% range: ≈93% for the Andasol system (industry-reported), ≈90% (NREL estimate), >97% for a modeled direct two-tank system (DOE-funded study), >98% for a Sandia simulation of a 1,200 MWh-thermal commercial-scale system; separately, Malta's own technical lead described storage losses as low as ≈1 °C of salt temperature per day — cited to: NREL/Sandia/DOE CSP thermal-storage literature via WebSearch synthesis; reported-by-delegate — cited source not opened by this analysis. **This figure measures storage efficiency alone, not round-trip electricity efficiency — it is not to be conflated with the claim's 85% round-trip figure.**
- **GT-5** Resistive (Joule) heating converts electricity to heat at ≈98-100% efficiency, with only minor incidental losses (wiring resistance, stray radiative/convective loss during charging) — source: definitional application of Joule's first law; provenance: physical law/direct derivation — no `?` suffix applicable

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion C1: Under the literal (resistive-heating + turbine) reading of the claim, an 85% round-trip electrical efficiency is not merely unachieved but physically impossible

GT-1 (Carnot ceiling ≈64.4% at 565°C hot / ambient cold) + GT-5 (resistive charging ≈100%)
→ crediting resistive charging as lossless (GT-5) and the discharge heat engine as perfectly reversible, the maximum possible round-trip electrical efficiency for a simple electricity-heat-electricity molten-salt cycle between 565°C and ambient is the Carnot-ceiling value from GT-1, ≈64%
→ this ≈64% figure already excludes any storage loss or real-turbine irreversibility, both of which can only reduce it further, never increase it
→ adding realistic storage loss, comparable to the 90-98% range reported for operating two-tank molten-salt systems (GT-4), narrows the ceiling further *[Assumes: A7 — multi-hundred-MWh loss figures applied unchanged at 5 MWh scale; if actual losses are worse at this small scale, the conclusion below only strengthens]*
→ realistic turbine irreversibility, where real units achieve roughly 55-70% of the Carnot-ideal conversion (GT-2), brings the practically achievable round trip down to roughly 33-43%
→ because 85% exceeds even the loss-free, perfectly-reversible theoretical ceiling of ≈64% established in hop 1, the claimed figure is not an engineering shortfall but a Second Law violation for this architecture
→ under Case A (resistive-heating charge, turbine discharge), 85% round-trip efficiency is physically impossible, not merely optimistic

### Conclusion C2: Under the charitable (heat-pump-driven Carnot-battery) reading of the claim, 85% is thermodynamically permitted in principle but unsupported by any evidence located in this analysis

GT-7a (Malta/SwRI "up to 60%" electricity-only) + GT-9? (Carnot-battery literature: 40-70% typical, 57% optimum, 81% theoretical) + GT-11? (lab prototype, 72.5% measured)
→ for an idealized, fully reversible heat-pump-charge / heat-engine-discharge Carnot battery operating across a single temperature span, the charge-side Carnot heat-pump COP and the discharge-side Carnot heat-engine efficiency are exact mathematical reciprocals *[Assumes: A12 — a single-span reversible model reasonably idealizes Case B's architecture]*
→ so the theoretical reversible-limit round-trip efficiency for this architecture approaches 100%, rather than being capped at the ≈64% ceiling that bounds simple resistive heating in chain C1
→ this reversible ideal is never reached in practice because compressors, expanders, and heat exchangers all generate entropy
→ across every credibly sourced real-or-near-term design found for this analysis — SwRI/Malta's "up to 60%" claim (GT-7a), a lab prototype's measured 72.5% (GT-11), and an optimization study's modeled 57% with "up to 81%" as an unachieved theoretical outer bound (GT-9) — no figure reaches 85%
→ the 85% figure therefore sits above the best theoretical-model optimum found in the literature and above every real-or-near-term-credible demonstrated figure
→ under the most charitable heat-pump-driven reading (Case B), the claim is unsupported by any evidence located in this analysis, though it is not a hard law violation the way Case A's figure is

### Conclusion C4: At 5 MWh scale, a molten-salt/power-cycle storage system is not cost-competitive with a same-capacity lithium-ion system under current technology and market structure

GT-13? (machinery dominates PTES cost, scales poorly at small size) + GT-14? (PTES capex/power-cost ranges, all studied at multi-MW-plus scale) + GT-15 (Li-ion ≈$125/kWh, standard modular 5-6 MWh container unit)
→ at 5 MWh, even a generously long discharge duration of 10 hours implies a power rating of only 0.5 MW for the thermal system's turbine/generator/heat-exchanger set
→ that 0.5 MW power rating is far below the multi-MW scale at which every cited PTES cost study (GT-14) was modeled *[Assumes: A8, A10 — today's thin sub-MW power-cycle market prices roughly in line with a downward extrapolation of multi-MW $/kW figures rather than carrying an even steeper minimum-scale premium; if the premium is steeper, the conclusion below only strengthens]*
→ extrapolating GT-14's $3,000-6,000-per-kW-class machinery costs downward to 0.5 MW implies a power-block cost on the order of $1.5-3 million for this one 5 MWh system
→ dividing that $1.5-3 million power-block cost by the 5,000 kWh of energy capacity yields roughly $300-600/kWh from the power block alone

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Treating GT-4's 90-98% storage-only efficiency range as if it already settled the 85% round-trip claim

**What was tried:** An early reading considered whether the reported 90-98% thermal-storage-only efficiency of operating two-tank molten-salt CSP systems (GT-4) could itself be the basis for confirming the claim's 85% figure.

**Why abandoned:** The claim explicitly states "round-trip electricity into heat and back," which specifies a closed electricity-in/electricity-out loop. GT-4 measures a different quantity — heat-in-to-heat-out storage efficiency, excluding both the charging and discharge power-conversion stages entirely. Treating GT-4 as settling the claim would repeat, rather than flag, the exact efficiency/round-trip conflation this analysis was asked to identify (see chain C3).

**What it ruled out:** Any shortcut reading that equates "molten-salt storage is >90% efficient" (true, for storage-only) with "this system round-trips electricity at >85%" (false under both Case A and Case B) — these are not the same claim, and the first does not imply the second.

### Dead End: Rejecting discharge heat to the 290°C cold tank instead of ambient, to try to raise the Carnot ceiling above 64%

**What was tried:** Since the claim's architecture includes a 290°C cold tank, one path considered whether using that tank (rather than true ambient temperature) as the heat engine's rejection sink could raise the theoretical efficiency ceiling closer to 85%.

**Why abandoned:** Recomputed directly in the adversarial pass below: rejecting heat to 290°C (563.15 K) instead of ambient (≈298.15 K) gives η = 1 − 563.15/838.15 ≈ 32.8%, which is lower, not higher, than the ≈64.4% ambient-rejection ceiling used in chain C1, because a smaller temperature span between hot and cold reservoirs always yields a smaller Carnot efficiency. This path was tested and found to work against the claim, not for it.

**What it ruled out:** Any rescue of the 85% figure via an alternative choice of discharge-sink temperature within the architecture the claim itself describes.

### Dead End: A much longer-duration (e.g., 50 kW / 100 h), lower-power 5 MWh design escaping the power-block cost penalty

**What was tried:** GT-14's particle-based PTES cost curve shows capex falling steeply with duration (≈$245/kWh at 10-hour discharge to ≈$38/kWh at 100-hour discharge) because the fixed power-block cost amortizes over more stored energy. This suggested a much lower-power, much-longer-duration 5 MWh design might escape chain C4's power-block cost penalty.

**Why abandoned:** No cost data for a genuine sub-100 kW thermal power-cycle unit was located by this analysis; commercially common ORC/turbine products cluster at hundreds of kW and above, so a 50 kW unit sits in an even thinner, less-standardized market segment than the 0.5 MW case already modeled in chain C4. This is speculative with no verification path this analysis could establish, so it is not cited on chain C4's head and stays here rather than being promoted to a conclusion.

**What it ruled out:** Nothing conclusively — it narrows where future verification effort (vendor quotes for sub-100 kW thermal power-cycle equipment) would need to focus before this specific rescue path could be confirmed or closed; it is named as the live, unsettled rival flagged on chain C4's confidence line.

### Dead End: Rescoring the economic claim on a cost-per-cycle / levelized-cost-of-storage (LCOS) basis instead of same-nameplate-kWh capex, to credit molten salt's long calendar life and minimal cycle degradation

**What was tried:** Molten-salt tanks do not degrade with cycling the way Li-ion cells do, and GT-14 shows thermal-storage LCOS improving sharply with duration and scale, which suggested that a cycle-life/calendar-life-adjusted comparison might favor the thermal system even though same-nameplate capex (chain C4) does not.

**Why abandoned:** This reframing answers a different question than the one the claim as literally worded asks — the claim specifies "cost-competitive... of the same nameplate capacity," which A6 (Phase 2) establishes is a capex/energy-capacity basis, not an LCOS or cycle-adjusted basis. Substituting a more favorable comparison basis than the one the claim names would answer a claim nobody made.

**What it ruled out:** Using cycle-life/calendar-life economics to rescue the cost-competitiveness verdict for the claim exactly as stated. It does **not** rule out that molten-salt/power-cycle storage could be economically competitive under a duration- or cycle-adjusted framing at larger scale — that remains a live, separate, out-of-scope question, consistent with GT-14's own observation that thermal storage economics improve with duration and scale.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Treat the compound claim as refuted. Under the literal reading implied by the claim's own wording (simple resistive-heater charging, turbine discharge), 85% round-trip electrical efficiency at 290-565°C is not merely unachieved but thermodynamically impossible (chain C1). Under the most charitable reading (a heat-pump-driven "Carnot battery" architecture), 85% is not a law violation but is unsupported by any real, near-term, or theoretical-model figure located in this analysis, all of which cluster at 25-81% (chain C2). The 85%-class figures actually circulating in industry (Malta's 85-95%) are traceable to a combined-heat-and-power accounting basis, not the electricity-only basis the claim describes (chain C3). Independently of the efficiency verdict, a 5 MWh molten-salt/power-cycle system costs several times more per kWh of nameplate capacity than a same-size lithium-ion system under current technology and market structure, because its power-conversion machinery dominates cost and scales poorly at sub-MW size while lithium-ion scales down almost linearly to exactly this size class (chain C4).

**Key insight:** The claim's surface plausibility comes from silently merging two separate industry numbers that are each real but describe different things: a combined-heat-and-power round-trip figure (85-95%) that credits delivered process heat as useful output, and a temperature range (290-565°C) that is itself a strong signal of a resistive-heated, CSP-derived architecture whose own physics caps electricity-only round-trip efficiency at roughly 64% even in the ideal, lossless limit (chain C1, chain C3). Reasoning by analogy to a headline industry percentage, without first decomposing which stage of the cycle and which accounting basis that percentage actually describes, is exactly what manufactures the appearance of feasibility here — the number is not fabricated, it is merely attached to the wrong process.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This verdict trades a sharp near-term refutation for two named, bounded uncertainties rather than overclaiming certainty everywhere: whether Malta's eventual full-scale plant could exceed today's 60% claim once it is actually built and measured (chain C2), and whether a thin sub-MW power-cycle equipment market could mature enough over the next decade to close most of the cost gap named in chain C4 (chain C4). This analysis also deliberately leaves out of scope — rather than silently assuming away — whether molten-salt/power-cycle storage could be cost-competitive at a larger scale or under a cycle-life/duration-adjusted cost basis instead of same-nameplate-kWh capex; that is a different claim than the one stated, not a settled "no" (chain C4; see also section 5).

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** Postgres table/index bloat and the need for `VACUUM`/autovacuum are driven by write activity (`UPDATE`/`DELETE`/`INSERT` churn creating dead or not-yet-visible row versions), not by read query volume; "frequent updates of statistics are more useful for heavily-updated tables than for seldom-updated ones," and `VACUUM` itself "creates a substantial amount of I/O traffic, which can cause poor performance for other active sessions." — source: PostgreSQL official documentation, "Routine Vacuuming"; read-at-source: the page's explanation of why `UPDATE`/`DELETE` row versions must be reclaimed, its write-vs-read framing of vacuum frequency, and its statement on vacuum's own I/O cost.

- **GT-2** A cache stampede ("dog-piling") is a cascading failure in which a popular cached item expiring or missing under heavy concurrent load causes many workers to recompute/re-query the backend simultaneously, which "can cause congestion collapse" by exhausting shared resources; once that happens, rebuilds can time out before completing, the hit rate can fall toward zero, and "overload prevents recovery," so the system can remain collapsed for as long as the load persists. — source: Wikipedia, "Cache stampede"; read-at-source: the article's definition and causes passage (cache expiry under heavy load, concurrent independent recomputation, resulting congestion collapse).

- **GT-3** In an M/M/1 queueing model, the average waiting time is ρ/(μ(1−ρ)) where ρ = λ/μ is utilisation; this quantity diverges as ρ→1, stability requires ρ<1, and "if, on average, arrivals happen faster than service completions the queue will grow indefinitely long" — i.e., the relationship between a reduction in arrival load and the resulting change in wait time is nonlinear, with large wait-time reductions concentrated near saturation and small ones far from it. — source: Wikipedia, "M/M/1 queue"; read-at-source: the formulas section (average time waiting, average number in system) and the stability/ρ→1 discussion.

- **GT-4** The evidence cited in the architecture-review document is limited to two figures: peak read-QPS on the listings endpoint, and the next-tier Postgres instance cost. — source: this analysis's own decision context, as supplied; read-at-source: "Evidence cited in the document: peak read-QPS on the listings endpoint, and the next-tier Postgres instance cost."

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: Accept the claim on textbook-pattern (convention) grounds

**What was tried:** The initial instinct was to approve the two-engineer-week cache work directly, on the grounds that Redis-in-front-of-Postgres is a standard, widely-deployed pattern (A-12).

**Why abandoned:** A-12 is a claim about the pattern's viability in general, not its fit to this specific workload. Chain C1 shows the cited evidence cannot establish the pattern is even aimed at the actual binding constraint; viability elsewhere does not substitute for fit here.

**What it ruled out:** Convention-grounded approval as sufficient evidence for an upgrade-deferral decision. Any future "just add a cache" proposal must clear the same ceiling test C1 applies before a deferral decision is made on its strength.

### Dead End: Assume read-bound by typical-workload prior

**What was tried:** Reasoning that e-commerce listing/catalog APIs are "usually" read-heavy, so A-2 (read-path is the binding constraint) could be accepted without measurement.

## 6. Conclusion

**Verdict on the claim as stated:** The claim asserts a fact — that the cache will reduce DB load enough to make a ≥2-quarter deferral safe — and, as stated, that fact does not hold: chains C1 through C4 show it is unverified against the only evidence cited (peak read-QPS, next-tier cost), and mechanically plausible to be false (C1's write/vacuum-bound alternative, C2's hit-rate sensitivity, C3's utilisation/mechanism gap, C4's unpriced stampede risk). The claim holds, at most, **conditionally** — on A-2, A-4, A-5, A-6, A-7, and A-9 each being confirmed — and the recommended approach below is designed specifically to produce that confirmation without betting the irreversible side of GT-5's asymmetry on it first (chain C1, chain C2, chain C3, chain C4).

**Recommended approach:** Execute the scheduled Postgres upgrade now — keep the cheaply-reversible side of GT-5's asymmetry resolved on the safe side — and ship the Redis cache in parallel, on an expanded scope that explicitly covers invalidation/staleness handling and anti-stampede protection rather than only the GET/SET mechanics GT-6's two-engineer-week estimate may cover. Name a checkpoint owner and a calendar date, before the next capacity-planning cycle, to review the measured hit-rate and read/write load-composition data the cache produces and decide, on that evidence, whether a future upgrade can be skipped or downsized (chain C5, chain C6).

**Key insight:** The architecture-review document's own cited evidence — peak read-QPS and next-tier instance cost — cannot distinguish a read-bound constraint from the write/vacuum/lock-bound constraint that Postgres's MVCC architecture makes equally plausible (chain C1). Even where read load is the binding constraint, "reduces average load" and "cuts p95 enough" are two different claims whose gap is governed by current DB utilisation and by which latency mechanism actually dominates the tail (chain C3). The decision presents as an architecture choice about caching, but its real load-bearing gap is a measurement gap, and the plan as proposed resolves that gap by betting the expensive-to-reverse side of GT-5's asymmetry on it rather than measuring it first.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** This recommendation costs strictly more in the near term than either named option alone: the next-tier instance cost is paid now — the exact cost cancelling the upgrade was meant to avoid — and the cache's engineering scope is widened beyond GT-6's two-week estimate to cover correctness work that option (a) as proposed did not budget for (chain C5). It also accepts a new on-call dependency and incident-diagnosis branch regardless of outcome (chain C6). The entire benefit of paying this extra cost is forfeited if the checkpoint reviewing the measured data has no named owner and date (chain C6) — this is a disclosed, not hidden, way for the recommendation to fail to pay off.

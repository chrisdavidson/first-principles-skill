## Section 3: Ground Truths

Verified via **5-Whys reduce-to-primitives** (each bottomed out at a physical law, definition, or cited measurement). Unverified-but-needed inputs carry the `?` suffix.

**Physics / materials ground truths (verified):**

- **GT-1:** Solar salt (60 wt% NaNO₃ / 40 wt% KNO₃) has a **liquidus/melting point ≈ 223 °C** and a practical operating window of ~290–565 °C. *(Source: SQM-DLR property report; ScienceDirect Solar Salt overview.)*
- **GT-2:** Solar salt **decomposes meaningfully above ~565 °C** (nitrate→nitrite→oxide, O₂/NOₓ release); 565 °C is the practical hot-tank ceiling. *(Source: OSTI molten nitrate development; DLR report.)*
- **GT-3:** Solar salt **specific heat ≈ 1.5 J/g·K** (1.49–1.57 over 250–425 °C). *(Source: Nature Sci. Reports; DLR.)*
- **GT-4:** Corrosion rate is **Arrhenius in temperature** — it rises sharply (often exponentially) with temperature. *(Physical law: rate = A·e^(−Ea/RT).)*
- **GT-5:** In **nitrate** salt at ~565 °C, austenitic stainless steels (304/316) corrode at modest, tolerable rates (tens of µm/yr regime) and are the conventional compatible alloy; carbon steel is only acceptable at the cold-tank (~290–316 °C) condition. *(Source: OSTI 1020472; Springer J. Mater. Eng. Perf.)*
- **GT-6:** In **chloride** salt (MgCl₂-KCl-NaCl) at ~700 °C, **304 SS corrodes at ~8 mm/yr** — catastrophic — while even Ni-based alloys (Inconel 625 ~2.8 mm/yr, Haynes ~3.1 mm/yr) corrode fast and need impurity/Mg control. *(Source: ScienceDirect SS310/SS304 chloride studies.)*
- **GT-7:** **Thermal cycling increases corrosion vs. isothermal exposure** — Type 316 saw 25–50% more corrosion under cycling. *(Source: corrosion review literature above.)*
- **GT-8 (Second Law / Carnot):** Stored thermal energy at temperature T_hot delivered to a heat engine rejecting at T_cold can yield at most the **Carnot fraction** of work: η_max = 1 − T_cold/T_hot (absolute temperatures). Deliverable **exergy ≪ stored thermal energy.** *(Physical law.)*
- **GT-9:** Typical large molten-salt TES capital cost ≈ **$27–41/kWh-th** (NaNO₃→solar salt→KNO₃), with the broader range €15–80/kWh-th; DOE target is <$10/kWh-th. *(Source: Wiley Bauer 2021; NREL; OSTI.)*

**Project-specific inputs (unverified — must be supplied):**

- **GT-10?:** Target hot-tank temperature `T_hot`. *(Determines A4, A5, A8, exergy.)*
- **GT-11?:** Chosen salt chemistry (nitrate vs. chloride/carbonate). *(Determines liquidus, decomposition, corrosion regime.)*
- **GT-12?:** Tank/piping alloy + wall thickness + corrosion allowance. *(Determines A5/A6 lifetime.)*
- **GT-13?:** Minimum ambient/operating temperature and installed heat-trace kW. *(Determines freeze margin A1/A2.)*
- **GT-14?:** Capacity in kWh-th, salt $/kg, and budget. *(Determines A10.)*
- **GT-15?:** Daily cycle count and design life. *(Determines fatigue A7.)*
- **GT-16?:** Cold-tank temperature `T_cold` and whether storage feeds a heat engine. *(Determines exergy DC-6.)*

## Section 4: Derivation Chains

Each conclusion traces to named ground truths with ≥1 intermediate step. **Second-order thinking** applied as a forward pass after the chains (no extension contradicted a ground truth, so none routed back to Phase 2). **Estimate** applied in DC-5; **theoretical-limit** in DC-6.

**DC-1 — Freeze risk is the highest-probability unraveling mode.**
GT-1 (liquidus ≈223 °C) + GT-13? (min ambient often well below 223 °C, even sub-zero) → the salt is *always* trying to freeze; staying liquid is an active, energy-consuming defense, not a passive state → if heat-trace (A2) or drain-down (A3) fails during an outage, salt freezes in pipes/valves → frozen plugs are extremely hard to re-melt and can rupture lines on re-expansion → **freeze is the most likely single point of failure.**
*Confidence: HIGH that freeze is the dominant risk class; the specific margin is MEDIUM pending GT-13?.*
*Second-order:* a freeze event during commissioning (cold start, crews inexperienced) is more likely than in steady state — schedule risk compounds physical risk.

**DC-2 — If target temp approaches/exceeds 565 °C with nitrate salt, the project unravels chemically.**
GT-2 (decomp >565 °C) + GT-10? (T_hot) → if T_hot ≥ ~565 °C the nitrate degrades, generating gas, consuming salt, shifting melting point, and fouling the system → **the salt chemistry must match the temperature ambition.**
*Confidence: HIGH conditional on GT-10?. If the "upgrade" raises temperature past 565 °C, nitrate salt is the wrong fluid — this is a go/no-go gate.*

**DC-3 — The corrosion-rate cliff is the load-bearing materials assumption.**
GT-4 (Arrhenius) + GT-5/GT-6 (nitrate-on-SS tolerable; chloride-on-SS catastrophic at ~8 mm/yr) + GT-7 (cycling adds 25–50%) + GT-12? (alloy + allowance) → corrosion rate × design life must be < corrosion allowance → **wrong salt/alloy pairing fails the wall in months, not years.** A 304 SS tank in chloride salt at 700 °C (8 mm/yr) eats a 10 mm allowance in ~15 months even before cycling penalty.
*Confidence: HIGH that pairing is decisive; specific lifetime LOW pending GT-11?/GT-12?.*
*Second-order:* corrosion products contaminate the salt, which raises the salt's own corrosivity and lowers its cp — a self-accelerating loop.

**DC-4 — Thermal-cycling fatigue is a slower, schedule-spanning unraveling mode.**
GT-7 + GT-15? (cycles/day × life) + A7 → daily charge/discharge imposes cyclic thermal strain on welds, tank floor, and foundation; fatigue life ∝ (strain range)^−m → larger ΔT and more cycles shorten life → **welds/foundation, not bulk wall, are the fatigue-limited elements.**
*Confidence: MEDIUM; needs GT-15? and weld/foundation detail. This rarely kills year-1 but can cause leaks mid-life — exactly the "unravel later" the owner fears.*

**DC-5 — Cost-per-kWh-th rebuilt from first principles (Estimate technique).**
Target units: $/kWh-th. Unit-factor decomposition:

- Salt mass per kWh-th = 1 / (cp × ΔT). With GT-3 (cp ≈ 1.5 kJ/kg·K = 0.000417 kWh/kg·K) and ΔT (e.g., 290→565 °C ⇒ 275 K): energy/kg = 0.000417 × 275 ≈ **0.115 kWh-th/kg** → **~8.7 kg salt per kWh-th**.
- Salt material cost: nitrate solar salt bulk ≈ **$0.7–1.0/kg** (industry range; **GT-14?** to confirm). → salt material ≈ 8.7 × $0.85 ≈ **$7.4/kWh-th**.
- Add BOS (tanks, foundations, heat-trace, pumps, insulation, instrumentation), which dominates: GT-9 anchors total at **$27–41/kWh-th**, so BOS ≈ $20–33/kWh-th (≈ 70–80% of total).

**Bracket [lower, central, upper]: ~$25 / $34 / $45 per kWh-th** for nitrate solar salt at this ΔT.
*Decision-resolution:* the bracket sits **well above the DOE $10/kWh-th target** and is dominated by BOS, not salt. If the project budget assumed near salt-material cost (~$7/kWh-th), it is **off by ~4–5×.**
*Confidence: MEDIUM; salt $/kg and capacity (GT-14?) and a smaller ΔT would shift it upward (smaller ΔT ⇒ more salt mass ⇒ higher $/kWh-th).*
*Second-order:* a smaller usable ΔT than assumed simultaneously raises cost (DC-5) and lowers stored energy (DC-7) — the same error hits two budgets.

**DC-6 — Exergy ceiling (Theoretical-limit technique).**
Governing law: **Second Law / Carnot.** Convention stripped: "we stored X kWh-th, so we can deliver X kWh of useful work." Law-permitted ceiling: η_max = 1 − T_cold/T_hot.
Worked at T_hot = 565 °C = 838 K, ambient sink T_cold = 30 °C = 303 K: η_max = 1 − 303/838 = **0.64**. With a more realistic engine cold side (e.g., condenser at 50 °C = 323 K): η_max = 1 − 323/838 = **0.61** — and real engines reach perhaps 60–70% *of Carnot*, so **deliverable work ≈ 0.35–0.45 × stored thermal energy.**
**Gap:** of the stored thermal energy, **roughly 55–65% can never become work** — it is irreducibly bound by the Second Law (not engineering slack). As the tank discharges and T_hot droops toward T_cold, the *marginal* exergy of the last kWh-th approaches zero.
*Confidence: HIGH (physical law).* **Project-must-supply:** GT-16? (T_cold and whether output is work or heat). If the load is *heat* (e.g., process/district heating), this ceiling does **not** apply and stored ≈ usable — so this gate is conditional on the use case.

**DC-7 — Usable capacity ≠ nameplate.**
GT-3 (cp) + A8 + GT-10?/GT-16? → usable energy = m × cp × (T_hot − T_cold,usable), where T_cold,usable is bounded below by the load's minimum acceptable temperature, *not* by the salt liquidus → **usable ΔT is narrower than the full liquid range**, so nameplate capacity computed on the full range overstates deliverable capacity.
*Confidence: MEDIUM pending GT-10?/GT-16?.*

**Trade-off (technique) — nitrate vs. higher-temp chloride/carbonate salt:**

| Criterion (higher=better) | Weight | Nitrate (solar salt) | Chloride/carbonate ternary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corrosion compatibility w/ common alloys | 5 | 5 | 1 |
| Max usable temperature / exergy ceiling | 4 | 3 | 5 |
| Freeze margin (lower liquidus better) | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| Cost/availability | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| Maturity/proven operation | 4 | 5 | 2 |
| **Weighted total** | | **5·5+4·3+3·4+3·4+4·5=81** | **5·1+4·5+3·2+3·3+4·2=48** |

Weights locked before scoring. **Nitrate dominates (81 vs 48)** for a conventional upgrade. Chloride only wins if the upgrade's *required* T_hot exceeds the nitrate decomposition ceiling — in which case the corrosion/maturity penalties become the new dominant risk. The trade-off result is a fork keyed entirely to GT-10? (target temperature).

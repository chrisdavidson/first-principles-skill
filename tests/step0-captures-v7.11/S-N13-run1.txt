# Carnot/Exergy Ceiling for Molten-Salt (Solar Salt) Two-Tank Thermal Storage

## Governing Law

**The Second Law of Thermodynamics**, expressed through the **Carnot theorem**: no heat engine operating between two thermal reservoirs can exceed the efficiency

**η_Carnot = 1 − T_cold / T_hot**

with temperatures in **absolute Kelvin**. This is the irreducible ceiling on heat-to-work (exergy) conversion. It is a statement of the unavailability of energy: heat carries entropy, and the entropy rejected to the cold reservoir (T_cold · ΔS) is work that can never be recovered.

Temperature anchors (converting °C → K, K = °C + 273.15, rounded to the values you specified):
- Hot tank: 565 °C → **838 K**
- Cold tank: 290 °C → **563 K**
- Ambient (heat rejection / dead state): 30 °C → **303 K**

---

## 1. Peak-Temperature Carnot Ceiling

Heat drawn at the hot-tank peak, rejected to ambient:

η_peak = 1 − T_ambient / T_hot = 1 − 303 / 838 = 1 − 0.36158 = **0.6384 ≈ 63.8%**

So at most **~64%** of heat delivered *at the top temperature* could become work. This is the optimistic, single-point ceiling.

---

## 2. Realistic Average-Temperature (Gliding) Exergy

The salt does not deliver all its heat at 838 K. As it cools from 565 °C (838 K) to 290 °C (563 K), it gives up heat across a **gliding temperature**. Each increment of heat dQ is available only at its instantaneous temperature T, so its exergy is dQ·(1 − T_amb/T). The peak Carnot figure overstates reality.

**(a) Arithmetic mean temperature** (your T_avg):
T_avg = (838 + 563) / 2 = **700.5 K**

η_avg(arith) = 1 − 303 / 700.5 = 1 − 0.4326 = **0.5674 ≈ 56.7%**

**(b) Log-mean temperature** (more correct for a constant-c_p sensible-heat glide, since dQ ∝ dT):
T_lm = (T_hot − T_cold) / ln(T_hot / T_cold) = (838 − 563) / ln(838/563)
= 275 / ln(1.4885) = 275 / 0.39775 = **691.4 K**

η_avg(log) = 1 − 303 / 691.4 = 1 − 0.4382 = **0.5618 ≈ 56.2%**

**(c) Exergy integral** (the rigorous form — exergy fraction of the total sensible heat, with constant c_p so dQ = c_p·dT):

Exergy fraction = (∫[1 − T_amb/T] dT) / (T_hot − T_cold) over [T_cold, T_hot]
= [ (T_hot − T_cold) − T_amb·ln(T_hot/T_cold) ] / (T_hot − T_cold)
= 1 − T_amb · ln(T_hot/T_cold) / (T_hot − T_cold)
= 1 − 303 · (0.39775 / 275)
= 1 − 303 · 0.0014464
= 1 − 0.4383 = **0.5617 ≈ 56.2%**

Note this is identically the log-mean result (the integral *defines* T_lm), confirming consistency.

**Realistic exergy ceiling ≈ 56–57%**, not the 64% peak figure. The glide costs roughly **7 percentage points** of theoretical availability versus the peak-temperature reading.

---

## 3. Conventional Figure

Real CSP molten-salt power blocks use a **steam-Rankine cycle**, achieving roughly **38–42% gross thermal-to-electric efficiency** (call it ~40% nominal; net, after parasitics, is a few points lower). State-of-the-art supercritical designs push the upper end; legacy subcritical plants sit at the lower end.

---

## 4. Bracketing the Gap

| Tier | Value | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Peak Carnot (838 K → 303 K) | **63.8%** | Idealized single-point ceiling |
| Realistic exergy (gliding, log-mean 691 K) | **56.2%** | True Second-Law ceiling for *this* heat source |
| Actual Rankine gross | **~40%** | Engineering reality |

**Decomposition of the gap from 63.8% down to 40%:**

- **63.8% → 56.2% (≈7.6 pts lost): IRREDUCIBLE — Second Law.** This is not engineering slack. It is the unavailability baked into delivering heat over a falling temperature glide rather than all at peak. No machine, however perfect, recovers it. Strictly a consequence of the entropy content of off-peak heat.

- **56.2% → 40% (≈16 pts lost): CONVENTION — engineering headroom.** This is the **Second-Law (exergetic) efficiency** of the real power block:
 η_II = η_actual / η_exergy_ceiling = 40 / 56.2 = **~0.71**, i.e. the Rankine block captures ~71% of the realistically *available* work.

 This ~16-point gap is recoverable in principle and is consumed by real irreversibilities: finite-ΔT heat exchange in the steam generator, turbine and pump isentropic losses (~85–90% efficient), condenser approach temperature, throttling, mechanical/generator losses, and the fact that a water-Rankine cycle's mean heat-addition temperature is itself below the salt's. Bottoming cycles, supercritical/sCO₂ blocks, and reheat narrow this band but cannot cross into the irreducible region.

**Headline bracket:** Of the ~24-point total gap between the 63.8% peak ceiling and the ~40% real plant, roughly **one-third (~8 pts) is irreducible Second-Law loss** from the temperature glide, and **two-thirds (~16 pts) is engineering convention** — the cycle's distance from its own exergy ceiling.

---

## 5. Round-Trip / Standby Exergy Degradation (qualitative flag)

Everything above assumes heat is withdrawn at the nominal temperatures. Storage over time erodes the delivered exergy *before* the power block even sees it:

- **Standby heat loss:** Tanks, piping, and the hot salt lose heat to ambient over hours-to-days of dwell. Energy loss is modest (well-insulated tanks lose ~1%/day), but the **exergy** loss is disproportionate because every lost joule was high-temperature, high-availability heat.
- **Temperature degradation (the bigger exergy hit):** Even small drops in *delivered* hot-tank temperature directly lower T_hot in 1 − T_amb/T_hot. A few degrees of cooldown shaves availability off the top — and the top is where exergy density is highest. Thermal stratification decay and mixing in the tanks blunt the peak.
- **Parasitic re-heating / freeze protection:** Solar salt freezes around 220 °C, so trace heating and recirculation consume work to *keep* the salt available — a direct exergy debit on the stored inventory.
- **Round-trip consequence:** The effective ceiling the turbine actually sees is below the 56.2% nominal exergy figure, and falls further the longer heat is stored. Longer-duration storage trades availability for dispatchability.

**Bottom line:** Second Law (Carnot) sets a hard ~64% peak / ~56% realistic exergy ceiling for this 838 K → 303 K system; real Rankine plants deliver ~40%, meaning ~71% Second-Law efficiency. About one-third of the ceiling-to-reality gap is irreducible physics (the temperature glide), two-thirds is engineering convention, and standby/degradation losses quietly lower the achievable ceiling further over storage duration.

Solar salt freezes at ~238 C (liquidus ~220–240 C). Every wetted surface that isn't self-heated by circulating hot salt must be held above ~250 C (with margin) by electric **heat tracing** + its own insulation, continuously, especially during outages.

### Why RELIABILITY, not magnitude, is the real failure mode

180 kW is trivial against a plant that moves ~hundreds of MW_th. The danger is **loss of tracing during an outage**. Heat trace is a long series of cables, contactors, controllers, and thermostats strung over kilometres — a high-component-count, single-points-of-failure system. A blackout that knocks out trace power (when there's no hot salt circulating to self-heat the lines) starts an irreversible clock. Frozen salt in a pipe is not a soft failure: it plugs the line, can rupture pipes/valves on re-thaw expansion, and can take the plant down for weeks. So freeze protection is designed for **N+1 power (UPS/backup gen), redundant zones, and continuous monitoring**, because the consequence asymmetry is enormous relative to the kW number.

```

t = τ · ln[(T₀−T_∞)/(T_f−T_∞)]
```
Use T₀ = 565 C, T_f = 238 C, T_∞ = 20 C → (T₀−T∞)/(T_f−T∞) = 545/218 = 2.50 → ln = **0.916**. (Starting from a cooler 290 C cold-leg: 270/218 = 1.24 → ln = 0.21, i.e. ~4× faster — cold-side lines freeze first.)

```
τ = 1.3×10⁵ / 1.3 ≈ 1.0×10⁵ s ≈ 28 h
t_freeze = 28 h × 0.916 ≈ 26 h  →  ~1 day (insulation intact, trace off)
```

## Time-to-Freeze (the critical reliability argument)

Freeze risk is governed by **thermal mass ÷ loss rate** = lumped-capacitance time constant. Cooling from operating T to freeze point:

**τ ≈ (m·cp·ΔT_margin) / (U·A·ΔT_drive)**

### Large insulated hot tank
- m·cp = 8.7×10⁶ kg × 1,500 J/kgK = 1.3×10¹⁰ J/K
- Allowable drop before freeze: 565→240 °C ≈ 325 K → energy buffer = 4.24×10¹² J
- Loss at ~392 kW (3.92×10⁵ W): t = 4.24×10¹² / 3.92×10⁵ = 1.08×10⁷ s ≈ **125 days**

→ A full hot tank is effectively freeze-immune for **months**. Huge thermal inertia.

### Thin pipe section, de-energized trace
- DN50 line (0.06 m OD, ~0.05 m ID), 1 m length. Salt volume = π(0.025²)(1) = 1.96×10⁻³ m³ × 1,850 = 3.6 kg
- m·cp = 3.6 × 1,500 = 5,450 J/K
- Buffer 565→240 = 325 K → 1.77×10⁶ J
- **If trace fails AND insulation is modest**: bare-ish pipe h≈10 W/m²K, A = π×0.06×1 = 0.19 m², ΔT≈ (avg 400−20)=380 K → Q = 10×0.19×380 = **717 W**
- t = 1.77×10⁶ / 717 = 2,470 s ≈ **41 minutes**
- Even *insulated* (U≈0.5): Q≈36 W → t ≈ 13.6 h

→ Thin stagnant lines freeze in **tens of minutes (bare) to ~half a day (insulated)** once trace power is lost.

| System element | Thermal mass | Time-to-freeze (no heat input) |
|----------------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| Full hot tank | Enormous | **~125 days** |
| Large header (DN300) | Moderate | ~1–2 days (insulated) |
| Small branch line (DN50) | Tiny | **~40 min bare / ~14 h insulated** |

**The parasitic *magnitude* is modest (~100 kW, <0.1% of inventory power).** The danger is **continuity**: solar salt's ~220–240 °C freezing point means any de-energized, low-thermal-mass line is minutes-to-hours from a frozen plug. A frozen plug:
- blocks flow → can strand the whole charge/discharge loop,
- risks thermal expansion rupture on re-melt (salt expands ~5% on melting; trapped between two plugs → burst),
- is slow and hazardous to recover.

So freeze protection is a **reliability/redundancy problem** (N+1 tracing, power backup, fast leak/temperature detection, drain-down design), not a power-budget problem. A few hours of grid outage with failed backup is enough to freeze the vulnerable lines while the tanks sit safely hot — meaning the *tank* gives a false sense of security while the *pipework* is the real exposure.

2. **Piping wetted area & penetration count** (2–3× span) — sets trace load.
3. **Backup-power reliability** — not a magnitude but a probability; dominates real-world freeze risk.

## The five weak points (ranked)

1. **Freeze-protection RELIABILITY — the #1 way these projects unravel.** The parasitic load is trivial (~180 kW), so it gets treated as solved. But that's the wrong frame: a full hot tank coasts for *months*, while a thin de-energized pipe freezes in *40 min–1 day*, and trapped salt ruptures pipework on re-melt (5% melt expansion). **The kilometres of piping are the time-critical single point of failure** — a single backup-power gap during a grid outage is the classic failure. Must be engineered as **N+1 reliability** (redundant trace zones, UPS/backup-gen, fast detection, drain-down), not merely *sized* to a kW budget.

2. **Decomposition-ceiling margin.** The conventional 565 °C hot tank sits *right at the edge* of the ~565–600 °C nitrate decomposition ceiling. If your "upgrade" is a **temperature uprate, that margin may already be gone.** Needs a hard over-temperature interlock.

3. **Salt inventory cost & lead time.** ~**$8.7M per GWh_th** (bracket $6.5–18.1M), volatile commodity price, long-lead kt-scale procurement. A first-order budget *and schedule* line, not a rounding error.

4. **Containment alloy + tank ratcheting** (especially on a retrofit) — life-limiting and currently unverified. Hot-side metallurgy must match *actual* peak temperature; a retrofit inherits unknown ratcheting/foundation history.

5. **Standby + parasitic erosion of the economics.** ~1%/day standby loss + continuous trace load, against ~40% Rankine delivery. The model must use **delivered exergy** — and note there's an **irreducible ~8-point Carnot glide loss no power-block upgrade can ever recover.**

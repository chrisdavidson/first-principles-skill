# Pre-Mortem: Storage Integration Timeline (Board Approval)

**MODE:** focused-pre-mortem · **Confidence frame:** Every cause below is conditioned on unstated facts. Nothing here is a finding about *your* project; each is a hypothesis to confirm or kill once the clarifying questions (last section) are answered.

## Problem Essence

Surface the failure modes of the *timeline being presented* before board approval converts it into a commitment (capital, go-live date, and/or external customer promise). The object under analysis is the **schedule and its embedded assumptions**, not the storage technology itself.

Success criterion: the board approves with eyes open — every load-bearing assumption is either validated or explicitly risk-accepted on the record, not silently absorbed into a date.

## The Prospective-Hindsight Premise

> **It is 12 months from now. The timeline failed — the milestone slipped, the integration didn't deliver, or the board's commitment was breached. We are writing the post-mortem. What caused it?**

I list causes without filtering first, then cluster. The premise deliberately assumes failure as fact — this is what makes people name causes they would otherwise suppress in front of a board.

## Unfiltered Failure Causes (raw list, pre-clustering)

Schedule was set top-down to fit a board/fiscal date, not bottom-up from work · estimates had no buffer for the unknown-unknowns · the "integration" was scoped as a connection but was really a re-architecture · a single vendor/team/person was the critical path and they slipped/left · the interface spec between systems was assumed stable and changed · vendor lead times (hardware) or API maturity (software) were taken on faith · regulatory/permitting/compliance gate (grid interconnection, data-residency, security review) was discovered mid-flight, not budgeted · data migration / cutover was treated as a final-week event and blew up · the team was shared with other priorities and got pulled · scope crept because "while we're in there…" · acceptance criteria were never defined, so "done" was contested · the assumption that environment X mirrors production was false · testing/commissioning window was compressed to protect the go-live date · rollback path didn't exist, so a partial failure became a full stall · the board approved a number nobody on the delivery side actually committed to · dependency on a partner deliverable that was outside our control · key estimate was an analogy ("last project took 6 months") not a derivation.

## Structural Weaknesses (clustered roots) + Board-Ready Treatment

Each weakness below names the recurring root, its dependence on unstated facts, and either a **mitigation** or an **explicit named risk-acceptance** the board can adopt on the record.

### S1 — Over-optimistic / top-down schedule
**Root:** Date chosen to fit the board cycle or a fiscal/customer event, then work back-fitted into it.
**Depends on (untested):** Was the timeline built bottom-up from estimated work, or top-down from a target date?
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Present the timeline as P50/P90 ranges, not a single date. Approve the **P90 date** as the commitment and treat P50 as the internal stretch goal. If only a point date exists, that itself is the finding.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board accepts a point-date with no schedule buffer; overrun probability is unquantified."

### S2 — Single points of dependency (critical-path concentration)
**Root:** One vendor, one engineer, one approval, or one partner deliverable gates everything.
**Depends on:** Is there a named critical path? Is any single party irreplaceable on it?
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Identify the top-3 critical-path dependencies by name; require a named backup or a "what if this slips 4 weeks" answer for each before approval.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board accepts concentration risk on [dependency]; no second source exists within the timeline."

### S3 — Assumptions never validated
**Root:** Estimates and feasibility rest on beliefs treated as facts (env parity, API maturity, vendor lead time, throughput).
**Depends on:** Which assumptions are validated vs. asserted? This is the single highest-leverage unknown.
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Require a one-page assumptions register; the board approves the *register*, not just the date. Any unvalidated load-bearing assumption gets a validation spike *before* the date is locked.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board notes assumptions [list] are unvalidated; the date is conditional on them holding."

### S4 — Integration / interface risk
**Root:** The boundary between the storage system and the existing stack (the actual integration) is the least-controlled, least-tested part.
**Depends on:** Is the interface spec frozen and owned by both sides? Has it been exercised end-to-end, even once?
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Require an early integration proof-of-life (thin end-to-end slice) before the bulk of work — de-risks the boundary first, not last.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board accepts that integration is first exercised late in the schedule."

### S5 — Vendor / supply risk
**Root:** External lead times (hardware) or external maturity/SLA (software/partnership) sit on the critical path and are outside direct control.
**Depends on:** Are vendor commitments contractual with penalties, or verbal/best-effort? Are long-lead items ordered?
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Confirm contractual lead times and place long-lead orders at approval, not after. Hold a schedule reserve sized to the largest single vendor slip.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board approves before vendor terms are contractually firm."

### S6 — Resource contention
**Root:** The delivery team is shared; another priority preempts them mid-flight.
**Depends on:** Is the team ring-fenced for this work, or matrixed across initiatives?
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Secure a named, protected allocation as a condition of approval. Unprotected capacity = the timeline is fiction.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board accepts that the team is shared and may be deprioritized."

### S7 — Scope creep
**Root:** "While we're in there" expansions erode the schedule the board approved.
**Depends on:** Is scope frozen with a written change-control gate?
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Approve a frozen scope; any addition triggers an explicit date/cost re-baseline back to the board.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board accepts scope may grow without re-baselining."

### S8 — Hidden regulatory / compliance gate
**Root:** A permitting, interconnection, security-review, or data-residency gate is discovered mid-flight and is non-negotiable and slow.
**Depends on (interpretation-sensitive):** *Grid storage* → interconnection/permitting; *software storage* → security/privacy/data-residency review; *vendor deal* → procurement/legal/regulatory approval. Which gates apply, and are they on the timeline?
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Map every external approval gate and put its lead time *on* the critical path before approval. These gates rarely compress.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board approves before compliance gates are confirmed; gate timing is unmodeled."

### S9 — Data migration / cutover risk
**Root:** Migration or go-live cutover is treated as a final-week event with no rehearsal and no rollback.
**Depends on:** Is there a tested cutover plan and a rollback path? (Most relevant to software-storage and grid-commissioning interpretations.)
**Board treatment — mitigation:** Require a rehearsed cutover with a defined rollback and a "go/no-go" decision point separate from the headline date.
**Risk-acceptance alternative:** "Board accepts an unrehearsed cutover with no rollback."

## The Ones You'd Suppress in the Board Room (high-signal, politically uncomfortable)

These are the causes a group dynamic tends to silence. Flagging them is the core value of a pre-mortem:

- **"Nobody on the delivery side actually committed to this date — it was set for the board."** (S1) The most common silent killer; raising it implies the presenter's number is theatre.
- **"Our estimate is an analogy to the last project, not a derivation."** (S3) Uncomfortable because it admits the plan isn't grounded.
- **"The integration boundary has never been exercised once."** (S4) Suppressed because it concedes the riskiest part is unproven.
- **"The team isn't actually ours — they'll get pulled."** (S6) Politically charged; names a peer's competing priority.
- **"There's a regulatory/security gate we haven't confirmed."** (S8) Suppressed because it can derail the whole approval if surfaced.
- **"There is no rollback — a partial failure becomes a full stall."** (S9) Avoided because it forces discussing failure at an approval meeting.

A board presentation that does not visibly address at least these six is optimizing for approval, not for delivery.

## Best-effort pre-mortem now (generic storage-integration timeline)

Since you want to think before the presentation, here's the prospective-hindsight frame applied to the failure modes that recur in storage-integration timelines specifically. The premise: **it is six months past the board presentation, the timeline failed, and we're explaining why.** These are the usual structural causes — treat each as a hypothesis to confirm or kill against your reality.

**1. Data migration was estimated as a copy, but behaved as a transformation.**
The single most common storage-timeline killer. Migrating data almost never means moving bytes — it means reconciling schema differences, encoding mismatches, and orphaned/dirty records. Migration effort scales with data *quality* problems you can't see until you start, not with data *volume* you can measure upfront. If your timeline has a fixed-duration "migrate data" block, that's the fragile date.

**2. The cutover was treated as an event, not a window.**
Timelines often show a clean switch from old storage to new. In reality you need a dual-write / dual-read period, a backfill, validation, and a rollback path. If there's no explicit rollback step in the plan, the timeline is implicitly betting the cutover works first try.

**3. A dependency outside your control gates the critical path.**
Vendor provisioning, a security review, a compliance sign-off, a networking/infra change, or another team's API. These rarely sit on the team's own board and so get under-weighted. Ask: what on this timeline depends on someone who isn't in the room?

**4. Performance/consistency surprises surface only at production scale.**
A new storage backend behaves differently under real load — latency tails, consistency semantics, throttling. These are discovered late, after integration "looks done," and force rework. If load/chaos testing is at the *end* of the timeline rather than threaded through, that risk is concentrated at the worst moment.

**5. The estimate had no slack and no explicit confidence level.**
A single-point date with no buffer is a forecast presented as a commitment. Boards remember the date, not the caveats. If the timeline shows one date rather than a range (or P50/P90), the downside is reputational the moment it slips.

**6. Rollback and data-integrity verification were the line items that got cut.**
When timelines compress, validation and reversibility are the first casualties because they don't produce visible features. That's exactly backwards for storage, where a silent data-corruption bug is the catastrophic outcome.

### How to use this in the board context
The board-facing reframe isn't "here's what could go wrong" — it's: present the timeline as a **range with named risks and their mitigations**, so a slip looks like a managed contingency rather than a missed promise. The three questions to walk in with answers to: *What's on the critical path that we don't control? Where's the rollback path? Is this date a P50 or a P90?*

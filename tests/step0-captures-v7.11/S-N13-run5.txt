## 1. Problem Essence

**Essence Statement:** *Will upgrading this molten-salt (or to-molten-salt) thermal storage installation deliver the intended added capacity/temperature/capability without introducing a failure mode — physical, materials, controls, or interface — that the existing-system baseline did not have, such that it fails at or after the design review?*

The triggering event is a feeling, not a number. Stripped of framing, the user is not asking "is molten-salt storage a good idea" (the technology works at scale — multiple operating CSP plants prove it). They are asking a narrower, sharper question: **does the *upgrade* — the delta from existing to new — cross a physical or engineering wall that the original design respected?** An upgrade is uniquely dangerous because it (a) inherits an installed base it did not design, and (b) is sold on pushing one parameter (capacity OR temperature) that may have been the original design's safety margin.

**Success criteria** (what a correct answer must satisfy — checkable against Section 6):
1. Identifies the *delta-specific* failure modes, not just generic molten-salt risks the original design already handled.
2. Anchors every physical claim in a law or established engineering fact, independent of the missing project numbers.
3. Ranks assumptions by (load-bearing-ness × un-verified-ness) so the user knows what to attack first at review.
4. Makes explicit which conclusions collapse if a withheld parameter turns out adverse.

A note on the theoretical-limit reframe: the user's fear may hinge on whether a target temperature is *a convention* (others run cooler for margin) or *a physical wall* (nitrate decomposition). That distinction is run explicitly in Phase 4 (DC-2).

## 4. Derivation Chains

Each chain has ≥1 intermediate step and a confidence rating. Estimates (DC-3, DC-4) use the estimate procedure with explicit brackets. Conclusions inheriting a `GT-N?` input carry the caveat per D-07. A second-order pass follows.

**DC-1 — Freeze/plugging is the dominant catastrophic risk, and an upgrade enlarges it.**
GT-3 (narrow window) + GT-5 (frozen line blocks/damages) + GT-8 (continuous heat-trace required) + GT-9/GT-10 (retrofit consumes margin) → an upgrade that adds tankage, lines, or HX surface adds dead-legs and surface-area-to-volume that all need freeze protection, while possibly *reusing* legacy heat-trace sized for the old plant → **if heat-trace (GT-14?) is not re-sized, zoned, redundant, and backup-powered for the new geometry, freeze-up at the weakest cold spot is not merely possible but probable over plant life.**
Confidence: **HIGH** that this is the dominant mode; **MEDIUM** on whether *this* project is exposed — collapses to HIGH-risk if GT-14? shows reused/undersized trace. *Verification that raises to HIGH: heat-trace load calc + redundancy + backup-power one-line for the upgraded geometry.*

**DC-2 — If the upgrade's purpose is higher temperature, the decomposition ceiling is a physical wall, not a convention (theoretical-limit reframe).**
Named governing constraint: nitrate thermal-decomposition chemistry (GT-2). Law-permitted ceiling: ~565-600 C for nitrate solar salt — *set by chemistry, not by what competitors run.* Conventional figure: operators typically cap hot tanks ~565 C, often lower (~550 C) for margin. Gap analysis: the headroom between a typical 565 C operation and the ~600 C onset is **irreducible margin against an Arrhenius cliff**, not slack to be spent.
GT-2 + GT-13? + GT-16? → **if the upgrade raises hot temperature toward or past ~565 C on a nitrate salt, it is spending the salt's chemical safety margin; "higher temperature" is achievable only by changing the salt (chloride/carbonate), which changes corrosion (GT-6), materials (GT-15?), and freeze point — i.e., it is a different project.**
Confidence: **HIGH** on the physics; **fragile on applicability** — entirely gated by GT-16? (is this a temperature upgrade?) and GT-13?. *If P-1 = temperature upgrade on nitrate salt, this is likely the thing that unravels the project.*

**DC-3 — Parasitic heat-trace energy: order-of-magnitude bracket (estimate procedure).**
Target: standing heat-trace power as a fraction of storage throughput. Decompose: heat-trace load ≈ (piping + tank surface area) × heat-loss flux through insulation. Unit-factors: insulated hot-pipe heat loss is order **10-10 W/m** of pipe (≈ tens of W/m, insulation-dependent); large CSP-scale salt systems carry order **kilometres** of traced piping plus tank walls.
- Central: ~10^3-10^4 m of pipe × ~10-10 W/m → **order 10^4-10^5 W = tens of kW to low hundreds of kW** continuous, plus tank standby losses.
- Lower bound (short, well-insulated, small system): ~10 kW.
- Upper bound (long runs, cold ambient, modest insulation, large tanks): several hundred kW to ~1 MW.
Against a storage block of order hundreds of MWh-th / tens of MW-th, **standing parasitic load is typically low single-digit % of rated power** — *small in normal operation but never zero, and it is a* **standing OPEX and a single point of catastrophic failure** *(GT-8): its value is in availability, not energy.*
Confidence: **MEDIUM** (order-of-magnitude; collapses to a real number only with GT-6/GT-14? geometry). Decision-resolution: the bracket straddles "annoying OPEX" to "material parasitic," so the load matters for economics — but for *failure* analysis the conclusion is robust regardless of where in the bracket it lands: loss of this load = freeze = catastrophic.

**DC-4 — Corrosion-allowance lifetime: bracket and the upgrade's effect (estimate procedure).**
Target: years of service before corrosion consumes the wall/corrosion allowance, and how a temperature upgrade changes it. GT-6: nitrate-salt corrosion of stainless is order **~10 µm/yr** at ~565 C for suitable austenitics, rising sharply with T and impurities; carbon steel is far worse and confined to the cold side.
- Central: a corrosion allowance of order 1-3 mm ÷ ~10 µm/yr → **order 10^2 years** for a well-chosen hot-side alloy at design T (i.e., not life-limiting *if* the alloy and temperature match).
- Lower bound: wrong alloy / higher T / chloride contamination → rate ×5-10 → allowance consumed in **~10-30 years** — life-limiting.
- Upper bound: ideal alloy, clean salt, conservative T → effectively non-limiting.
GT-6 + GT-10 + GT-15? → **a temperature upgrade moves the corrosion rate up the Arrhenius curve; an alloy adequate at the old temperature can become life-limiting at the new one. The legacy "it lasted X years" record is not transferable to the upgraded duty.**
Confidence: **MEDIUM**; gated by GT-15? and GT-13?. *Verification to HIGH: alloy-specific corrosion data at the new design temperature + remaining-thickness inspection of retained equipment.*

**DC-5 — Cold-ambient commissioning is a concentrated, schedule-driven freeze risk.**
GT-1 (freeze ~220-240 C) + GT-5 + GT-17? → first fill and commissioning require the *entire* wetted system pre-heated above liquidus before salt enters and held there until steady state → **commissioning in cold ambient with incomplete heat-trace or schedule pressure to fill before pre-heat is validated is a direct path to a frozen first fill — a failure that occurs precisely "at/after the design review," matching the user's fear.**
Confidence: **HIGH** on mechanism; applicability gated by GT-17?.

### Second-order pass (Phase 4 forward extension)

1. **DC-1 → 2nd order:** a freeze event forces a thaw-and-inspect outage → 3rd order: thaw-induced expansion damage (GT-5) → potential weld/HX failure → unplanned capital, not just downtime. *No contradiction with ground truths; extends DC-1.*
2. **DC-2 → 2nd order:** pushing temperature for performance accelerates corrosion (DC-4) AND raises decomposition risk (DC-2) simultaneously → 3rd order: the two failure modes are *coupled* — the same knob worsens both. *This coupling is a load-bearing finding: temperature is not a free performance lever.* No contradiction; reinforces.
3. **DC-3 → 2nd order:** heat-trace is a standing electrical load with a single-point-of-failure character → 3rd order: grid/backup-power loss in winter = system-wide freeze risk, not localized. Extends DC-1's severity. No contradiction.
4. **DC-4 → 2nd order:** corrosion products contaminate the salt → accelerate further corrosion and alter melt point (impurities can *raise* freeze risk) → couples DC-4 back into DC-1. No contradiction; reveals a feedback loop.

No second-order effect contradicts a Ground Truth, so no chain returns to Phase 2. The coupling findings (temperature worsens two modes; corrosion feeds freeze risk) are promoted into the Conclusion.

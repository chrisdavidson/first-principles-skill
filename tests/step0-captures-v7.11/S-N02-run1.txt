## The core finding
Your confidence is itself the top untested assumption. It's resting on three beliefs that haven't been verified as facts: that rollback works, that "validation = matching row counts," and that sample-scale behavior predicts production-scale behavior.

## Five structural weaknesses (these matter more than any single risk)

1. **Verification theatre** — "it passed" usually means row counts matched, not that the *contents* are correct. Counts can match while money sums, encodings, and precision are silently wrong.
2. **Untested rollback** — the safety net often exists only on paper. A rollback that has never been executed end-to-end is not a rollback. *(Highest stakes.)*
3. **Sample-scale confidence** — everything proven small, nothing proven at full volume or on edge cases. Throughput doesn't scale linearly (indexes, locks, memory).
4. **Source not frozen / not understood** — data changing underneath the migration, or field semantics guessed rather than documented (especially risky if the one person who knows the legacy schema is unavailable at cutover).
5. **No atomic cutover** — no defined consistent state to land in if it fails partway.

## The two highest-leverage actions
Convert these two from belief to verified fact and most of the risk list collapses:
- **Execute the rollback in a dry run** (not just write it)
- **Run a full-volume dry run** on a production-sized copy, timed against your migration window, with real edge-case records fed through

Specific failure modes worth checking: encoding corruption (UTF-8/Latin-1), column truncation, timezone/DST shifts, decimal precision loss on money, NULL-vs-empty collisions, broken foreign keys, and downstream consumers (reports, caches, integrations) breaking on the new schema.

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Can a two-tank solar-salt store cycled between 290 °C and 565 °C return more than 85% of the electricity used to charge it, and if it could, would that by itself make it cost-competitive with a lithium-ion battery of the same nameplate capacity?

The claim joins two separate assertions with "making it". The first is a thermodynamic claim about electricity → heat → electricity. The second is an economic claim that says efficiency is the reason the store is cost-competitive. Each is tested on its own, and then the link between them is tested. The trigger, a headline figure of "above 85%", is a symptom. The real question is what the laws of thermodynamics allow at these temperatures and what actually sets storage cost.

**Success criteria:**

1. The answer states the highest electricity-to-electricity round-trip efficiency (RTE) the second law allows at 290–565 °C, with every computed figure shown and recomputed, and says whether 85% sits above or below it.
2. The answer covers both ways of turning electricity into heat (resistive heating and a heat pump), because the claim names neither.
3. The answer puts the thermal store and the lithium-ion battery on the same energy basis (kWh of electricity delivered) before comparing them, and says what "same nameplate capacity" actually compares.
4. The answer says whether cost-competitiveness follows from the efficiency figure, and names the quantity that competitiveness actually turns on.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** Carnot bound. A heat engine taking heat at absolute temperature T_h and rejecting it at T_0 has efficiency at most 1 − T_0/T_h. When the heat comes from a sensible store cooling from T_h to T_l, integrating dS = δQ/T with δQ = m·c_p·dT gives the bound η_max = 1 − T_0·ln(T_h/T_l)/(T_h − T_l). — source: second law of thermodynamics (physical law) together with the definitions Q = m·c_p·ΔT and dS = δQ/T; read-at-source: not an external figure; the derivation is reproduced step by step in C1.
- **GT-2** Resistive (Joule) heating turns electrical energy into heat at no more than 100%. — source: first law of thermodynamics (physical law); read-at-source: not an external figure; conservation of energy.
- **GT-3** Solar salt (60:40 sodium nitrate : potassium nitrate) has a heat capacity of 1.53 J/(g·K) and is liquid between 260 and 550 °C. — source: Wikipedia, "Molten salt"; read-at-source: quoted passage "60:40 mixture of sodium nitrate and potassium nitrate is a liquid between 260 and 550 °C" and "a heat capacity of 1.53 J/(g·K)". This is a published property value.
- **GT-4** In molten-salt CSP practice the cold tank is held at 288 °C and the hot salt is heated to 566 °C. This matches the claim's 290–565 °C configuration. — source: Wikipedia, "Thermal energy storage"; read-at-source: quoted passage "kept liquid at 288 °C (550 °F) in an insulated 'cold' storage tank ... heats it to 566 °C". This is a published operating value.
- **GT-5** Storing the heat itself loses little: an estimate of 99% annual efficiency for heat retained in storage. — source: Wikipedia, "Thermal energy storage"; read-at-source: quoted passage "Estimates in 2006 predicted an annual efficiency of 99%". This is an **estimate**, not a measurement.
- **GT-6** Supercritical steam plants with turbine inlet temperatures of 538–566 °C reach about 40% plant efficiency. — source: Wikipedia, "Rankine cycle"; read-at-source: quoted passage "inlet temperature between 538 °C and 566 °C, which results in plant efficiency of 40%". ## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: A better power cycle

**What was tried:** Checking whether a more advanced heat-to-power cycle (supercritical CO₂ Brayton, combined cycles) could lift the discharge efficiency enough to reach 85%.

**Why abandoned:** GT-1 bounds every engine, whatever its design, at 64.4% from a 565 °C source to a 25 °C sink, and at 56.9% for the sliding 565→290 °C discharge (C1). No cycle choice moves a Carnot bound.

**What it ruled out:** Any rescue of the 85% figure through power-block technology. This settles C1's rival.

### Dead End: Heat pump drawing on a warm source

**What was tried:** Letting the heat pump draw from a source warmer than ambient (industrial waste heat, a hot reservoir), which raises the achievable COP and could push electricity-out ÷ electricity-in above 85%, or even above 100%.

**Why abandoned:** Outside heat then enters the system, so the ratio is no longer a round trip of electricity. Under A-4, which is the same boundary the lithium-ion figure GT-7 uses, it is not comparable. The waste heat has its own value, which the ratio leaves out.

**What it ruled out:** Counting outside heat as efficiency. This settles C3's rival.

### Dead End: Reading 85% as a measured electric round trip

**What was tried:** Taking the claim at face value, as a vendor-measured electricity-to-electricity figure.

**Why abandoned:** C2 (resistive ceiling 56.3%) and C3 (heat-pump ceiling 70.3% with the published engine) make a measured value above 85% impossible at these temperatures. The only figure above 85% the system can honestly report is the storage-stage retention (C4).

**What it ruled out:** The face-value reading. This settles C4's rival.

### Dead End: Comparing costs on nameplate MWh

**What was tried:** Comparing $ per nameplate MWh of the thermal tank with $ per nameplate MWh of the lithium-ion system, as the claim's "same nameplate capacity" invites.

**Why abandoned:** The two nameplates are on different bases. A 5 MWh thermal tank delivers 1.98 MWh of electricity, a factor of 2.53 less (C5), so the figures are not comparable.

**What it ruled out:** Any cost verdict expressed per nameplate MWh. Comparisons in this analysis are per delivered kWh of electricity.

### Dead End: Building a full capital-cost estimate for the thermal plant

**What was tried:** Building the thermal plant's total capital cost from components to settle the cost half of the claim.

**Why abandoned:** No ground truth here prices a 1–1.25 MW steam island, steam generator or salt heater (GT-11? is unverified and gives only a direction). Filling the gap with "a comparable CSP project cost X" would be analogy used as evidence.

**What it ruled out:** A directional cost verdict on current evidence. C7 records the question as undetermined and names the quote that would settle it.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** Reject the claim. A 290–565 °C solar-salt store cannot return more than 85% of its charging electricity. The ceiling is 56.3% with resistive charging and a perfect engine (chain C2), and 70.3% with a perfect heat pump and the published 40% steam plant (chain C3). The realistic resistive figure is 39.6% (chain C2).

**Key insight:** The "above 85%" figure is true only for heat in and heat out of the tank (about 99%), not for electricity in and electricity out (chain C4). Even if it were true, efficiency parity with lithium-ion would only remove a handicap and could not make the store cost-competitive (chain C6).

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** Whether a 5 MWh salt store is cheaper per delivered kWh than lithium-ion is left undetermined. It turns on the cost of the heater, tanks and steam power block at about 1.25 MW, which no ground truth here prices (chain C7). Comparisons must be made per delivered kWh, because a 5 MWh thermal tank delivers about 1.98 MWh of electricity (chain C5).

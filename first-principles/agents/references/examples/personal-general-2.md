# Worked Example: Personal and General (Second)

A complete first-principles analysis of the question "I have surplus cash and a fixed-rate mortgage — should I pay the mortgage down or invest the cash in a broad-market index fund?" The Phase 4 emphasis demonstrates a quantitative expected-value chain anchored in interest rates and expected returns, with a values-laden tie-breaker (sleep-at-night vs expected utility) deciding the close cases. Authored in Phase 33 as the second personal-general worked example; meaningfully distinct from the v1.0 personal-general analysis, which is a stated-goal → real-goal re-framing exercise (career relocation). This analysis does not re-frame the goal — the goal (maximise the household's chosen utility function over the relevant horizon) is taken as given — and instead does the quantitative work the v1.0 example deliberately did not.

**Scenario.** A household holds $60,000 in cash above an already-funded six-month emergency reserve, and carries a remaining $240,000 balance on a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage at a 6.25% APR, with 27 years remaining on the term. Mortgage interest is currently *not* itemised on the household's tax return (the standard deduction exceeds Schedule A's would-be total, so the marginal mortgage-interest tax shield is effectively 0%). The household's marginal federal+state income-tax rate on investment returns held in a taxable brokerage account is approximately 28% on ordinary income / 18% on long-term capital gains and qualified dividends. Two deployments are under consideration: (A) apply the full $60,000 as an additional principal payment to the mortgage, and (B) invest the full $60,000 in a broad-market index fund in a taxable brokerage account. The decision horizon is 10 years.

**The shape of this analysis.** The decision is genuinely quantitative — the inputs are rates, the outputs are dollars, and the chains can be computed. The first-principles operation is *not* to re-frame the goal (the goal is given) but to (1) strip the comparison down to the right after-tax, after-cost rates rather than the headline nominal rates, (2) acknowledge that the expected-value comparison is governed by an `untested belief` (future market returns) that no amount of historical data converts into a `physical law`, and (3) refuse to let the quantitative chain alone decide when its assumptions are close to a tie, because the variance and the household's drawdown tolerance are themselves load-bearing.

---

## 1. Problem Essence

**Core problem:** Given $60,000 in surplus cash and a fixed-rate mortgage at a known after-tax rate, which deployment — accelerated principal paydown or broad-market index investment in a taxable account — maximises the household's chosen utility function over a 10-year horizon, where utility is some function of expected terminal net worth *and* drawdown tolerance under stress scenarios?

**Success criteria:**

- The comparison uses the after-tax mortgage rate (not the nominal APR) and the after-tax, after-cost expected market return (not the nominal historical mean), because these are the only two numbers that can be directly compared.
- The expected-value chain is computed under at least two assumption profiles (a central-estimate profile and a worst-decile profile), and the chain is shown to flip its recommendation between them.
- The conclusion is contingent on a small number of explicitly stated inputs — household-specific (tax bracket, emergency-reserve status, drawdown tolerance) and market-state (expected real return distribution) — and a skeptic can recompute the recommendation by substituting their own values.
- The values-laden tie-breaker (sleep-at-night, cash-flow comfort under stress) is named as a legitimate input rather than smuggled in or rationalised away, *and* it is bounded: it decides close cases and is not used to override a decisive quantitative gap.
- At least one reasoning path that a casual analyst would have terminated on (e.g., "expected return exceeds the mortgage rate, therefore invest") is walked far enough to surface the assumption it hides, then abandoned with the reason recorded.

## 3. Ground Truths

- **GT-1** The mortgage's nominal APR is 6.25%; the remaining balance is $240,000; the remaining term is 27 years; the rate is contractually fixed — source: mortgage note and most recent statement.

- **GT-2** The household's marginal mortgage-interest tax shield is approximately 0% because the standard deduction exceeds the household's would-be Schedule A total under the current tax code and the household's other deductible items — source: prior-year federal return + current standard-deduction figure for the household's filing status. The after-tax cost of the mortgage is therefore approximately the full 6.25% — source: GT-1 × (1 − 0) = 6.25%.

- **GT-3?** The expected long-run real total return of a broad-market index over 10-year-plus horizons is approximately 6.5% per year — unverified: this is a central estimate from a historical distribution and is not a forward-looking measurement. Worst-decile 10-year real returns over the same source period are roughly 0–2% real, with negative real-return decades observed (e.g., the decades ending in 1939, 1974, 1981, 2009). Source category: published total-return series for broad US-market indices over rolling windows; specific numbers depend on index, inflation series, and window. Used with `GT-3?` notation; any conclusion that depends on this input inherits a MEDIUM-or-lower confidence caveat per D-07.

- **GT-4** The household's marginal income-tax rate is approximately 28% on ordinary income and approximately 18% on long-term capital gains and qualified dividends — source: prior-year federal+state return at current income level. Effective annual tax drag on a low-turnover broad-market index in a taxable account in this bracket is approximately 0.4–0.6 percentage points (dividend tax on ~1.5% yield + small distributed-capital-gains drag); the bulk of the realised gain is deferred to long-term cap-gains tax at horizon.

- **GT-5** The household holds a six-month emergency reserve in cash, separately from the $60,000 under decision — source: direct verification of household cash position. The $60,000 under decision is genuinely surplus to short-horizon liquidity needs.

- **GT-6** A one-time additional principal payment of $60,000 against a $240,000 / 27-year / 6.25% mortgage reduces the remaining term and the total interest paid over the life of the loan by an amount computable from standard amortisation — source: amortisation arithmetic. Order of magnitude: the $60,000 prepayment saves approximately $90,000–$110,000 in nominal future interest over the remaining term and shortens the loan by roughly 7–8 years; on a 10-year horizon, the recoverable benefit is the avoided interest in years 1–10 (roughly $30,000–$35,000 nominal, equivalent to a guaranteed 6.25% return on the deployed capital, compounded against the mortgage balance).

## 4. Derivation Chains

### Conclusion: Under a central-estimate market profile, broad-market index investment narrowly beats accelerated mortgage paydown on after-tax expected terminal wealth at the 10-year horizon — by a margin that is small relative to the variance of the input

GT-1 + GT-2 (after-tax mortgage cost is approximately the full 6.25% because the tax shield is currently ~0%) + GT-3? (central-estimate real index return ~6.5% real, which at ~2.5% expected inflation is ~9% nominal) + GT-4 (effective annual tax drag in a taxable account ~0.5 percentage points, plus deferred long-term cap-gains tax of ~18% on realised gains at horizon)
→ The relevant comparison is: paydown returns a guaranteed 6.25% nominal (the avoided mortgage interest, since the tax shield is ~0%), while taxable index investment returns approximately 9% nominal − 0.5 percentage points effective annual tax drag = ~8.5% nominal expected return during the holding period, with a further ~18% long-term cap-gains tax on the realised appreciation at the 10-year horizon. The after-tax-at-horizon nominal expected return of the index branch is approximately 9% × (1 − 0.5/9) compounding annually, with terminal gains taxed once at 18%; for a $60,000 deployment held 10 years, the expected after-tax terminal value is approximately $60,000 × (1.085)^10 × (1 − 0.18 × (1 − (1/(1.085)^10))) ≈ $113,000–$120,000. The paydown branch's recoverable 10-year benefit is approximately $60,000 × (1.0625)^10 ≈ $110,000 in mortgage-balance-equivalent terms.
→ The expected-value gap at the central estimate is small — approximately $3,000–$10,000 over 10 years on a $60,000 deployment, or roughly 5–9% of the deployed principal, in favour of indexing. The two numbers are close enough that the margin is well within the noise of the input assumptions.

**Confidence:** LOW. The chain consumes `GT-3?` (the central-estimate real market return). The point-estimate comparison is sensitive in the first decimal place of the return assumption — a 1-percentage-point reduction in the assumed real return (from 6.5% to 5.5%) closes the gap and a 2-percentage-point reduction (to 4.5%) reverses the recommendation. Verification that would raise confidence to MEDIUM: explicitly carrying the *distribution* of 10-year returns through the chain instead of the central point estimate — which Chain 2 does.

---

### Conclusion: Under a worst-decile market profile, the recommendation inverts — accelerated mortgage paydown produces a higher terminal outcome than taxable index investment

GT-1 + GT-2 (after-tax mortgage cost ~6.25%, guaranteed) + GT-3? (worst-decile 10-year real return is approximately 0–2% real; allowing for ~2.5% expected inflation, that is approximately 2.5–4.5% nominal annualised over a 10-year window) + GT-4 (tax drag still applies; in a flat or down market, the drag is larger relative to the gain)
→ Under a 3% nominal annualised return assumption, the index branch's after-tax-at-horizon expected terminal value for a $60,000 deployment held 10 years is approximately $60,000 × (1.025)^10 × (1 − 0.18 × small fraction) ≈ $76,000–$78,000. The paydown branch, unchanged at a guaranteed 6.25% mortgage-balance-equivalent rate, produces approximately $110,000.
→ The expected-value gap at the worst-decile market profile is approximately $32,000 in favour of paydown — roughly half the principal — and the paydown branch is also lower variance by construction (the avoided mortgage interest is contractual, not market-dependent).

**Confidence:** LOW — same `GT-3?` dependency; this is the other tail of the same distribution. The chain establishes that the recommendation is contingent on the input, not robust to it. The contingency itself, however, is HIGH confidence: that the worst-decile outcome favours paydown is a structural feature of comparing a guaranteed rate against a variable rate, not an artifact of the specific numbers.

---

### Conclusion: When the central-estimate quantitative gap is small relative to the variance, the load-bearing tie-breaker is the household's drawdown tolerance and the cash-flow consequences of the worst-decile branch — not the central-estimate expected value

GT-5 (six-month emergency reserve is held separately, so the index branch's worst-decile outcome does not force a sale to cover living expenses) + the diagnostic-flagged drawdown-tolerance assumption (the stated tolerance is verified to the extent the household has lived through a real prior drawdown) + the convention-challenged expected-utility assumption (expected terminal net worth approximates utility only if the worst-case branch does not cross a hard floor)
→ If the household has lived through a prior 30–50% drawdown without forced selling or destabilising behaviour, and the worst-decile index outcome (≈ $76,000–$78,000 terminal after a 10-year flat-real-return period) does not cross any hard floor — because GT-5 holds and the mortgage continues to be paid from current income, not from the $60,000 — then expected terminal net worth is an adequate utility proxy and the central-estimate chain (Chain 1) governs: a small expected-value advantage to indexing, with the household accepting the variance as a real cost paid in volatility tolerance for a small expected gain.
→ If the household has *not* lived through a prior drawdown (the diagnostic verification is weak), or if a worst-decile $32,000 underperformance versus the paydown branch crosses a household-specific hard floor (planned major expense in years 8–10, retirement-date sensitivity, partner risk-aversion not yet surfaced), then expected terminal net worth fails as a utility proxy in the worst-case branch and the load-bearing comparison is variance, not expected value. Under that condition, paydown's guaranteed 6.25% dominates a slightly higher expected return that is paired with a left-tail outcome the household cannot absorb. The intermediate (split deployment: e.g., $30,000 to each branch) becomes the dominant option, because it preserves most of the expected-value upside while halving the worst-decile underperformance, at the cost of also halving the central-estimate upside — a convex trade favourable to the variance-averse household.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The structural argument — that variance becomes load-bearing when the expected-value gap is small relative to the variance — is HIGH confidence and does not depend on `GT-3?`. The specific recommendation hinges on the diagnostic verification of drawdown tolerance and on the presence or absence of household-specific hard floors in the 10-year window; both are resolvable facts the household can verify. The `GT-3?` dependency from Chains 1 and 2 carries through indirectly: the size of the variance the tie-breaker has to weigh is itself a feature of the return distribution.

## 5. Abandoned Reasoning

### Dead End: "Expected market return exceeds the mortgage rate, therefore invest"

**What was tried:** The chain starts from the observation that the historical mean real total return of a broad-market index (~6.5%) is comfortably above the after-tax mortgage cost (~6.25%), reasons that expected value strictly favours indexing, and concludes that the household should invest the $60,000 in the index. Build the chain as: GT-3? records the central-estimate return; GT-1+GT-2 establish the mortgage cost; the gap is positive; therefore invest.

**Why abandoned:** Two specific failures. First, the chain uses the *point estimate* of an input whose entire defining feature is its variance — `GT-3?` is an `untested belief — economic-hinge`, and the historical distribution behind the 6.5% mean includes 10-year windows with real returns of 0% or worse. A chain that consumes the mean and discards the distribution is computing the wrong quantity: it answers "what would happen if next decade is average?" when the load-bearing question is "what is the distribution of outcomes, and is the household indifferent within that distribution?" Chain 2 shows the recommendation flipping under a worst-decile profile and Chain 3 establishes when that flip is decision-relevant. The mean-only chain hides that contingency entirely. Second, even taking the mean as given, the chain ignores tax drag: the after-tax expected return is approximately 8.5% nominal during holding minus an 18% terminal cap-gains tax on the gain, not the headline ~9% nominal pre-tax figure. Without applying GT-4, the chain compares an after-tax mortgage rate against a pre-tax market rate — a category error that overstates the gap by approximately 1 percentage point. Combined, the two failures mean the chain reaches a conclusion the corrected chain does not support.

**What it ruled out:** Treating this decision as a one-line expected-value comparison. The comparison is genuinely quantitative, but it is quantitative on the *distribution* of after-tax outcomes, not on the difference of two headline rates. Any reasoning path that elides either the variance or the tax drag fails before producing a chain the corrected analysis would endorse.

### Dead End: "Paying off debt is always safer, therefore paydown"

**What was tried:** The chain starts from the convention that debt reduction is unambiguously safer than market exposure, reasons that the paydown branch removes a fixed obligation while the index branch adds market risk, and concludes that the household should always favour paydown when surplus cash is available. Build the chain as: GT-1 records the debt; debt is a risk; reducing risk is safer; therefore paydown.

**Why abandoned:** The convention is doing work it cannot sustain. Liquidity is also a form of safety, and the paydown branch converts liquid cash into illiquid home equity that cannot be withdrawn without either a refinance event (which itself depends on interest rates and credit availability at the time of need) or a sale of the home. GT-5 establishes that a separate emergency reserve already exists, which partially mitigates the liquidity loss — but a household-specific job-loss buffer scenario inverts the convention: if the household lost income for 18 months, the indexed $60,000 could be sold (potentially at a loss, but available) while the paydown $60,000 is locked in home equity and cannot service the continuing mortgage payment, which the lender still expects in full each month regardless of how much extra principal was paid earlier. The convention "debt reduction is always safer" is true for some failure modes (interest-cost minimisation) and false for others (liquidity preservation under income loss). A chain that treats the convention as universal collapses once the liquidity scenario is named.

**What it ruled out:** Treating debt reduction as a dominant strategy independent of liquidity, income-stability, and emergency-reserve facts. Paydown is a real candidate — Chain 2 shows it dominates under worst-decile market profiles — but its merit is contingent on the household's specific liquidity position, not on the convention's general appeal. Any chain that assumes the convention without verifying the specific liquidity inputs reaches a conclusion the corrected analysis would not endorse.

## 6. Conclusion

**Recommended approach:** The recommendation is conditional on three household-specific facts that the analysis identifies but cannot resolve from external data alone:

1. **If** (a) the household has lived through a prior real 30–50% drawdown without forced selling or destabilising behaviour, **and** (b) there is no household-specific hard floor in the 10-year window (planned major expense, retirement-date sensitivity, partner risk-aversion not yet surfaced), **then** indexing the full $60,000 is the recommended deployment. The central-estimate expected-value advantage is small but real, the worst-decile downside is absorbable, and the tax-deferral feature of a taxable-account index position adds a small additional benefit not present in the paydown branch.

2. **If** the drawdown-tolerance verification is weak (no prior real-drawdown experience; stated tolerance is a hypothetical-question answer) **or** any household-specific hard floor exists in the 10-year window, **then** a split deployment (e.g., $30,000 paydown + $30,000 index) is the recommended deployment. The split preserves roughly half the central-estimate upside while halving the worst-decile underperformance — a convex trade that suits a variance-averse household and a partial verification of tolerance.

3. **If** the household's income stability is materially weaker than the GT-5 reserve assumes (e.g., a planned career change, a single-earner household, sector-specific employment risk), **then** the full-paydown branch is dominated by *holding the cash* — a different decision that the analysis would re-scope to. Within the stated scope (income stability is adequate and the emergency reserve is funded), paydown alone is the recommendation only if drawdown tolerance is verified-low AND the household's preference for the cash-flow comfort of a smaller fixed obligation is strong enough to compensate for the small expected-value cost.

Use the after-tax mortgage rate (6.25%, since the tax shield is currently ~0%) and the after-tax expected market return (~8.5% nominal during holding, less ~18% on realised gains at horizon) in any household financial planning. Do not use the headline 6.25% APR and the headline ~9% historical nominal mean — those are pre-adjustment numbers that overstate the gap.

**Key insight:** The decision is framed as a quantitative one and is genuinely quantitative — but the quantitative chain alone is decisive only when the central-estimate gap is large relative to the variance of the input. Here the gap is small and the variance is the load-bearing feature. The values-laden tie-breaker (drawdown tolerance, sleep-at-night, cash-flow comfort under stress) is a legitimate input — not a softening of the analysis but a recognition that expected terminal net worth approximates utility only when no branch crosses a hard floor. A purely expected-value analysis that ignores variance and ignores the household's drawdown tolerance produces an answer that is mathematically clean and decision-theoretically incomplete: it answers a question about averages when the household lives through a single realisation.

**Trade-offs acknowledged:** The indexing recommendation accepts approximately $32,000 of worst-decile 10-year underperformance versus paydown, in exchange for an approximately $3,000–$10,000 central-estimate expected-value advantage and the optionality of a liquid (if appreciated, taxable) position. The paydown recommendation accepts a small expected-value cost in exchange for a guaranteed, contractual return and lower variance, at the cost of converting liquid cash into illiquid home equity. The split recommendation accepts most of both branches' downsides and most of both branches' upsides — explicitly the convex-utility trade. None of the three deployments is dominant across all assumption profiles, which is what the contingency is recording.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM — the structural recommendation (use after-tax rates; treat the return distribution rather than the mean; let drawdown tolerance govern the close cases) is HIGH confidence and does not depend on `GT-3?`. The specific deployment recommendation depends on `GT-3?` (the central-estimate market return distribution) and on the household's verification of drawdown tolerance and hard-floor presence — both of which are resolvable facts. Verification that would raise the overall confidence to HIGH: the household completes the drawdown-tolerance differential check (prior real-drawdown behaviour) and confirms the 10-year hard-floor inventory. Until both are completed, the deployment recommendation is the conditional one above; the methodology that produces it is not contingent.
